## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday, in the first seven verses of chapter 11, the author showed how the early heroes before the flood (Abel, Enoch and Noah) walked with God by faith. Now he moves on to the patriarchs of Israel: **Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph** (Genesis 12 to 50). More space in this chapter is given to Abraham than to anyone else, because for Jewish Christians Abraham was the prime example of how a person responds to God's call by faith.

What makes the faith of the patriarchs so remarkable? Their whole lives were marked by **pilgrimage and a focus on the future**. They left their safe, settled lives behind and lived in tents for decades – not because they liked wandering, but because their spiritual eyes were fixed on an eternal city designed by God himself. Even on their deathbeds these men and women did not look back with regret, but looked forward with unshakeable certainty to God's future.

### Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into the riches of the original Greek terms and the theological foundations of this passage:

1. **Obedient departure, and tents versus eternal foundations (*paroikeō* – παροικέω, *technitēs* – τεχνίτης and *dēmiourgos* – δημιουργός, vv. 8–10):** When God called Abraham out of Ur of the Chaldeans – a highly developed, prosperous city of the ancient world – Abraham obeyed at once (in Greek, *kaloumenos* – καλούμενος is a present participle: *while the call was still sounding*, he was already packing!). He went out *"not knowing where he was going"*, without a map, with only God's voice as his compass. More than that: even when he had arrived in Canaan, he lived there *"as a guest"* (*paroikeō* – a temporary resident without rights of citizenship). For the rest of his life he lived in tents with Isaac and Jacob. Why was Abraham content with a temporary canvas over his head? Verse 10 gives the deep reason: *"For he was waiting for the city that has the foundations* (*tous themelious* – τοὺς θεμελίους)*, whose designer* (*technitēs*) *and builder* (*dēmiourgos*) *is God."* A tent has only tent pegs in the dust; the heavenly Jerusalem has eternal foundations. The word *technitēs* refers to the master architect who draws up the blueprint, and *dēmiourgos* to the creator-builder who brings the city into being.

2. **Life from a body as good as dead (*nenekrōmenou* – νενεκρωμένου, vv. 11–12):** Faith does not only set out for an unknown land; it trusts God for what is humanly impossible. Abraham was nearly a hundred years old – physically speaking *"as good as dead"* (*nenekrōmenou* – dead as far as fathering children was concerned) – and Sarah was barren and far past the age of bearing children. Although Sarah at first laughed when she heard the promise (Genesis 18:12), doubt gave way to faith. Verse 11 says of Abraham: *"By faith he received power to father offspring – together with Sarah herself – even when he was past the age for it, since he considered the one who had promised faithful"* (*piston* – πιστόν). (Many older translations make Sarah the subject of v. 11; the Greek base text we follow speaks of Abraham, with Sarah included.) Their focus shifted from their own worn-out bodies to the faithfulness of the One who promised, and from that one "dead" man came descendants as many as the stars and the sand of the sea.

3. **Strangers who greet from afar, and a God who is not ashamed (*aspazomai* – ἀσπάζομαι and *patris* – πατρίς, vv. 13–16):** Verses 13 to 16 form a beautiful theological commentary on the patriarchs' attitude to life. *"In faith"* (*kata pistin* – κατὰ πίστιν) they all died without holding the final fulfilment of the promises in their lifetime on earth. But look at the three verbs in v. 13: they **saw** them from afar (*idontes* – ἰδόντες), **greeted** them (*aspasamenoi* – ἀσπασάμενοι) and **confessed** (*homologēsantes* – ὁμολογήσαντες)! The word *aspazomai* means to welcome something with joy or to embrace it with open arms – like sailors who, after a long voyage, see the mountain peaks of their homeland appear on the horizon and cheer for joy, though they are still miles from the harbour! They openly confessed *"that they were strangers* (*xenoi* – ξένοι) *and temporary residents* (*parepidēmoi* – παρεπίδημοι: travellers who stay only for the night on the way to their real destination) *on the earth"*. They were seeking *"a homeland"* (*patris* – a true home where you belong with the Father). Had they kept longing for the comforts of Mesopotamia, they could easily have turned the camels round and gone back (v. 15). But because their hearts were set on the heavenly homeland, v. 16 gives one of the most moving statements in Scripture: *"Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God."* God proudly identified himself as the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac and the God of Jacob (Exodus 3:6) – three tent-dwellers with no city or land of their own, strangers in the eyes of the world's rich, but whose hearts were wholly set on his eternal city!

4. **The ordeal on Moriah: the logic of faith and the picture of resurrection (*logizomai* – λογίζομαι and *en parabolē* – ἐν παραβολῇ, vv. 17–19):** The author returns to Abraham's greatest test of faith (Genesis 22). The Greek tenses in v. 17 are striking: when he was tested, *"Abraham … offered up Isaac"* (*prosenēnochen* – προσενήνοχεν, perfect: in his heart and in the decision of his will the offering was already complete), and he *"was ready to offer up his only son"* (*prosepheren* – προσέφερεν, imperfect: he was in the act of offering him). This was not merely an emotional test for a father; it was a **theological crisis**. God had expressly promised: *"Through Isaac offspring will be named for you"* (v. 18). If Isaac died on the altar before his wedding and was burned to ashes, how could God's promise come true? Abraham was not paralysed by this apparent contradiction. *"He reckoned"* (*logisamenos* – λογισάμενος: he drew a mathematical, logical conclusion from God's character) *"that God is able even to raise from the dead"*: if God had made the promise and given the command, then **God must raise Isaac from the ashes**! Although up to that point in biblical history there had not been a single resurrection from the dead, Abraham's faith reached as far as resurrection. And *"as a symbol"* (*en parabolē* – so to speak, as a foreshadowing of Christ's resurrection) he received Isaac back from death.

5. **Deathbed faith that blesses the future (vv. 20–22):** What does true faith look like when the body weakens and death knocks at the door? The author names three deathbed scenes from the rest of Genesis:
   - **Isaac (v. 20):** Despite his human failings in Genesis 27, he pronounced in faith the covenant blessing over the future of Jacob and Esau, knowing that God's plan would go on.
   - **Jacob (v. 21):** On his deathbed in Egypt this old pilgrim blessed the two sons of Joseph (Ephraim and Manasseh) with crossed hands according to God's sovereign choice (Genesis 48), and bowed in worship before God, *"leaning on the top of his staff"* – grateful to the God who had led him like a shepherd all his life. (Hebrews follows the Greek translation of Genesis 47:31 here; the Hebrew text reads "at the head of the bed".)
   - **Joseph (v. 22):** Joseph was the viceroy of Egypt – surrounded by pyramids, power and wealth. He could easily have stayed buried in Egypt's splendour. But on his deathbed (Genesis 50:24–25) Joseph did not think of his Egyptian fame! He spoke of the future **exodus** (*exodos* – ἔξοδος) of Israel (which would not happen for almost four centuries!) and gave instructions that his coffin with his bones should not stay buried in Egypt, but be carried to the promised land. To his last breath Joseph was in effect declaring: "Egypt is not my home; my future lies in God's promise!"

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Faith obeys without the whole map.** Abraham went out not knowing where he was going, with only God's voice as his compass.
2. **Faith trusts God for what is humanly impossible.** The focus shifts from our own worn-out possibilities to the faithfulness of the One who promised.
3. **Believers are pilgrims on the way to a better homeland.** Their true home is the city that has the foundations, whose designer and builder is God – and so God is not ashamed to be called their God.
4. **Faith reckons with resurrection.** The God who promised is able even to raise from the dead; so we can lay our most precious things on his altar with open hands.

## Living It Out

Hebrews 11:8–22 puts a searching question to our modern way of life: **Are we living today as permanent citizens of the earth, or as pilgrims and tent-dwellers on the way to the city that has the foundations?**

Every day we are conditioned to think that our happiness depends on how deeply we can drive our tent pegs into this earthly ground. But when, like the patriarchs, we understand that we are only *parepidēmoi* (travellers passing through) here, on the way to our true *patris* (homeland), it radically changes three things in our everyday lives:

First, it frees you **to obey even when you cannot yet see the whole map (v. 8)**. We often want the Lord first to give us steps 1 to 10 of the next five years in writing before we are willing to take step 1. But Abraham went out *"not knowing where he was going"*. You don't need to know what tomorrow holds if you know the One who calls you today.

Second, it keeps you from **turning your eyes back to your old life (v. 15)**. When the pilgrim road gets rough, temptation often whispers that you should think back to the comforts of the "old homeland" from which we were called. If you let your thoughts keep circling (*mnēmoneuō* – μνημονεύω) around what you have given up, or how "easy" compromise would be, you will soon be looking for an opportunity to turn back. But when every morning you see the eternal city from afar and greet it with your heart (*aspazomai*), the world loses its charm. And think of the wonder of v. 16: when you refuse to seek your ultimate security in this temporary world and set your whole hope on his heavenly inheritance, **God is not ashamed to be called your God!**

Third, Abraham on Moriah and Joseph in Egypt teach us **to hold our most precious things with open hands before the God of resurrection (vv. 17–22)**. What is the "Isaac" in your life – the good gift, dream, child or ministry that the Lord himself gave you, but to which you may be clinging so tightly that it is becoming more important than the Giver? When we reckon (*logizomai*) that our God is the God who raises the dead, we can safely lay our future, our children and even our last breath on his altar.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- **Tent pegs or foundations?** If someone looked at your biggest worries, how you spend your time and your dreams, would they conclude that this earth is your final home, or that you are a pilgrim looking forward to the city whose designer and builder is God?
- **Obedience without the full map (v. 8):** Is there a particular step of obedience to which the Lord is calling you at the moment, but where you are still hesitating because you want to see all the answers and guarantees first?
- **Joseph's coffin and Egypt's pyramids (v. 22):** Which earthly success or "treasures of Egypt" sometimes threaten to make you forget that your true citizenship and future lie with God's covenant people? And what legacy of faith (like Jacob on his staff and Joseph's instruction) are you leaving today for your children and the generation after you?

## Prayer

*God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob – the living God who is not ashamed to be called my God – today, like Jacob of old, I bow before you in worship. Thank you for your gracious hand, which has led and carried me like a Shepherd to this very day.*

*Lord, when you call me to take new steps of obedience, keep me from the paralysing fear of the unknown. Give me the faith of Abraham to set out even when I cannot yet see the whole road ahead, because I know that you are with me. And where I face humanly impossible circumstances, let me, like Abraham and Sarah, not stare at my own weakness, but believe firmly that you, who gave the promise, are completely faithful.*

*Loosen my heart today from too strong an attachment to this temporary world. Remind me that I am only a pilgrim and a tent-dweller here, on the way to my true homeland – the eternal city with unshakeable foundations, whose Designer and Builder you yourself are. Today I lay my "Isaac" – my future, my family and everything precious to me – on your altar with open hands, trusting firmly in your resurrection power. Let me live, and one day die, with my eyes full of joy, fixed on your promises. Amen.*
