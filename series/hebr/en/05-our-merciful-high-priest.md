## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In the ancient Greco-Roman world one of the most important philosophical attributes of God was *apatheia* (ἀπάθεια) – complete imperviousness. The pagan philosophers believed that a true god could feel no pain, no grief and no human weakness, because to suffer would mean that someone or something had power over that god. Even for many Jews, the thought that the exalted Messiah would be tortured and die an accursed death on a cross was unthinkable.

In Hebrews 2:10–18 the author breaks radically with this cold, distant picture of God. He shows that it was precisely in keeping with the character and majesty of the Creator God ("it was fitting for him" – *eprepen* – ἔπρεπεν) to climb down into the mud and blood of our broken humanity. Jesus did not shout our salvation to us from the safe distance of heaven; he became flesh and blood to buy us back from the inside. Here in verse 17 the great central theme of the whole letter to the Hebrews is introduced for the very first time: **Jesus as our merciful and faithful high priest.**

### Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into the riches of the Greek terms in this passage:

- **The pioneer made perfect through suffering (*archēgos* – ἀρχηγός and *teleioō* – τελειόω, v. 10):** God's great goal is "bringing many sons to glory". To do this he appointed Jesus as "the pioneer of their salvation". The Greek word here is *archēgos*, a rich and dynamic word meaning a pioneer, a trailblazer or a champion. A well-known sermon illustration pictures the *archēgos* as the brave sailor who leaps from a ship into a stormy sea, swims through the breakers to the rocky shore and ties a rescue line there, so that everyone else on board can cross safely to land. The text goes on to say that God had "to make the pioneer of their salvation perfect through sufferings" (*teleioō*). This does not mean in any way that Jesus was morally imperfect or sinful (see Hebrews 4:15). *Teleioō* means to bring something to its full goal, or to qualify someone fully for a task.
- **Our brother who is not ashamed of us (vv. 11–13):** Because Jesus took on our true human nature, the one who makes holy and we who are made holy "are all from one" (one common Father and a human family bond). And then comes one of the tenderest sentences in the Bible: "That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers and sisters." To prove it, the author quotes three Old Testament sayings and puts them on the lips of Jesus:
  - **Psalm 22:22** (v. 12): The great psalm of the cross, which begins "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?", turns at verse 22 into a song of victory in which the rescued Messiah stands with his brothers and sisters in the assembly and sings the Father's name. Jesus leads our praise!
  - **Isaiah 8:17** (v. 13a): "I will put my trust in him." Just as the prophet Isaiah put his whole trust in God in the midst of an unbelieving people, Jesus stands among us as the one who trusted the Father in human dependence.
  - **Isaiah 8:18** (v. 13b): "See, here am I, and the children God has given me." Together with the faithful remnant, Jesus presents us to the Father as the children entrusted to him.
- **The devil's power broken (*katargeō* – καταργέω, vv. 14–16):** Verse 14 says that "the children share in blood and flesh" – the whole fragile, mortal human existence. That is why Jesus "in the same way took part in the same things" (*paraplēsiōs* – παραπλησίως). Ironically, his weapon was the very weapon with which the enemy had held humanity captive: death! Through his own substitutionary death he came to "break the power of" the devil. The Greek word *katargeō* does not mean that the devil ceased to exist, but to make something powerless, to disarm it, to put it out of action. Verse 16 confirms that he did not come to rescue angels; "it is the offspring of Abraham he takes hold of" (*epilambanomai* – ἐπιλαμβάνομαι – he grips them firmly in order to rescue them), that is, those who believe.
- **Our merciful high priest and atonement (*hilaskomai* – ἱλάσκομαι and *boētheō* – βοηθέω, vv. 17–18):** Unlike a prophet, who speaks to the people on God's behalf, a high priest stands before God on the people's behalf. To be able to do this, Jesus "had to be made like the brothers and sisters in every respect". He is both **merciful** (*eleēmōn* – ἐλεήμων – deeply compassionate towards our need) and **faithful** (*pistos* – πιστός – utterly reliable towards God and towards us). His core task as high priest was "to make atonement for the sins of the people" (*hilaskomai* – to turn aside and remove God's righteous anger over sin through a blood sacrifice). And because in his humanity he endured the full assault of testing and suffering without ever giving in, he is now able to "help" us. The Greek word for help is *boētheō* (made up of *boē* – βοή = a cry for help, and *theō* – θέω = to run). It literally means: **to rush towards a cry for help!**

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Our Saviour was qualified through suffering.** Through his real human suffering Jesus gained the qualification of experience to be the perfect Redeemer and High Priest for suffering people.
2. **Jesus is our brother.** Because the one who makes holy and those who are made holy are all from one, he stands with us as our brother in the assembly and leads our praise to the Father.
3. **The accuser has been disarmed.** Satan's power over death lay in his ability to accuse us as sinners before a holy God, for the wages of sin is death. When Jesus paid the debt on the cross, the accuser's trump card was torn out of his hand. As a result we are set free from the lifelong slavery of the fear of death.
4. **Our high priest comes to our aid.** Because he himself carried the full weight of testing, he rushes towards our cry for help with exactly the grace we need.

## Living It Out

This passage speaks directly to three of our deepest human struggles: **shame, fear and loneliness in temptation.**

First, think about your **shame**. When we stumble, doubt or wrestle with old sinful habits, the enemy whispers to us that Jesus is disappointed in us and ashamed of us. We feel like the "black sheep" of God's family and hide away. But look at verse 11: because he has made you holy with his own blood, Jesus is not ashamed to call you his brother or sister! He stands beside you today and presents you to the Father with joy.

Second, look at your **fear**. Much of our daily anxiety – about illness, safety, growing old and the unknown future – is rooted at its core in the fear of death (v. 15). That fear holds people captive in a cramped effort to stay in control. But Christ has broken through the grave and come out on the other side. For a child of God, death is no longer an execution but a passage into the Father's house. You have been set free to live for him fully and without fear.

Third, remember who he is when you are **tempted and tested**. When temptation knocks at your door or suffering overwhelms you, Jesus does not stand in heaven with folded arms, waiting to see whether you will fall. He knows how heavy the full weight of temptation feels, because he never gave in – he withstood the storm to the very end. As soon as you call, he rushes towards your cry (*boētheō*) with exactly the grace you need.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- **Hiding in shame:** Is there an area of your past or your present struggle that makes you feel God is ashamed of you? How does verse 11 ("That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers and sisters") change your confidence to come to him today?
- **Slavery to fear:** In what practical ways do you still let the fear of loss, illness or death dictate your decisions and your peace, when Christ has already bought you free from it?
- **The cry for help in temptation:** When you are tempted, do you first try to fight it in your own strength (and fail), or do you immediately call on your high priest, who promises to run towards your cry for help (*boētheō*)?

## Prayer

*Lord Jesus, my pioneer and my merciful high priest, my heart overflows with gratitude today. It is beyond my understanding that you, through whom and for whom all things were created, were willing to take on flesh and blood and to become like me in every respect – yet without sin.*

*Thank you that you are not ashamed of me. Though you know every weakness, every scar and every failure in my life, you still call me your brother, your sister, and you sing over me in the presence of the Father. Thank you that through your death on the cross you broke the devil's power over death and made full atonement for my sins.*

*Lord, set my spirit free today from every remnant of fear that still wants to hold me in slavery. And when I go through temptation or testing today, remind me that I am not fighting alone. You know the pain of the struggle. Hear my cry for help, merciful and faithful high priest, and come to my aid with your strength and grace. Amen.*
