## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

With Hebrews 11:23–40 the famous "Hall of Fame of Faith" reaches its dramatic climax. Yesterday the author showed us how the patriarchs (Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph) lived and died as pilgrims. Now he focuses on **Moses**, the exodus and the conquest of Canaan (vv. 23–31), before summing up the rest of Old Testament history in a sweeping flood of stories of faith (vv. 32–40).

For first-century Jewish Christians who had lost their social standing, safety and property through persecution, this passage was like fresh water in a desert. The author shows them that true faith has two sides, both equally honoured by God: **faith that experiences supernatural deliverance and victory** (vv. 32–35a), and **faith that refuses to compromise and is ready to endure suffering for the sake of a better resurrection** (vv. 35b–38).

### Insights from the Greek

Let us look at the insights from the original Greek and the theological structure of this passage:

1. **Moses' radical valuation and spiritual sight (*hēgeomai* – ἡγέομαι and *kartereō* – καρτερέω, vv. 23–28):** Moses' story of faith begins with his parents (Amram and Jochebed), who saw that the child was *asteios* (ἀστεῖος) – not just outwardly sweet, but exceptional and marked by God's favour (see Acts 7:20) – and who defied Pharaoh's murderous edict without fear. When Moses turned forty (Acts 7:23), he stood at the top of Egypt's pyramid of power. Yet he made a conscious decision of the will: he *"refused"* (*ērnēsato* – ἠρνήσατο: renounced his royal privileges), *"choosing rather to be mistreated along with the people of God than to have the fleeting enjoyment of sin"* (*helomenos* – ἑλόμενος). Why did he make such a seemingly foolish choice? Verse 26 shows his spiritual bookkeeping: *"for he considered the reproach of the Christ* (*ton oneidismon tou Christou* – τὸν ὀνειδισμὸν τοῦ Χριστοῦ) *greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt"* (*hēgēsamenos* – ἡγησάμενος: he made a carefully reasoned, calculated valuation)! By identifying himself with God's messianic covenant people, Moses was already sharing ahead of time in the suffering of Christ, because his eyes were firmly fixed (*apeblepen* – ἀπέβλεπεν: looking away from everything else to focus on one point) on the eternal reward. In v. 27 lies the secret of his staying power: *"for he held on* (*ekarterēsen* – ἐκαρτέρησεν: like a soldier who stays standing, strong and unshaken) *as though he saw the one who is invisible"*! When you see the invisible King of the universe with the eye of faith, the fury of an earthly pharaoh shrinks to nothing. That is why, in faith, he could also have the Passover blood put on the doorposts as a shield against judgment (v. 28).

2. **Walls that fall and grace for an outsider (vv. 29–31):** From the crossing of the Red Sea the author moves on to Jericho. How did Jericho's impregnable walls fall? Not by battering rams or military skill, but by believing obedience to God's strange command to march around the city for seven days. And who is named right alongside Moses and Joshua in the hall of fame? **Rahab**, a Canaanite prostitute! She had no share in Israel's covenant history, but while her whole city hardened itself in unbelief, she did not perish, *"because she had welcomed the spies in peace"*, believing that Israel's God is the true God of heaven and earth (Joshua 2:11). Faith saves the least of outsiders and places her right in the family tree of the Messiah (Matthew 1:5)!

3. **Two sides of the same faith: victory and martyrdom (*apolytrōsis* – ἀπολύτρωσις and *tympanizō* – τυμπανίζω, vv. 32–38):** At v. 32 the author quickens his pace. He names six judges, kings and prophets – Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David and Samuel – men full of human failings who nevertheless trusted God at the critical moment (they *"were made strong out of weakness"*, v. 34). They *"shut the mouths of lions"* (Daniel; compare also Samson and David) and survived fiery furnaces (Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego). Women (like the widow of Zarephath with Elijah and the Shunammite woman with Elisha) even received their children back from death (v. 35a). But notice the sudden turn in the middle of v. 35: **"Others…"** (*alloi de* – ἄλλοι δέ). The same great faith that rescued some from the lions' den enabled others *to die faithfully in the lions' den and under torture*! The word for "tortured" (*etympanisthēsan* – ἐτυμπανίσθησαν) refers to the *tympanon* – a cruel rack or drum on which prisoners were stretched out and beaten to death (as in the Maccabean persecution under Antiochus Epiphanes in the period between the Testaments). They were offered release (*apolytrōsis*) if only they would deny their faith, but they refused it, *"so that they might obtain a better resurrection"* (*kreittonos anastaseōs* – κρείττονος ἀναστάσεως)! Whereas the sons of these women in v. 35a were only called back to this mortal life to die again later, the martyrs looked forward to the final resurrection to eternal glory. They were stoned (like Zechariah in 2 Chronicles 24), sawn in two (according to ancient Jewish tradition the fate of the prophet Isaiah under King Manasseh), and wandered about in sheepskins and goatskins (like Elijah). And then in v. 38 the Holy Spirit gives these despised wanderers the highest possible title of honour: *"the world was not worthy of them"*! The world thought these believers were not worthy to live in society; God declares that the world was too poor and unworthy to house such treasures!

4. **Made perfect together with us (*chōris hēmōn* – χωρὶς ἡμῶν, vv. 39–40):** The chapter closes with a surprising revelation. All these Old Testament giants of faith received a wonderful testimony from God, yet they died on earth without experiencing "the promise" (the final coming of the Messiah and the completed new covenant). Why? *"since God had foreseen something better for us, so that they would not be made perfect apart from us"* (*chōris hēmōn*).

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Faith weighs value in the light of eternity.** Moses considered the reproach of the Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, because his eyes were fixed on the reward.
2. **Faith sees the invisible One.** When you see the invisible King with the eye of faith, the fury of earthly powers shrinks to nothing.
3. **Faith has two sides, both equally honoured by God.** The same faith that rescued some from the lions' den enabled others to die faithfully under torture, looking to a better resurrection.
4. **Made perfect together.** In God's great plan of salvation, even the finally completed salvation and cleansing of conscience of Abraham, Moses and David waited for the death on the cross and the high-priestly work of Jesus Christ, which has dawned in our New Testament age. We and the Old Testament believers are brought **together** to our eternal destination through the one sacrifice of Christ!

## Living It Out

Hebrews 11:23–40 breaks down two very dangerous misconceptions in our modern Christian thinking and replaces them with unshakeable strength.

The first misconception is that the world's glittering offers are really worth it. Every day our culture holds "the treasures of Egypt" before us – status, comfort, instant gratification and the approval of people – if only we are willing to turn our backs on God's radical call. Look how honest the Bible is in v. 25: sin *does* bring enjoyment, but it is only **"fleeting"** (*proskairon* – πρόσκαιρον: for a short time). Moses looked through Egypt's glossy varnish and realized that the richest palace without God is a dead end, while the hardest road with Christ ("the reproach of the Christ") leads to eternal treasure. You can resist Egypt's temptations and threats only when, like Moses in v. 27, you live every day **as though you saw the one who is invisible**.

The second misconception is that great faith will always protect you from suffering, illness or misfortune. Some voices claim that if you only have enough faith, you will always be in the first half of the list (vv. 32–35a): walls will fall, lions' mouths will close, and every crisis will vanish overnight. But look at vv. 35b to 38! The people who suffered in poverty, imprisonment, chronic affliction and martyrdom did not have *less* faith than those who experienced miracles; on the contrary, it is about *them* that God exclaims: **"The world was not worthy of them!"** Whether God chooses today to calm the storm around you (as at the Red Sea), or to carry you through the fire without your compromising – in both cases your faith triumphs, because your eyes are fixed on the "better resurrection".

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- **Moses' scales (vv. 24–26):** If today you put the world's approval and comfort on one side of the scales, and obedience to Christ (even if it brings discomfort or reproach) on the other – which side weighs more heavily in your practical choices?
- **Do you see the invisible One (v. 27)?** What "fury of the king" or earthly pressure is making you afraid at the moment? How does your fear change when you turn your eyes away from the earthly threat and focus on the invisible King who stands beside you?
- **Faith in the "Others…" seasons (v. 35b):** How does your faith respond when God does *not* take your problem away at once, as in vv. 33–34, but calls you to endure faithfully in a difficult situation, looking to the eternal reward?

## Prayer

*Almighty and invisible King, today I bow before you in deep awe. Thank you for the mighty cloud of witnesses who have gone before us – ordinary, weak people like Moses, Rahab, David and the prophets, who out of their weakness were strengthened by faith in your power.*

*Lord Jesus, purify my spiritual values today. Where the glittering treasures and the fleeting enjoyment of this world tug at my heart, give me the clear sight of Moses to see how quickly it all passes away. Let me count it my greatest wealth and honour to belong to you and even to bear your reproach, with my eyes firmly fixed on the eternal reward and the better resurrection you have purchased for me.*

*Open my spiritual eyes today, so that in every challenge I may hold on as though I saw the one who is invisible. Whether you lead me today through wonderful deliverance, or call me to stay standing faithfully under a heavy load without compromise – let my faith glorify your name. Thank you that in Christ you have foreseen something infinitely better for us and are bringing us, together with all the saints of all ages, to perfection. Amen.*
