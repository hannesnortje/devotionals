## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday we saw the Lamb take the scroll from the hand of the one seated on the throne, while the whole of heaven sang. Today he begins to open it. "And I saw when the Lamb opened one of the seven seals." What follows are some of the best-known and most feared images in the Bible: four horses and their riders, souls under the altar, and a sky rolled up like a scroll.

The images do not come out of nowhere. Zechariah saw horses of different colours, sent out by the LORD himself to patrol the earth (Zechariah 1:8–11; 6:1–8). Ezekiel names sword, famine, wild beasts and pestilence – exactly the list of verse 8 – and God calls them "my four severe judgements" (Ezekiel 14:21). This is the language of the covenant curses (Leviticus 26:21–26), and it echoes the plagues on Egypt: as God once judged Pharaoh to set his people free, so now he judges the empire that oppresses his people. The first readers did not read this as fantasy. They knew war (the Jewish War and the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, the civil war of AD 69), they knew famine (Acts 11:28), and on Rome's eastern frontier the Parthians, mounted archers, were a constant threat. But they would have understood that this was not simply Rome's history but God's judgement on Rome. As Assyria was once "the rod of my anger" (Isaiah 10:5), God often uses human violence itself – and Rome's own sword turns against Rome.

What did this mean for the churches in Asia? Rome praised itself as the bringer of peace and plenty – the *Pax Romana* could be seen on coins, in temples and in the marketplace. The seals pull back the curtain: behind that peace ride war, hunger and death, and it is not the emperor who opens the seals. For many of them the third seal was everyday reality. *"A quart of wheat for a denarius"* means a whole day's wage (cf. Matthew 20:2) for one person's bread – many times the usual price. The poor church in Smyrna (2:9) knew this well; for the rich church in Laodicea (3:17) it was a warning that wealth protects no one. From this period we also know of an edict of the emperor Domitian to uproot vineyards and plant grain instead, which was withdrawn after protests from the provinces; some interpreters hear this in the words about the oil and the wine.

How do we read these seals? They are not a timetable of disasters that will begin only at the end of history. Jesus himself said that wars, famines and earthquakes would mark the whole time until his return, but that this is not yet the end (Mark 13:7–8). The first four seals show the world as it is between Christ's first and second coming – for the churches in Asia then, and for us now – and who holds it in his hand. Only the sixth seal takes us right up to the great day itself.

### Insights from the Greek

- **Come! (*Erchou* – Ἔρχου, vv. 1, 3, 5, 7):** Each time one of the living creatures at the throne summons the rider. The horses do not charge out of their own accord; they come only when the Lamb opens a seal and heaven calls.
- **Was given (*edothē* – ἐδόθη, vv. 2, 4, 8, 11):** The crown, the sword and the authority are *given* to the riders. In biblical language the passive points to God as the one who gives: he sends the riders out – and he sets the limits. The black horse may not harm "the oil and the wine", and Death is given authority "over a fourth of the earth", not over the whole earth. Even in judgement God holds back, so that there is still time for repentance (cf. 9:20–21).
- **Slaughtered (*esphagmenōn* – ἐσφαγμένων, v. 9):** The souls under the altar are those "who had been slaughtered because of the word of God". It is the same word as for the Lamb (5:6). The witnesses share the lot of their Lord. In the temple the blood of the sacrifices was poured out at the base of the altar (Leviticus 4:7); so their lives have been offered to God.
- **Master (*despotēs* – δεσπότης, v. 10):** The word for a lord with full authority over his household and his servants. The martyrs are not crying out into emptiness; they call to the one who is truly in charge.
- **Who can stand? (*tis dynatai stathēnai* – τίς δύναται σταθῆναι, v. 17):** The question of Nahum 1:6: before his fury who can stand?

### Core Theological Truths

1. **The Lamb sends the seals – even war and hunger are not outside his hand.** The conqueror on the white horse, the sword, the costly bread and death are real and terrible. (Some see the first rider as Christ himself, since he rides a white horse in 19:11; but because he rides out with the other three, many read him as conquest and war, perhaps even as a counterfeit of Christ.) Yet these are not Rome's riders: they ride out only when the Lamb opens a seal and the living creatures at the throne call. It is God's judgement on a world that rejects him – and at the same time a call to repentance. History is not out of control.

2. **God hears the cry of his suffering people.** "How long, holy and true Master" is the prayer of Habakkuk ("How long, LORD, shall I cry for help", Habakkuk 1:2) and of the psalms (Psalm 79:5, 10). God does not rebuke them for it. He gives each of them "a white robe" and tells them "to rest a little longer". Their cause is not forgotten; it is simply not yet the end. For Pergamum, which had buried Antipas (2:13), and for Smyrna, which was facing prison (2:10), this was the answer to a burning question: where are our brothers and sisters who died for Christ's sake? They are not lost – they are with God, under his altar.

3. **The great day is coming, and no one can stand in their own strength.** The sixth seal uses the prophets' language for the day of the LORD: the sun black, the moon like blood, the sky rolled up like a scroll (Joel 2:31; Isaiah 34:4). Those who hide are the whole pyramid of Rome – kings, nobles, generals, the rich – but also "every slave and free person". They cry, as in Hosea 10:8, "Fall on us". Before the face of God there is no rank any more. For a small church under Rome's rule this was a startling comfort: the emperor, the governor, the commanders and the rich merchants – all before whom they had to bow – themselves hide from the Lamb.

4. **The wrath of the Lamb is the wrath of the one who was slaughtered for the world.** It sounds like a contradiction, but it is both the most serious and the most hopeful word in the chapter. The Judge is the same one who gave his blood. And the question of verse 17 receives its answer tomorrow: a great crowd that no one could count, "standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (7:9).

## Living It Out

For the churches in Asia, Revelation 6 said three things: Rome's peace is not as secure as it looks; you who suffer are not forgotten; and the powerful whom you fear will themselves have to stand before God. The same three things hold for us. We too live with promises of security – economic growth, technology, a strong government, a good pension – and we do not have to look far to see the four riders. The news is full of war, of prices that put bread beyond people's reach, of disease and death. That can paralyse you with fear, or send you searching for signs and dates to work out how near the end is. Revelation 6 gives a different answer: look at who opens the seals. The Lamb who was slaughtered for the world does not let chaos have the last word.

This does not mean that every illness or disaster in your life is a punishment for a particular sin (cf. John 9:3). The seals are about a world that rejects God, and his children too share in its suffering (v. 9) – but they are sealed and belong to the Lamb (7:3).

This also gives us the freedom to pray honestly. The souls under the altar are not polite and content; they cry, "How long?" Perhaps there is an injustice in your life that has gone on for years, a child living far from God, an illness that will not lift. You may cry it out before the throne. And think of the brothers and sisters who are persecuted today in many countries and die for their faith: they are not outside God's sight, and we may pray with them. Your cry is not ignored, and the "little longer" has an end that God knows.

And then the great question: "who can stand?" Not the powerful with their caves and rocks, and not we with our good intentions. Only those who stand with the Lamb, who have washed their robes in his blood (7:14). You do not have to flee from the face of the one seated on the throne; in Christ you may run to him.

When you read the news this week, choose one place of war or hunger and pray for it – and say it aloud: the Lamb holds the seals in his hand.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Which of the "riders" – war, economic pressure, illness, death – frightens you most at the moment (vv. 2–8)? What changes when you remember who opens the seals?
- What have you long been crying "How long?" about (v. 10)? Do you dare to lay it honestly before God?
- Where are you tempted to keep your witness quiet so that you need not pay the price of the souls under the altar (v. 9)?
- On whom or what are you relying to stand on the great day (v. 17)?
