## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday we saw the countless crowd before the throne and heard the promise that God will wipe away every tear. Heaven was full of voices: angels, elders, living creatures, a throng from every nation. And then the Lamb opens the last seal – and nothing happens. "And when he opened the seventh seal, there was silence in heaven for about half an hour."

After chapters full of hymns of praise, it is as if the whole of heaven holds its breath. The prophets knew this silence. Habakkuk, who had himself asked, "How long, LORD", receives the answer: "But the LORD is in his holy temple – silence before him, all the earth!" (Habakkuk 2:20). And Zephaniah cries: "Silence before the Lord, the LORD! For the day of the LORD is near" (Zephaniah 1:7). It is the silence before God acts.

But John also tells us why heaven falls silent. An angel takes his stand at the golden altar – the altar of incense that stood in the tabernacle and the temple right in front of the curtain, where the priest burnt incense morning and evening (Exodus 30:1–8). When Zechariah, the father of John the Baptist, once offered the incense, the whole people stood outside praying (Luke 1:9–10). So it is here: the angel is given much incense, "so that he would put it with the prayers of all the saints on the golden altar before the throne". Heaven falls silent because there is prayer – and God is listening.

The churches in Asia were small and without influence; no one in Rome heard their prayers in houses and workshops. But here those prayers rise "before God". And then something unexpected happens: "And the angel took the censer and filled it with the fire of the altar and threw it on the earth" (cf. Ezekiel 10:2). Thunder and an earthquake follow, as at Sinai (Exodus 19:16–18), and the seven angels make ready to blow the trumpet. The prayers of the saints come back to earth as God's action.

The trumpets that follow sound like the plagues of Egypt – hail and fire mixed with blood, a sea turned to blood, darkness (Exodus 7–10). As once against Pharaoh, God acts again against a power that oppresses his people – against Rome and every empire that behaves like Egypt.

### Insights from the Greek

- **Silence (*sigē* – σιγὴ, v. 1):** An ordinary word for silence. It is not an empty silence, but the silence of a courtroom before the judge speaks – and of a Father who is listening.
- **With the prayers of the saints (*tais proseuchais tōn hagiōn* – ταῖς προσευχαῖς τῶν ἁγίων, vv. 3–4):** The incense is added to the prayers and rises *with* them. In 5:8 the golden bowls full of incense were already "the prayers of the saints". Prayers are not merely tolerated; in heaven they are tended and brought before God.
- **Threw it on the earth (*ebalen eis tēn gēn* – ἔβαλεν εἰς τὴν γῆν, v. 5):** The same verb returns in verses 7 and 8: hail and fire are *thrown on the earth*, a mountain is *thrown into the sea*. The fire comes from the altar – the same altar under which the souls cried out, "How long, holy and true Master" (6:9–10).
- **A third (*to triton* – τὸ τρίτον, vv. 7–12):** Twelve times in six verses. The seals struck a fourth (6:8), the trumpets a third, the bowls later everything. The judgements are limited. They are warnings, not yet the end.
- **Woe, woe, woe (*Ouai ouai ouai* – Οὐαὶ οὐαὶ οὐαὶ, v. 13):** It is an eagle that cries out (*henos aetou* – ἑνὸς ἀετοῦ), not an angel, as older translations read following later manuscripts.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Heaven falls silent to listen.** The God who is worshipped day and night in heaven lets all the songs fall silent so that the prayers of his people can be heard. No prayer is too small for that silence.

2. **Prayer is part of how God governs history.** The trumpets do not begin with a decision made without us, but at the altar where the prayers rise. The answer to "How long?" is not indifference but God's justice set in motion. And behind the angel at the altar stands the great High Priest, Christ, "since he always lives to intercede for them" (Hebrews 7:25).

3. **The trumpets announce a new exodus.** Like the plagues of Egypt, they show that the idols are powerless and that creation belongs to God (Exodus 12:12). Their aim is not the destruction of creation but the liberation of God's people from the grip of the power that oppresses them – and, as we shall see tomorrow, a call to the world to repent.

4. **Even the warning is grace.** In Israel the trumpet was the watchman's alarm (Ezekiel 33:3–6). A God who warns has not yet given up on the world. The eagle's "Woe" is a serious word, but also a merciful one: there is still time.

## Living It Out

Many of us pray and hear nothing. The child stays far away, the diagnosis does not change, the injustice at work goes on. We begin to wonder whether our prayers get past the ceiling. Revelation 8 pulls back the curtain and shows us what we cannot see: heaven falls silent, an angel tends our prayers like precious incense, and they rise "before God". Your prayers are not lost.

This also teaches us something about silence. Our lives are full of noise, and even our prayers are often just one more stream of words. But if heaven can fall silent to listen, we too can become still before God – not to persuade him, but to remember who sits on the throne.

And when we hear of disasters – floods, fires, droughts – we need not fit each one into a timetable or guess which trumpet has just sounded. The trumpets show that this world is not as secure as we think, and that God is still calling: turn back before it is too late. For God's people that is not a reason for fear, but for prayer and witness.

Sit in silence for five minutes today before you pray. Then write down one injustice you have long been praying about, and bring it before God again – knowing that heaven is listening.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- When did you last become truly still before God, without speaking (v. 1)?
- Which prayer have you given up on because you thought no one was listening (vv. 3–4)?
- Do you believe that your prayers are part of how God acts in the world (v. 5)? How would that change your intercession?
- How do you respond to news of disasters – with fear, with speculation, or with prayer and repentance (vv. 7–13)?

## Prayer

*Holy God, before you even heaven falls silent. You sit on the throne, and yet you listen to the prayers of your people, mine too. Thank you that no prayer I have ever prayed has been lost, and that it rises before you with the incense.*

*Forgive my impatience and my discouragement, and that I have stopped praying because I heard nothing. Forgive the noise with which I fill my days, so that I can no longer be still before you.*

*Lord Jesus, you always live to intercede for us. Teach me to pray with you for the world, for the persecuted and for those who suffer. Make me still before you, and in every storm of this world let me hear your voice calling me to turn back. Amen.*
