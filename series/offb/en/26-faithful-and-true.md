## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday heaven sang: "the wedding of the Lamb has come." Today the Bridegroom himself comes. "And I saw heaven opened, and there was a white horse." In chapter 4 John saw a door open in heaven, so that he could go in and see the throne. Now heaven itself opens, so that the King can come out. This is the picture of Christ's return – which, together with the last judgement and the new creation, is the part of the great story that still lies ahead of us.

In the cities of Asia a rider on a white horse was the picture of a conqueror. Rome's generals and emperors celebrated their victories with triumphal processions through the city, and Roman poets sang of "eternal" Rome. But this Rider bears a name no emperor could bear: *Faithful and True*. That is exactly how the Lord Jesus introduced himself to Laodicea, as "the faithful and true witness" (3:14). The one who knocked at the door of the lukewarm church is the same one who comes as King.

The description is woven from the prophets. Isaiah says of the Messiah that he will strike the earth with the rod of his mouth and kill the wicked with the breath of his lips (Isaiah 11:4), and the Servant of the LORD says that God made his mouth a sharp sword (Isaiah 49:2). In Psalm 2:9 the Greek translation reads that the king will shepherd the nations with an iron rod. In Isaiah 63:1–3 the Lord comes from Edom with garments stained red, because he has trodden the winepress alone. And the terrible call to the birds comes from Ezekiel 39:17–20, where God invites the birds to eat the flesh of Gog's armies.

Notice too what John does not describe: a battle. The beast and the kings of the earth gather "to make war" – the same war we heard of at Armageddon (16:14, 16) and shall meet again tomorrow at Gog and Magog (20:8). In all three places the Greek has *ton polemon* (τὸν πόλεμον), "the war", and so many interpreters see here one and the same final conflict from different angles. And each time there is really no fight at all. The beast is captured, the false prophet with it, and the rest fall by the sword from the Rider's mouth.

### Insights from the Greek

- **Faithful and True (*pistos … kai alēthinos* – πιστὸς … καὶ ἀληθινός, v. 11):** *Pistos* is someone who keeps his word; *alēthinos* is what is genuine, the real thing as against the counterfeit. In a book full of counterfeits – a beast that looks as though slain (13:3), a false prophet with horns like a lamb (13:11) – he is the Genuine One.
- **Dipped in blood (*bebammenon haimati* – βεβαμμένον αἵματι, v. 13):** His robe is already red before there is any fight. In Isaiah 63 it is the blood of the enemies, but many interpreters think here of his own blood, by which he purchased people for God (5:9; 12:11). The Rider is the Lamb.
- **The Word of God (*ho Logos tou Theou* – ὁ Λόγος τοῦ Θεοῦ, v. 13):** This recalls John 1:1, 14. The one through whom God created all things is also the one through whom God judges evil.
- **A sharp sword (*rhomphaia oxeia* – ῥομφαία ὀξεῖα, v. 15):** John already saw it in chapter 1, "out of his mouth came a sharp two-edged sword" (1:16), and Pergamum was warned with it (2:16). The weapon is not in his hand but in his mouth: it is his word.
- **Shepherd (*poimanei* – ποιμανεῖ, v. 15):** Literally "he will be shepherd". It is the same verb as in 7:17, where the Lamb leads his people to springs of the water of life. The shepherd who protects is also the shepherd who drives off the wolves.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **The King who comes is the Lamb who was slain.** The eyes like a flame of fire (1:14; 2:18), the robe full of blood and the name "King of kings and Lord of lords" all point to the same one. The dragon had seven diadems (12:3), the beast ten (13:1), but on the Rider's head are "many diadems": he alone is truly king. And he has "a name written that no one knows except himself" – we shall never finish knowing him.

2. **Christ conquers by his word.** The armies that follow him carry no weapons, only "fine linen, white and clean" – the same robe as the bride's (19:8). They are his people, "called and chosen and faithful" (17:14). They do not fight; they follow. For the word of God is living and effective and sharper than any double-edged sword (Hebrews 4:12). What has carried the church through the centuries was not power but testimony.

3. **There are two suppers.** Yesterday: "Blessed are those who are invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb." Today: "Come, gather for the great supper of God." The harsh images from Ezekiel 39 are not meant to whet our appetite for blood; they say that evil is utterly and finally defeated, and that no rank – "free and slave, small and great" – protects anyone who rises against the Lamb. But the first invitation in the chapter is to the wedding. Everyone is invited there first.

4. **The end of evil is certain.** History is not an eternal draw between good and evil. The beast, which to the first readers looked like invincible Rome, is captured without a fight. What looks like the eternal power of the world has an end; Christ has none.

## Living It Out

We live in a world of broken promises. Politicians promise and forget, employers promise and restructure, and we ourselves promise more than we can keep. That is why the Rider's name is such a comfort: *Faithful and True*. What he has said, he will do. What he promised to Smyrna, to Philadelphia and to you stands firm.

The chapter also asks how we fight against evil. It is so easy to pick up the beast's weapons: anger, manipulation, the harsh word that wins the argument but loses the person. The armies behind the Rider wear only their white robes. Our fight is fought with truth, with love, and with what 13:10 calls "the endurance and the faith of the saints".

And if the powers of our time frighten you – the economy, politics, an illness, a person who makes your life bitter – remember that they share the beast's fate. They are great, but not eternal. There is only one on whose thigh it is written: *King of kings and Lord of lords*.

Choose one conversation this week in which you are tempted to win with harsh words. Pray Hebrews 4:12 beforehand, and then speak one true word in love instead of the word that wounds.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Which of God's promises do you find hard to believe at the moment – and what does it mean to you that he is called *Faithful and True* (v. 11)?
- When did you last fight with the beast's weapons – anger, manipulation, power – rather than with the sword from his mouth (v. 15)?
- Which power in your life looks as invincible as the beast just now, and what changes when you read verse 20?
- Which supper does your life take part in – the wedding supper of the Lamb, or the table of the world (vv. 9, 17)?

## Prayer

*Lord Jesus, Faithful and True, Word of God, King of kings and Lord of lords: you are coming, and you come as the Lamb who was slain for me. Your eyes see through everything, and your word is stronger than all the powers of this world. I worship you.*

*Forgive me that I am so often afraid of powers you have already defeated, and that I fight with the weapons of this world – with anger, with clever words, with the desire to win. Forgive me for the promises I have broken, while you have never broken a single one of yours.*

*Let me follow you, dressed in the white robe you have given me, with only your word as my sword. Thank you that I am invited to the wedding supper. Keep me faithful until I see you coming. Amen.*
