## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

After the first four trumpets – hail, blood, bitter water and darkness, as in Egypt – an eagle in midheaven cried, "Woe, woe, woe". Now the first two of those woes arrive, and it is the darkest page so far.

At the fifth trumpet John sees "a star that had fallen from heaven to the earth, and the key to the shaft of the abyss was given to him". In Revelation stars often stand for angels (1:20), and in 20:1 an angel comes with the same key. Others think of a fallen power, like the morning star of Isaiah 14:12 that fell from heaven. Either way, the key is *given*. Even the abyss opens only when God allows it.

From the shaft smoke rises "like the smoke of a great furnace" – the smoke of Sodom (Genesis 19:28) – and out of the smoke come locusts. Here the eighth plague of Egypt (Exodus 10:12–15) merges with the prophet Joel's vision of a locust army charging like horses, with teeth like lions, running ahead of the day of the LORD (Joel 1:4–6; 2:4–11). But the locusts of Revelation do not eat grass. They are to harm "only the people who do not have the seal of God on their foreheads". What we saw on day 13 comes true here: God's people are marked. Their king is *Abaddon*, Hebrew for "destruction" (Job 26:6), in Greek *Apollyon*, "the Destroyer". Many interpreters hear in it a dig at the god Apollo, with whom emperors, especially Nero, liked to be identified: the power that Rome worships is in reality a destroyer.

At the sixth trumpet a voice sounds "from the horns of the golden altar" – the same altar where yesterday the prayers rose. Four angels "bound at the great river, the Euphrates" are released. For John's readers the Euphrates was the eastern frontier of the empire, beyond which the dreaded Parthian horsemen waited; for Israel it was the river across which Assyria and Babylon had come (Isaiah 8:7). John hears the number of the mounted troops – two hundred million – an army too large to grasp, with fire, smoke and sulphur as over Sodom (Genesis 19:24).

And then come the most shocking words of the chapter: "And the rest of humankind, who were not killed by these plagues, did not even repent of the works of their hands". Like Pharaoh, whose heart grew harder after every plague (Exodus 7:13; 9:35), the world clings to its idols.

### Insights from the Greek

- **Torment (*basanismos* – βασανισμὸς, v. 5):** A word for pain that goes deep and will not stop; the same word family is used for the woman in torment in childbirth (12:2). The locusts may not kill, only torment for five months – a limited time. The pain is real, but it is limited, and it is still a warning, not the end.
- **The abyss (*tēs abyssou* – τῆς ἀβύσσου, vv. 1–2):** In the Greek Old Testament *abyssos* is the primeval deep (*tehom*) of Genesis 1:2, the chaos before God's creating word, and in Jesus' day the place to which the demons did not want to be sent (Luke 8:31). Behind the locusts stands the power of chaos that wants to undo creation.
- **They did not repent (*oude metenoēsan* – οὐδὲ μετενόησαν, v. 20; *ou metenoēsan* – οὐ μετενόησαν, v. 21):** *Metanoeō* is a turning round of mind and life. It is the same verb that Jesus used in five of the seven letters (2:5, 16, 21; 3:3, 19). The call to repent is addressed first to the church, then to the world.
- **Sorceries (*pharmakōn* – φαρμάκων, v. 21):** The word comes from *pharmakon*, a remedy, poison or potion. In the ancient world it was the magic by which people tried to manipulate the gods and fate – like Nineveh, the "mistress of sorceries" (Nahum 3:4).

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Evil has a key, but God holds the whole bunch.** The abyss is deep and the destroyer is cruel, but every step is limited: how long, whom, how many. The churches in Asia did not need to think that Rome, or the demonic powers behind Rome, had the last word.

2. **Judgement alone does not change the heart.** This chapter is disturbingly honest. Plagues, pain and death do not automatically turn people to God; they can harden them, as they hardened Pharaoh. Suffering is a call, but not a guarantee.

3. **People become like the idols they worship.** The idols of gold and silver and bronze and stone and wood, "which can neither see nor hear nor walk", come from Daniel 5:23 and Psalm 115:4–7, where it also says: those who make them become like them (Psalm 115:8). Behind the idols stand demons (Deuteronomy 32:17). And idolatry does not stay in the temple: it leads to murders, sorceries, sexual immorality and thefts – God's commandments broken one after another.

4. **What the plagues cannot do, the Lamb does.** The trumpets do not end in repentance. But the story goes on: in chapters 10 and 11 God sends his witnesses, and afterwards people "gave glory to the God of heaven" (11:13). It is not fear but God's kindness that leads to repentance (Romans 2:4). The Lamb conquers through witness and self-giving, not through locusts.

## Living It Out

We all know the temptation to think that a great shock will make people turn round: "If only they see how bad things are, they will come back to God." But Revelation 9 and our own experience say otherwise. Crises come and go, and most people simply carry on. So do we. How often has an illness, an accident or a loss shaken us awake – and a few weeks later everything was back as it was?

So the question is not only about "the world out there". What are the works of your hands that you trust in? Today they are seldom images of stone and wood, but it may be your career, your savings, your reputation, the phone in your hand. It can neither see nor hear nor walk, and yet we cling to it as though our life depended on it. And slowly we become like it: deaf to God, blind to our neighbour.

The good news is that Jesus did not only tell the churches to repent; he also said, "Look, I am standing at the door and knocking" (3:20). Repentance is not a price we pay to put God in a good mood; it is turning round towards someone who is already waiting for us. Joel's locusts ended with the same invitation: tear your hearts, return to the LORD, for he is gracious and compassionate (Joel 2:13).

Ask yourself honestly today: which work of my hands has taken my heart captive? Name it before God, and take one small step away from it – and towards him.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Which crisis in your life turned you to God – and which one made you harder instead (v. 20)?
- What are the works of your hands – things you have made or earned yourself – that you trust more than God (v. 20)?
- In what ways do you try, as with *pharmaka*, to control your life and your fate yourself instead of trusting God (v. 21)?
- Do you hear Jesus' call to repent as a threat or as an invitation (2:5; 3:19–20)?

## Prayer

*Lord God, you hold the keys of heaven and of the abyss. Nothing happens outside the limits you set, and your people are marked with your seal. Thank you that I need not fear the powers of darkness, for they are on your leash.*

*My heart is harder than I want to admit, Lord. I have gone through hard times without turning to you, and I cling to the works of my hands – things that can neither see nor hear nor save. I confess that I have sometimes become like my idols: deaf to your voice and blind to my neighbour. Forgive me.*

*Lord Jesus, you are standing at the door and knocking. Soften my heart and give me the grace to repent – not out of fear, but because you are good and compassionate. Make me a witness of your love in a world that does not want to turn round. In Jesus' name, Amen.*
