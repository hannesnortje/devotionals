## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

On earth the kings, the merchants and the sailors stand far off and weep over the ruins of Babylon. But John hears heaven's answer. Where the earth mourns, there sounds something like "the loud voice of a great crowd in heaven": "Hallelujah! The salvation and the glory and the power belong to our God."

This was no abstract poetry. The believers in Asia lived in cities where Rome's wealth rolled past every day – in the markets of Ephesus, in the banks of Laodicea, in the harbour of Smyrna. Some of their brothers and sisters had already lost their lives for their witness. In chapter 6 the souls under the altar cried out: "How long, holy and true Master, will you not judge and avenge our blood …?" (6:10). Here is the answer: "for true and just are his judgements … and he has avenged the blood of his servants." Their prayer has not been forgotten.

The word *Hallelujah* occurs in the whole New Testament only here, four times in six verses. It comes from the Psalms (e.g. Psalm 104:35; 106:1), where it is a call: Praise the LORD! Psalms 113–118, the so-called Hallel, were sung at Passover according to Jewish tradition – the feast of the exodus from Egypt. It fits: the fall of Babylon is the last exodus, and God's people sing as Israel sang on the shore of the sea (Exodus 15; cf. 15:3).

Then the music changes key. The song about judgement becomes a wedding song: "For the Lord God, the Almighty has begun to reign. Let us rejoice and be glad … for the wedding of the Lamb has come, and his wife has made herself ready." The Old Testament prepared this picture long ago. God calls himself Israel's husband (Isaiah 54:5) and promises in Hosea 2 to betroth her to himself for ever. Ezekiel describes how God washed the neglected girl Jerusalem and clothed her in fine linen (Ezekiel 16:8–13) – and then the painful word: she used her beauty to play the harlot (Ezekiel 16:15). The Bible begins with a wedding in the garden (Genesis 2:22–24), it is torn apart by unfaithfulness, and it ends with the wedding of the Lamb. John the Baptist called Jesus the Bridegroom (John 3:29), and Jesus himself told of a king's wedding feast (Matthew 22:1–14).

### Insights from the Greek

- **Hallelujah (*Hallēlouia* – Ἁλληλουϊά, vv. 1, 3, 4, 6):** A Greek rendering of the Hebrew *hallelu-yah*, "praise Yah", with the short form of God's Name. John writes Greek, but he leaves the Hebrew word standing: heaven sings in the language of Israel's Psalms.
- **Has begun to reign (*ebasileusen* – ἐβασίλευσεν, v. 6):** The verb form here probably describes the beginning of an action. God has always reigned – "the LORD is king" (Psalm 93:1; 97:1) – but now his rule becomes visible and undisputed. No other throne competes with his any more.
- **She has made herself ready … it was given to her (*hētoimasen heautēn … edothē autē* – ἡτοίμασεν ἑαυτήν … ἐδόθη αὐτῇ, vv. 7–8):** Two sides of one thing. The bride makes herself ready, but the robe is *given* to her – the passive form we meet all through Revelation when God is the Giver.
- **Fine linen, shining and clean (*byssinon lampron katharon* – βύσσινον λαμπρὸν καθαρόν, v. 8):** The same cloth was on Babylon's cargo list (18:12), and the great city was dressed in it (18:16). The prostitute buys her splendour with the wealth of the nations; the bride receives hers as a gift.
- **Righteous acts (*ta dikaiōmata tōn hagiōn* – τὰ δικαιώματα τῶν ἁγίων, v. 8):** The same word as for God's "righteous acts" in 15:4. The faithfulness of the saints is not an achievement of their own, but a reflection of God's righteousness in their lives.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Heaven's Hallelujah is the answer to the martyrs' "How long?"** The praise over Babylon's fall is not gloating. It is the joy that evil does not have the last word, that God's judgements are "true and just", and that no tear and no blood of his servants is forgotten. Whoever has never suffered injustice may find it hard to sing about judgement; the churches in Asia understood.

2. **The goal of history is not just a victory, but a wedding.** God's plan does not end on a battlefield, but at a feast. What began in Eden, what was torn apart by unfaithfulness and restored at the cross, is completed here. Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her (Ephesians 5:25); now she stands ready.

3. **Two women, two robes.** In chapter 17 the prostitute was "dressed in purple and scarlet and adorned with gold" (17:4); here the bride stands in simple, clean linen. The one buys her splendour at the expense of others; the other receives hers by grace.

4. **God alone is worshipped.** Even John, overwhelmed by the vision, falls down before the angel. The answer is short: "Do not do that! I am a fellow servant with you." Angels, prophets and preachers are fellow servants, not objects of worship. And "the testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy": all true prophecy points to Jesus, and whoever bears witness to Jesus stands in the line of the prophets.

## Living It Out

The church does not always look like a bride. We know the scandals, the divisions, the weary congregations where fewer people turn up every year. It is easy to become cynical about the church – or to leave her quietly. Revelation 19 lets us see the church as Christ sees her: as his bride, for whom he paid with his blood and for whom he is coming back. If the Lamb loves her, we may not despise her.

The bride's robe also brings comfort for your own ordinary faithfulness. The prayers no one hears, the visit to the hospital, the honest answer at work, the patience with a difficult child – none of it vanishes into nothing. These are threads in the "fine linen". Not because you earn your place at the table with them – the robe is given – but because God weaves your small acts of love into the splendour of his bride.

And then there is the invitation: "Blessed are those who are invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb." In Laodicea the Lord Jesus promised to eat with everyone who opens the door (3:20). Here we see where that meal leads. Every time the church celebrates the Lord's Supper, we taste something in advance of the feast that is coming.

Think today of one fellow believer you find hard to love, and pray for him or her as part of the bride of Christ.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- What are you still crying "How long?" about – and does heaven's Hallelujah help you to believe that God's judgements are true and just (vv. 1–2)?
- Do you see the church more as a failure or as the bride of the Lamb (v. 7)? What would change if you saw her through his eyes?
- Are you trying to earn your robe yourself, or do you receive it as a gift (v. 8)?
- Which good things – leaders, experiences, even your ministry – are in danger of taking the place that belongs to God alone (v. 10)?

## Prayer

*Hallelujah! Lord God, the Almighty, the salvation and the glory and the power belong to you. Your judgements are true and just, and you do not forget the blood and the tears of your servants. With the great crowd in heaven I sing today that you reign.*

*I am sometimes cynical about your church, and I try to weave my own robe out of achievements and good works. Forgive me. Forgive me when I worship other things and other people, however good they are, instead of you alone.*

*Lord Jesus, Lamb of God, thank you that you love your bride and gave yourself for her. Thank you for the clean robe I could never have bought. Make me ready for the day of the wedding, and let my small acts of faithfulness today be part of her splendour. In your name, Amen.*
