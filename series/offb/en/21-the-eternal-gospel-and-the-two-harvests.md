## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday we saw the Lamb on Zion, with his people singing a new song. Now three angels fly over the earth, one after another, and then come two harvests. This is the whole message of Revelation in one chapter. Anyone who wonders why Revelation repeats the judgement so often – seals, trumpets, bowls, harvest, the fall of Babylon, the rider on the white horse, the great white throne – finds the key here. It is not a timetable of disasters coming one after the other, but the same reality seen each time through a different lens, and each time it ends at the end itself. In Reformed interpretation this is often called "progressive parallelism" (William Hendriksen, *More than Conquerors*). As one looks at a precious stone from different sides, John shows the judgement from the side of the world, from the side of the martyrs and from the side of the Lamb.

The first angel flies *"in midheaven"* – in the same place where the eagle cried its "woe" (8:13) – *"with an eternal gospel"*. His message is: *"Fear God and give him glory, for the hour of his judgement has come; and worship the one who made heaven and earth and sea and the springs of water!"* That sounds like Jonah. On the ship he confessed that he fears the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land (Jonah 1:9), and in Nineveh he proclaimed a judgement: forty more days, and Nineveh will be overthrown (Jonah 3:4). The announcement of judgement was the invitation – and Nineveh repented. But a century later Nahum tells of the same Nineveh, which had fallen back into blood and sorcery and fell (Nahum 3:1–4). The same two possibilities stand before the nations in Revelation 14: turn back like Nineveh in Jonah, or fall like Nineveh in Nahum.

The second angel cries, *"Fallen, fallen is Babylon the Great"* – words from Isaiah 21:9. It is the first time Babylon is named in Revelation; in chapters 17 and 18 we will see her full portrait. For the first readers Babylon was Rome, the city that, like Babylon of old, had destroyed God's temple (cf. 1 Peter 5:13). She makes the nations drink *"of the wine of the fury of her harlotry"* – like Babylon's golden cup that made the whole earth drunk (Jeremiah 51:7). And notice the verb: she *has* fallen, while Rome still stood in full glory. This is prophetic certainty. God's verdict has already been given.

The third angel brings the most serious warning in the book. He says that whoever worships the beast will also drink *"of the wine of the fury of God"*: whoever drinks from Babylon's cup also receives God's cup. Then come two harvests. On a white cloud sits *"one like a son of man"* (Daniel 7:13) with a sharp sickle, and the harvest of the earth is gathered in. After that the clusters of the vine are thrown into *"the great winepress of the fury of God"* – an image from Joel 3:13 and Isaiah 63:1–6, where the LORD treads the winepress alone. The blood comes out *"up to the horses' bridles, for a distance of a thousand six hundred stadia"* – four times four times a hundred: the four corners of the earth, a judgement over the whole world.

For the churches in Asia this was a reversal of everything they saw every day. The worshippers of the beast lived comfortably, with their guilds, feasts and markets, while the believers were excluded, accused and sometimes killed. Now the tables are turned: those who worship the beast *"have no rest day or night"*, but the dead who die in the Lord may *"rest from their toil"* (v. 13).

### Insights from the Greek

- **An eternal gospel (*euangelion aiōnion* – εὐαγγέλιον αἰώνιον, v. 6):** The only time the word "gospel" occurs in Revelation – and it is proclaimed *"to every nation and tribe and language and people"*. Even in the shadow of the beast, and even when the hour of judgement is announced, the door still stands open.
- **Undiluted (*kekerasmenou akratou* – κεκερασμένου ἀκράτου, v. 10):** In the ancient world wine was usually mixed with water. Here God's wrath is *"poured undiluted"*. The earlier judgements were limited – a quarter, a third – and mixed with mercy, so that people could turn back. At the end the cup is no longer diluted.
- **No rest day or night (*ouk echousin anapausin hēmeras kai nyktos* – οὐκ ἔχουσιν ἀνάπαυσιν ἡμέρας καὶ νυκτός, v. 11):** Almost the same words are used of the four living creatures: *"day and night they never rest"*, because they cry "Holy" without ceasing (4:8). The one restlessness is worship, the other torment. And over against both stands the rest (*anapausis*) of the dead who die in the Lord (v. 13).
- **The winepress (*lēnon* – ληνόν, vv. 19–20):** In Isaiah 63 the LORD treads the winepress alone, and his garments are stained red. In 19:15 it is Christ himself who *"treads the winepress of the wine of the fury"*. And here the winepress is trodden *"outside the city"* – where Jesus himself suffered (Hebrews 13:12).

### Core Theological Truths

1. **The judgement is repeated because it stands firm.** Revelation does not tell of the end once, but seven times, each time in a different image. Not so that we count and calculate, but so that we look: through every lens we see the same thing – God's judgement on evil is certain, and his people are kept.

2. **The judgement is announced so that people can turn back.** The eternal gospel comes first. As with Jonah, the announcement of judgement is an invitation: fear God, give him glory, worship the Creator. God does not warn with gloating, but because he wants people to live (Ezekiel 33:11).

3. **The judgement is real, final and just – and the Lamb is the Judge.** Revelation speaks in its own images of the end of those who worship the beast: the cup, the fire and sulphur as over Sodom (Genesis 19:24), the smoke that goes up *"for ever and ever"*. We need not explain more than the text says, but neither may we say less. The judgement is real – not an image that dissolves. It is final: a day is coming when the choice is fixed. It is just: each is judged according to what they chose, and the victims receive justice. And it happens *"before the Lamb"* (v. 10): the Judge is the one who was slaughtered for sinners. No one will be able to say that grace was not offered to them.

4. **The tables are turned – and the Lamb drank the cup himself.** Those who now seem at ease will have no rest; those who now suffer will rest. But the deepest comfort lies *"outside the city"*. There Jesus himself suffered, and in Gethsemane he prayed that the cup might pass from him – and drank it all the same (Mark 14:36). Whoever takes refuge in him need not drink the cup of wrath, for he has drunk it in our place.

## Living It Out

For the churches in Asia this chapter was promise and warning at once. The promise: your suffering is not the last word, and the tables will be turned. The warning: do not drink from Babylon's cup, not even a small sip at the guild's temple meal. *"Here is the endurance of the saints"* (v. 12).

We too live between the angels of this chapter: the one who proclaims the eternal gospel, and the one who says that Babylon has fallen. Babylon's cup looks different today – the promise of wealth, status and security without God – but it still makes people drunk. It numbs us, so that we no longer notice how dependent we have become. The repeated judgements of Revelation do not want to frighten us or have us calculate when the end will come, but to wake us up: the cup is not harmless.

Are you not yet sure to whom you belong? Then this chapter is for you like Jonah's sermon in Nineveh: a serious announcement that is at the same time an invitation. The judgement is real, but the eternal gospel comes first, and the door is still open. Nineveh turned back – and God spared the city (Jonah 3:10).

And then there is the comfort of verse 13 for everyone who has lost a loved one in the Lord: *"Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on."* They rest, and their works go with them. Nothing done in love for the Lamb is lost.

Ask yourself today: From which cup am I drinking? And tonight, pray for someone you know who has not yet heard the eternal gospel – as Jonah was sent to Nineveh.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Which cup of Babylon tempts you most – and what does it mean to you that in the end it is God's cup of wrath (vv. 8, 10)?
- How does the repetition of the images of judgement help you not to get stuck in fear or calculations?
- For whom in your life can you be like Jonah – someone who needs to hear both the warning and the invitation of the eternal gospel (vv. 6–7)?
- How does verse 13 comfort you about someone you have lost – or about your own death?
