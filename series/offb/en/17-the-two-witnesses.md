## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

A reed is put into John's hand: "Get up and measure the temple of God and the altar and those who worship in it." He has just eaten the little scroll – sweet in the mouth, bitter in the stomach – and heard that he must prophesy again about many peoples and nations and languages and kings (10:11). Chapter 11 shows what such a prophetic calling means. John hears of two witnesses who prophesy in sackcloth, are killed, lie in the street – and stand up again.

The measuring reed comes from Ezekiel 40–42, where the new temple is measured, and from Zechariah 2, where Jerusalem is measured as a sign that God will protect the city. To measure something is to say: this belongs to me, and I will keep it. But the outer court is not measured; the nations "will trample the holy city for forty-two months". The first readers knew what that felt like. The temple in Jerusalem had been destroyed by Rome in AD 70, and they themselves were shut out and trampled in their own cities. Yet in the New Testament the temple of God is the church itself (1 Corinthians 3:16). God's people are at once safe within – no one can touch their worship – and exposed without, where they suffer.

Forty-two months, a thousand two hundred and sixty days: this is Daniel's "a time and times and half a time" (Daniel 7:25; 12:7), the measured time of affliction under a godless ruler. Revelation uses it for the whole time of the church's witness between Christ's first and second coming – hard, but not endless. (Futurist interpreters take it as a literal three and a half years at the end of history, with two individual prophets, according to some Moses and Elijah themselves.)

Who are the two witnesses? John gives the key himself: "These are the two olive trees and the two lampstands". And the lampstands are the churches (1:20). The olive trees come from Zechariah 4, where the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua rebuild the temple, not by might nor by power, but by God's Spirit (Zechariah 4:6). Their signs are those of Elijah, who shut the sky (1 Kings 17:1), and of Moses, who turned the water into blood (Exodus 7:17–20). And there are two because the law requires two witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15). So the two witnesses are probably the witnessing church itself.

### Insights from the Greek

- **Witnesses (*martysin* – μάρτυσίν, v. 3):** We translate "witness", not "martyr". But this chapter shows how the two words grew together in the early church: whoever bears faithful witness sometimes pays the price – like Antipas, "my witness, my faithful one" (2:13), and like Jesus himself, "the faithful witness" (1:5).
- **Spiritually (*pneumatikōs* – πνευματικῶς, v. 8):** The great city is "spiritually called Sodom and Egypt" – not on the map, but through the eyes of the Spirit. Sodom stands for pride and hardness towards the poor (Ezekiel 16:49), Egypt for slavery. It is Jerusalem, "where their Lord too was crucified", but also Rome, and every city that tramples God's witnesses.
- **Breath of life from God (*pneuma zōēs ek tou theou* – πνεῦμα ζωῆς ἐκ τοῦ θεοῦ, v. 11):** The language of Ezekiel 37, where God's breath enters the dry bones and they stand on their feet (Ezekiel 37:5, 10). What God promised the "dead" Israel in exile, he does for his witnesses.
- **Who is and who was (*ho ōn kai ho ēn* – ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν, v. 17):** Until now God has been the one "who is and who was and who is coming" (1:4, 8). Here "and who is coming" drops away. He has come; he has "begun to reign".

### Core Theological Truths

1. **The church is a prophetic witness, not a safe fortress.** The two witnesses wear sackcloth – the clothing of mourning and repentance, like Nineveh when Jonah preached (Jonah 3:5). Those who live on the earth experience their message as torment (v. 10). Yet this is the calling of the church in every age: to bear witness to the truth, even when it makes no one popular.

2. **The witnesses follow their Lord on his way – into death and through death.** They die in the city "where their Lord too was crucified", lie in the street for three and a half days, and then go up to heaven in the cloud, like Elijah (2 Kings 2:11) and like Jesus himself (Acts 1:9). The beast will "conquer" them (v. 7) – but just as the Lamb conquered by being slaughtered (5:5–6), their defeat becomes their victory.

3. **God's aim is repentance.** In Elijah's day God spared seven thousand who had not bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18). Here it is turned around: seven thousand die, "and the rest were terrified and gave glory to the God of heaven." According to many interpreters this is the first time in the book that people repent – not through the plagues (9:20–21), but through the testimony of believers who were ready to die.

4. **The end is a kingdom, not a catastrophe.** The seventh trumpet brings no further plague, but a song: "The kingdom of the world has become our Lord's and his Christ's" (cf. Psalm 2:2; Daniel 7:14). Then the temple in heaven is opened and "the ark of his covenant" is seen – the ark that was probably lost when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem (586 BC). God has never forgotten his covenant.

## Living It Out

Many believers feel like the outer court that is not measured: outside, trampled, overlooked. At work you may weigh every word before you speak about your faith; at school your child may be laughed at for praying before lunch. Revelation 11 does not promise that this will not happen. It promises something deeper: the inner part has been measured. No one can take your place at the altar from you.

The two witnesses also teach us that witness is not about success. They prophesy for years – and then it looks as if it was all for nothing, and the world sends gifts to one another. That is the experience of many faithful people: the minister whose congregation is shrinking, the parents who have prayed for a child for years, the colleague who is the only one not going along with dishonesty. But the last word is not the party in the street. It is the voice from heaven: "Come up here!"

So you do not have to wait until you are strong and popular before you bear witness. A lampstand does not have to be the sun; it only has to burn. An honest word about Jesus, a refusal to join in a lie, a quiet act of service for a neighbour – that is light in a dark city, not by might nor by power, but by God's Spirit.

Think today of one person around you who does not yet know Christ. Pray for him or her by name, and ask God for one opportunity to share something of your hope – gently, not fearfully.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Do you sometimes feel like the court that is trampled (v. 2)? What does it mean to you that God has measured the inner part?
- Where are you tempted to stay silent because your witness would make you unpopular (vv. 3, 10)?
- When has it looked to you as if the beast had won – and how does the voice saying "Come up here!" (v. 12) help you to keep going?
- Where would it make a difference today if you truly believed that the kingdom of the world has already become our Lord's (v. 15)?

## Prayer

*Lord God, the Almighty, who is and who was: I thank you that you have taken your great power and begun to reign. The kingdom of the world belongs to you and to your Christ, and nothing on this earth can change that.*

*I confess that I stay silent when I should bear witness, and that I would rather be popular than faithful. Forgive me for the days when I thought the beast had won and my witness was in vain.*

*Thank you that you keep my inner life, and that you give the breath of life to those who die for your name's sake. Make me a burning lampstand in my city – not by might nor by power, but by your Spirit – until I hear your voice: Come up here! Amen.*
