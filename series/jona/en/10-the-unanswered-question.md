## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today, on the last day of our journey, we reach the final three verses of the book of Jonah – one of the most dramatic and unexpected closing scenes in all of world literature. After the worm has destroyed the plant and the scorching east wind has driven Jonah to the edge of sunstroke and exhaustion, God repeats in verse 9 exactly the question he asked in verse 4 – but with two Hebrew words added: *"Is it right for your anger to burn **over the plant**?"*

Where Jonah walked away in stubborn silence in verse 4, the dam of his frustration now bursts. Without any restraint or reverence he as good as shouts back at heaven: *"It is right for my anger to burn – to the point of death!"* And with that, Jonah walks straight into the loving trap God has set for him. By admitting that the loss of a mere plant upsets him to the point of death, he gives the Lord the perfect starting point to lay open the deepest lesson of the book.

Literarily, the book of Jonah ends with an **unanswered rhetorical question**. In the whole Old Testament only two books end with a question mark: **Jonah** and **Nahum** – and remarkably, both books are about the city of Nineveh! (In Jonah, God asks who could hold back his grace from a repentant Nineveh; in Nahum 3:19 he asks who could rescue a later, rebellious Nineveh from his just judgement.)

Why doesn't the author tell us what Jonah answered on the hill? Did Jonah finally weep and come to his senses – or did he get up angry and walk away? The silence at the end of verse 11 is a deliberate literary masterstroke: at the last second the camera swings away from Jonah and focuses squarely on **you, the reader**. God's question hangs in the air so that each of us must answer it with our own life.

### Insights from the Hebrew

The Hebrew text of these final verses pulses with the emotion of God's Creator-heart:

- **"Have pity" (*chus* – חוּס):** This key word in verses 10 and 11 means to look at something with deep compassion and pity, to want to spare it – or (according to some linguists) the eye shedding a tear of mercy. The Lord says to Jonah: *"You had pity (chasta) on the plant … and should I not have pity (achus) on Nineveh?"* Jonah does have compassion in him – but it is aimed at the wrong object.
- **"For which you did not labour and which you did not make grow" (*lo-amalta bo we-lo giddalto* – לֹא־עָמַלְתָּ בּוֹ וְלֹא גִדַּלְתּוֹ):** The verb *amal* refers to heavy, sweat-of-the-brow toil, and *giddel* means to nourish and raise a child with care – literally "to make great", the verb behind *gadol*, "great", which runs through the whole book. Jonah didn't plant the seed, water it or make it grow – and yet he was attached to it. Over against him stands God as the Creator who formed every inhabitant of Nineveh in the womb, fed them and sustained their breath of life. God has a personal investment in his creation!
- **"It came into being in a night" (*she-bin-laylah hayah* – שֶׁבִּן־לַיְלָה הָיָה):** Literally, the Hebrew calls the plant **"a son of a night"** (*ben-laylah*) – a lovely idiom for something fleeting, temporary and short-lived. How foolish to pledge your soul to "a son of a night" while people created for eternity are perishing all around you!
- **"Who do not know their right hand from their left" (*lo-yada bein-yemino lismolo* – לֹא־יָדַע בֵּין־יְמִינוֹ לִשְׂמֹאלוֹ):** This expression can be understood in two ways:
  1. It refers to **little children** who are too young to tell left from right (which would mean the total population of greater Nineveh was over 600,000).
  2. It refers to the spiritual and moral blindness of all **120,000 inhabitants** – people who grew up without God's law and grope about spiritually like lost children in the dark.

  Either way the point is the same: where Jonah looks at their ignorance with contempt, God looks at it with a father's mercy.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **God's argument from the lesser to the greater (*qal wachomer*).** In verses 10 and 11 God uses the classic Jewish *qal wachomer* argument ("how much more") that Jesus also used so often in the Gospels (see Matthew 7:11; 12:12). If a sinful, selfish man like Jonah can feel natural pity for a single, soulless plant that is only one night old, **how much more** will the infinite, loving Creator have compassion on hundreds of thousands of people and animals who are the work of his own hands!
2. **Spiritual blindness moves God to mercy.** When Jesus looked at the crowds, he was moved with compassion for them, because they were harassed and scattered like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36). God does not excuse Nineveh's sin, but he sees their spiritual helplessness. When they turn to him in repentance, his Father's heart refuses to crush them.
3. **God's care includes his whole creation ("… and many animals as well").** The very last words of the book of Jonah in the original text refer to the **many animals** (*u-vehemah rabbah*). To us it sounds almost like a strange anticlimax, but theologically it is wonderful: God's mercy reaches even the innocent animals that would otherwise perish in judgement on human beings (see Psalm 36:6 – Hebrew 36:7 – *"Man and beast you save, O LORD"*, and Psalm 145:9).
4. **The book's very existence is Jonah's confession.** Did Jonah ever learn his lesson? Ask yourself: *Who told the story of Jonah?* Only Jonah himself could have known what happened in the innermost part of the ship, in the belly of the fish, and in the private conversation between him and God on the eastern hill. The fact that we have this mercilessly honest book in our Bibles today – in which the prophet exposes his own pride and small-heartedness without any excuses, so that God alone is glorified – is a strong indication that God's final question broke Jonah's hard heart in the end!

## Living It Out

God's final question to Jonah echoes down the centuries into our own living rooms and churches. We live in a world full of **"sons of a night"** – temporary plants to which we pledge our hearts. We shed tears, lose sleep and get upset "to the point of death" over things that won't make the slightest difference in fifty years: a bruised ego, a political argument on social media, a financial gain or loss, our personal comfort, or our own way of doing things. We are experts at caring passionately about leaves that will wither tomorrow, while we shrug our shoulders at people heading without Christ towards an eternity without God.

How do you look today at the "Ninevites" in your life and in our society – the people who "do not know their right hand from their left"? When we look at a world that has lost its moral bearings, that calls good evil and evil good, our first reaction is often just like Jonah's: outrage, anger and a desire that they get what they deserve. But the Spirit of Christ calls us to look at spiritually lost people with God's *chus* – his tears of mercy. They are not enemies to be hated; they are lost creatures of God who need the gospel.

The book of Jonah ends without a full stop, so that today you can go and write the next chapter with your feet and your heart. Will you stay sitting on the hill of your own comfort and bitterness, grieving over a dead plant? Or will you get up, join the Father's heart, and go and share his incomprehensible grace with a broken world?

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- **Which "sons of a night" are stealing my deepest emotional energy right now?** Which temporary, earthly things am I currently more upset and worried about than the eternal well-being of the people around me?
- **How do I react to the spiritual blindness of our time?** When I see people outside the church living in sin and confusion – people who "do not know their right hand from their left" –, does it stir up proud contempt in me, or Christlike compassion?
- **Have I accepted God's right to be gracious to whomever he wills?** Am I willing to lay down my own small standards of who "deserves" grace, and to rejoice in God's boundless Father-heart?
- **How will I answer the open closing question of Jonah today?** As I look back over this whole ten-day journey through Jonah 1 to 4 – from the flight to Tarshish to the hill east of Nineveh – what one specific change is the Lord asking of me today in my attitude, my prayer life and my obedience?

## Prayer

*Lord my God, Creator of heaven and earth, at the end of this journey through the book of Jonah I fall silent and humbled at your feet. Your final question lays my heart bare to the bone. With deep repentance I confess how often I am like Jonah – how easily I grieve, complain and grow angry when my own little "plants" of comfort, pride and earthly security are touched, while the need of a lost world leaves me untouched.*

*Forgive me for being so often caught up in the things that come into being in a night and perish in a night. Break my small-heartedness and heal my spiritual short-sightedness. Thank you that you are the God who cares not only for Israel or for your own people, but whose Father-heart beats for every city, for every lost soul groping in the dark – and even for the animals of the field.*

*Lord Jesus, thank you that you are the true and perfect Prophet – that you did not sit on a hill waiting for our destruction, but out of love for your enemies died on the cross to save us. Pour your own compassion into my heart today through the Holy Spirit. I no longer want to stay sitting on the hill of my own selfishness. Today I get up to love what you love, to show grace as you have shown grace to me, and to place my life wholly in the service of your kingdom. Salvation belongs to you alone, now and for ever! Amen.*
