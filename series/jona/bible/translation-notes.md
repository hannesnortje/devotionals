# Jona – translation notes (for review)

Base text: Masoretic Text, Westminster Leningrad Codex (Open Scriptures Hebrew Bible).
Versification: Hebrew in all three languages (2:1 = the fish; English Bibles 1:17).
Aim: close to the Hebrew, natural to read; the book's key words kept the same every time.

## 2. Choices you should check

1. **Keeping *ra'ah* the same (1:2 → 4:1).** This is the book's main word play. In 3:10 God turns from the *ra'ah* he threatened; in 4:1 it is *ra'ah* — "a great evil" — to Jona. In 4:6 the plant is meant to deliver Jona "from his *ra'ah*" (his misery). Keeping one word is less smooth in 4:1 and 4:6, but it makes the irony visible. Alternative: smoother wording in those two verses with a footnote.
2. **AF: "kwaad" vs anger.** In Afrikaans "kwaad" also means "angry". That is why *charah* is rendered "toorn ontvlam" and never "kwaad word", so the two Hebrew roots stay distinct.
3. **The plant, *qiqayon* (4:6–10).** Probably the castor-oil plant (Ricinus). DE „Rizinusstaude“ (as in many German Bibles); EN "leafy plant"; AF "komkommerplant" kept on purpose, because Afrikaans readers know it from 1933/53 and your devotional titles use it. Alternative AF: "kasterolieplant". Please decide.
4. **3:3 *ir gedolah le'lohim*.** Literally "a great city to/for God". Rendered "groß sogar vor Gott / great even before God / groot selfs voor God". It can also be a superlative ("an exceedingly great city"). I kept the literal sense because it prepares for God's concern in 4:11.
5. **3:4 *nehpakhet*.** „umgekehrt / overturned / omgekeer“. In German and Afrikaans the double meaning survives (destroyed *or* turned around, i.e. converted), which is what actually happens. English "overturned" only keeps the first sense; a footnote is worth considering.
6. **1:6 the captain's "god".** *ha'elohim* in a pagan's mouth: „dieser Gott / that god / daardie god“, lower case in EN and AF, where it is ambiguous. Alternative: capitalise.
7. **1:5 / 1:6 *yarad* and *radam*.** Jona "goes down" (*yarad*) and "falls asleep" (*radam*); the captain calls him *nirdam*, "sleeper". This sound play can't be reproduced, but the devotional can mention it.
8. **2:3 *Sheol*.** „Totenreich / the grave / doderyk“. Alternative EN: "Sheol".
9. **2:5 *akh*.** Masoretic text: "Yet I will look again …". Some scholars change it to *eikh*, "How shall I look again …?" I followed the Masoretic text.
10. **2:6 *nefesh*.** „bis an die Kehle / up to my throat / tot by die keel“ (*nefesh* = throat as well as soul). In 2:8 the same word is translated "soul".
11. **2:9 *chesed*.** "abandon their faithful love" — either their own loyalty or the God who shows them *chesed*. I kept it open, as the Hebrew does.
12. **4:10 *giddalto*.** "made great" (the *gadol* root again). DE „großgezogen“ and AF „grootgemaak“ keep it; EN "make grow" loses it.
13. **4:11 *ribbo*.** 12 × 10,000 = "more than 120,000". "Do not know their right hand from their left" is translated literally in all three, since the devotional Day 10 discusses it.

## 3. Style

- Verse 1:1 and 3:1 open with *wayhi* ("and it came to pass"); I have left the "and" out for natural style.
- The psalm in chapter 2 (2:3–10) is set as poetry.
- Speech uses modern quotation marks: „…“ (DE), "…" (EN, AF).
- In the German devotionals the reader is addressed as "du", as agreed.
