## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

This passage is one of the highest peaks of Messianic prophecy in the whole Old Testament. When, centuries later, the wise men from the East arrive in Jerusalem and ask King Herod where the newborn King of the Jews is to be found (Matthew 2:1–6), the scribes quote these very words of Micah without hesitation.

(A note on verse numbers: today's passage is 4:14–5:4a in the Hebrew text, which we follow; English Bibles number it 5:1–5a. From 5:1 onward in this chapter, add one to our verse number to find it in an English Bible.)

To appreciate the beauty of this diamond, though, we first have to see it against the pitch-black velvet of the opening verse, 4:14 (English 5:1). Micah sketches the moment of Judah's deepest political and national humiliation. Jerusalem is addressed as *"daughter of troops"* (*bat gedud*), huddling in fear behind her walls while the enemy lays a siege (*matsor*) around the city. In the ancient Near East it was a deep, public humiliation to be struck on the cheek or jaw (*lechi*) with a stick in front of others (cf. 1 Kings 22:24; Job 16:10) – all the more for a king or "judge" (*shofet*). It was visible proof that the ruler was utterly powerless to protect his people.

Against the proud capital Jerusalem, whose earthly king is struck on the cheek and humiliated, God now swings his camera away to a forgotten country village less than ten kilometres south of Jerusalem: **Bethlehem Ephrathah**. When David's royal house in Jerusalem had decayed over the centuries into pride, corruption and abuse of power (as we saw in Micah 1–3), God goes back to the humble roots where David was first called as a shepherd boy (1 Samuel 16). Where human kingship ends in disgrace, God's eternal kingship begins.

### Insights from the Hebrew

- **The wordplay on "troops" (*titgodedi bat gedud* – תִּתְגֹּדְדִי בַת גְּדוּד):** In the first verse (4:14; English 5:1) Micah uses a sharp Hebrew wordplay. The root *gadad* has two related meanings: "to band together in troops" and "to gash yourself with knives" (as pagan mourners did in times of utter despair). Our translation reads: *"Now gather your troops, daughter of troops!"* – but the second meaning echoes underneath. Jerusalem, which like a raiding band exploited its own poor (Micah 2–3), now sits trapped and gashes herself in grief and mortal fear.
- **Judge (*shofet* – שֹׁפֵט) and Bethlehem (*Beit Lechem* – בֵּית לֶחֶם):** Notice the echo in the Hebrew between the *shofet* (judge, ruler) who is struck on the *lechi* (cheek, jaw) and *Beit Lechem*, **"House of Bread"**. The name *Ephrathah* is usually linked with **"fruitfulness"**. Out of the place of simple bread and fruitfulness comes the true Bread of Life.
- **Small (*tsa'ir* – צָעִיר) among the clans (*be'alfei Yehudah* – בְּאַלְפֵי יְהוּדָה):** Bethlehem was so insignificant that it does not even appear in the Hebrew text of the list of Judah's towns in Joshua 15 (only the Greek translation, the Septuagint, names it at Joshua 15:59). The word *tsa'ir* means not only geographically small but also "the youngest, least, most insignificant" (the same word Gideon uses of himself in Judges 6:15). God deliberately chooses what is lowly in the world's eyes to put the mighty to shame.
- **From of old, from ancient days (*miqqedem mimei 'olam* – מִקֶּדֶם מִימֵי עוֹלָם):** In 5:1 (English 5:2) our translation reads: *"his origins are from of old, from ancient days."* The word *'olam* can mean "the distant past", but also "eternity". In 7:14 *kimei 'olam* clearly means "as in ancient days"; here, however, *mimei 'olam* can just as well mean "from the days of eternity". These words breathe a mystery that rises far above an ordinary earthly descendant of David. On the one hand his origin lies in the ancient covenant promise to David (*miqqedem*); on the other hand the phrase *mimei 'olam* reaches back into the eternity before creation itself (cf. Proverbs 8:22–23; John 1:1–2). The Baby born in Bethlehem in time is at the same time the eternal Son who exists before all time!
- **He will stand and shepherd (*we'amad wera'ah* – וְעָמַד וְרָעָה):** Unlike the frightened kings of Judah, who hid or collapsed in times of crisis, this Ruler will **stand** (*'amad* – unshaken, watchful and decisive) and **shepherd** his people (*ra'ah*). As a result his flock will *"dwell secure"* (*weyashavu* – וְיָשָׁבוּ – literally: they will be able to sit down at rest, without fear of predators).
- **And this one will be peace (*wehayah zeh shalom* – וְהָיָה זֶה שָׁלוֹם):** Look very carefully at the wording of verse 4a (English 5:5a). The text does not merely say that the Messiah will *bring* peace or *bring about* peace. It says literally: *"And this one will be peace."* **This One (*zeh*) is himself the Peace (*shalom*)!** Peace is not a political treaty; Peace is a Person.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **God's greatest acts of salvation begin in the most unlikely places:** The world looks for rescue in the palaces of Jerusalem, Assyria or today's centres of power; God sends his Ruler out of the quiet streets of Bethlehem. When God works, he needs neither human prestige nor impressive resources.
2. **The two natures of Christ – truly man and truly God:** Micah holds two wonderful truths side by side: the Messiah is born as a real human being in a particular Jewish village, of a woman in labour (5:2; English 5:3) – and his origin lies in eternity (*mimei 'olam*). More than that: the earthly judge struck on the cheek with a staff in 4:14 finds his deepest fulfilment when Jesus Christ – the true King of Israel – was struck in the face during his trial and beaten on the head with a reed (Matthew 26:67; 27:30), to take our shame upon himself.
3. **The true Shepherd rules by the strength and majesty of God, not by violence:** In contrast to the leaders of Micah 3, who slaughtered and devoured the sheep, Christ shepherds his flock in the strength (*'oz*) and in the majesty of the name (*ge'on shem*) of his Father. His rule extends not just over a small patch of land in the Near East, but *"to the ends of the earth"*.
4. **True *shalom* is inseparable from the presence of Jesus:** Paul reaches back to this thought when he writes in **Ephesians 2:14** that Christ himself is our peace. Where Christ reigns as Shepherd and King, there the soul rests in complete security.

## Living It Out

Micah 4:14–5:4a (English 5:1–5a) meets us today exactly where our own earthly securities are under siege. We all know those seasons of life when it feels as if a siege has been laid against us – when financial pressure, health crises, strain in a marriage or uncertainty about the future press in on us from every side. In such moments our own "inner ruler" (our self-confidence, our clever plans and our ability to keep things under control) is, as it were, struck on the cheek. We discover how defenceless we really are.

Precisely there Micah calls us to turn our eyes away from our own besieged "Jerusalem" and to look at **Bethlehem**.

First, this passage frees us from the lie that we have to be "big" or "impressive" before God can use us. Do your life, your gifts, your faith or your daily obedience sometimes feel like Bethlehem – *"too small to be among the clans of Judah"*? Do not despise small things. The God of the Bible specialises in doing eternal work through "Bethlehem places" and humble hearts.

Second, verses 3 and 4a (English 5:4–5a) change the way we look for **peace**. How often do we tell ourselves: *"I'll have peace once this problem is solved, once my bank account is healthy, or once that difficult person has changed"*? As long as your peace depends on the absence of storms, you will always stay anxious. But Micah shows us the secret of how sheep can "dwell secure" even in a dangerous world: they look up and see that their Shepherd is **standing** (*we'amad*). While we sit and rest, Jesus Christ stands guard in the invincible strength of God.

You don't have to wait today for your circumstances to turn peaceful first; **he is your Peace**. When he stands in the middle of your boat, your heart can dwell secure, even while the storm still rages outside.

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Is there an area of your life (or of your ministry and calling) that feels like "Bethlehem" to you – too small, too insignificant or too weak to make a difference? How does God's choice of Bethlehem encourage you today?
- What is your inner peace built on at the moment: on your outward circumstances going well, or on the Person of Jesus Christ, who is himself your Peace (*"And this one will be peace"*)?
- Look at the picture in verse 3 (English 5:4): the Shepherd **stands** and shepherds in the strength of the LORD, so that the flock can **dwell secure** (literally: sit). Are you anxiously trying to man the watch post yourself through worry and control, instead of resting under his shepherd's staff?
- How does your response to today's crises show the world around you that your King is not a powerless earthly leader, but the eternal Ruler whose power reaches to the ends of the earth?

## Prayer

*Lord Jesus Christ, Son of David and eternal King from Bethlehem Ephrathah, I worship you today with all my heart! When our earthly strength was broken and our human pride plunged into disgrace, you stepped out of eternity into our fragile world. You allowed yourself to be struck on the cheek and crowned with thorns, so that I may live for ever as a redeemed child of God.*

*Thank you that you do not despise small and lowly places; come and live again today in the "Bethlehem" of my broken and dependent heart. My Good Shepherd, thank you that you stand today and watch over my life in the almighty power and majesty of the Father. Forgive me for all the times I went looking for peace in changing circumstances, while you yourself are my Peace.*

*Teach my soul to dwell secure under your shepherd's staff today. Be the Peace in my thoughts, in my home and in every storm that beats against my walls. I rest in you alone. Amen.*
