## Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

### Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

At the start of chapter 6, Paul rounds off his instructions for the different groups in the church by addressing the sensitive issue of slaves and their masters. In the first-century Roman Empire, slavery was the economic backbone of society; by some estimates a large part of the population of cities like Ephesus was enslaved. When a slave came to faith and heard that in Christ he was completely free and an equal brother (Galatians 3:28), the temptation arose to rebel against his master – or, if the master was also a Christian, to let his work slide and take advantage of their brotherhood. Paul knows that a violent social uprising at that time would have destroyed the young church and made the gospel look like nothing more than a political protest movement. So he calls slaves to do their work with such excellence and respect that the name of God is honoured – while hollowing out the institution of slavery from within by binding masters and slaves to one another as "brothers" and "beloved" (see the letter to Philemon).

From verse 3 on, Paul returns one last, devastating time to the false teachers in Ephesus. So far we have heard about their speculative genealogies (chapter 1) and their legalistic asceticism (chapter 4). Now Paul pulls back the final curtain and exposes the real motive behind their whole ministry: **they did it for money**. In the ancient world, travelling philosophers and orators often sought wealthy patrons and sold their lectures to fund a lavish lifestyle. The false teachers in Ephesus exploited the church in exactly the same way.

### Insights from the Greek

The Greek text exposes the sharp contrast between this corrupt pursuit of profit and true spiritual wealth:

- **"Sound words" versus "unhealthy craving" (*hygiainousi logois* / *nosōn* – ὑγιαίνουσι λόγοις / νοσῶν, vv. 3–4):** Paul sets "the sound words of our Lord Jesus Christ" (*hygiainō* – life-giving and wholesome) directly against the false teacher who has an "unhealthy craving" (*nosōn* – to be feverishly sick or spiritually obsessed) for controversies and "quarrels about words" (*logomachiai* – pointless hair-splitting). While sound teaching cultivates godliness and peace, the false teacher's obsession produces a poisonous harvest of envy, suspicion and "constant friction" (*diaparatribai* – rubbing one another raw, over and over).
- **"Gain" (*porismos* – πορισμός, vv. 5–6):** This business term means a profitable enterprise or a money-making scheme. False teachers see religion merely as *porismos* – a cash cow for personal enrichment and status. But Paul takes their favourite word and turns it on its head: *"But godliness with contentment is great gain" (porismos megas).* True, eternal gain does not lie in your bank balance but in your relationship with God.
- **"Contentment" (*autarkeia* – αὐτάρκεια, v. 6):** This was one of the highest virtues of Stoic philosophy, where it meant the pride of being completely "self-sufficient" and independent of all outward things and emotions. Paul takes over the word and baptises it in the grace of Christ. For the believer, *autarkeia* is not a cold self-sufficiency but **Christ-sufficiency** (see Philippians 4:11–13) – the deep inner peace of a soul that knows the Father provides, whether it has much or little in earthly possessions.
- **"Clothing" (*skepasmata* – σκεπάσματα, v. 8):** Where our translation speaks of "food and clothing", the Greek word *skepasmata* literally means "coverings". It includes both clothes to cover the body and a roof over your head. With these basic necessities, the pilgrim on the way to eternity can be content.
- **"Those who want to be rich" and "trap" (*hoi boulomenoi ploutein* / *pagis* – οἱ βουλόμενοι πλουτεῖν / παγίς, v. 9):** Notice carefully that Paul does not say it is a sin to own money – in verse 17 he even addresses believers who are rich. The danger lies with *hoi boulomenoi ploutein* – those who have made a settled decision of the will, who are obsessed and determined to get rich. They step into a *pagis* (a hidden bird-snare or trap), and their senseless desires "plunge" them (*bythizō* – to sink like an overloaded ship to the bottom of the sea) into ruin and destruction.
- **"Love of money" and "pierced" (*philargyria* / *periepeiran* – φιλαργυρία / περιέπειραν, v. 10):** "For the love of money (*philargyria* – literally love of silver) is a root of all kinds of evil." When money becomes your idol, there is no sin – from fraud and adultery to betrayal and murder – that it cannot lead to. Those who crave it (*oregomai*) wander away from the faith and have "pierced themselves with many pains" (*peripeirō*). This shocking Greek word means to skewer meat from every side on a spit. People think wealth will buy them a soft cushion of happiness, but in reality they are impaling their own souls on a sharp iron spit of anxiety, broken relationships and regret.

### Core Theological Truths

1. **Our daily work is a direct witness to the gospel.** How a Christian works under authority – especially when the employer is also a believer – determines whether the world will honour God's teaching or blaspheme it.
2. **Any gospel that sells godliness as a means to material wealth is a false gospel.** The truth of Christ leads to holiness and humility, never to greed and self-enrichment.
3. **We stand between two nakednesses.** Like Job (Job 1:21), Paul reminds us that we came into the world empty-handed and will leave it through the grave empty-handed. Only what is invested in God goes with us through death.
4. **Contentment is the most powerful weapon against the lure of Mammon.** Whoever has found rest in God's provision cannot be bribed or caught by the world's glittering traps.

## Living It Out

This passage cuts like a two-edged sword through two of the greatest temptations of modern life: our attitude at **work** and our relationship with **money**.

First, look at verses 1 and 2. Although we do not live in the Roman slave system today, most of us spend the greater part of the week in a workplace under the authority of a manager, a board or clients. What is your work ethic like? If your colleagues know you are a Christian, but you arrive late, constantly complain about the boss or hand in half-finished work, the name of God is blasphemed because of you. And if you work for a fellow believer, Paul warns against the subtle trap of making less effort "because we're brothers in the Lord, after all". On the contrary: precisely because your employer is beloved by Christ, you should give your very best service!

Second, look at the masterful diagnosis of our consumer culture in verses 3 to 10. Every day we are bombarded by thousands of advertisements designed to kill one particular virtue in your heart: **contentment**. The world whispers ceaselessly: *"You don't have enough yet. If only you had that bigger house, that newer car or that higher salary – then you would be happy."* Even in parts of today's church a so-called "prosperity gospel" is preached that does exactly what Paul warns against in verse 5: it teaches that godliness is simply a lever for getting rich.

Paul calls us to wake up before the trap of *philargyria* (love of money) snaps shut around our feet. A hearse never tows a trailer. When you breathe your last, your portfolio, your property and your earthly status stay behind. How tragic it is when people neglect their families, sell their conscience and deny their faith in the mad chase after wealth – only to discover at the end of their lives that they have pierced their own hearts with untold pain! True wealth is looking this morning at the food on your table and the clothes on your back, and being able to say with a deep, grateful smile: *"Lord, because I have you, I have enough."*

### Questions for Personal Reflection

- Do my punctuality, honesty and dedication at work reflect such a high standard that they make the gospel of Jesus Christ attractive and credible to my employer and colleagues?
- Am I living today in true *autarkeia* (contentment in God's provision), or are my mood and peace determined by what I still want to buy and how much money is in my account?
- Is there any area where the desire to get ahead financially is tempting me right now to sacrifice my integrity, my family time or my devotion to the Lord?
