Journey through Jonah · Day 1 / 10

The Call to Nineveh and the Flight to Tarshish

Scripture Jonah 1:1–3

1The word of the LORD came to Jonah son of Amittai: 2"Get up, go to Nineveh, the great city, and cry out against it, for their evil has come up before my face." 3So Jonah got up – to flee to Tarshish, away from the face of the LORD. He went down to Joppa and found a ship bound for Tarshish. He paid the fare and went down into it, to go with them to Tarshish, away from the face of the LORD.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

The book of Jonah stands apart among the Twelve Minor Prophets. The other prophetic books mostly record the sermons and visions of their prophets. Jonah, by contrast, is a masterfully told prophetic story – about the prophet himself. Here God holds up a mirror not only to a sinful world, but to his own servants.

Jonah son of Amittai was no fictional character. We meet him in 2 Kings 14:25, ministering in the reign of King Jeroboam II of Israel (about 786–746 BC). He came from Gath Hepher in Galilee and preached a popular message: territorial expansion and national prosperity for the northern kingdom. Jonah was a patriot – a man who loved his people and was used to announcing God's favour over Israel.

Then God's voice breaks into his comfortable ministry with a shocking command: "Go to Nineveh, the great city." Nineveh was one of the capitals, and the very symbol, of the Assyrian Empire – the most brutal and feared superpower of the ancient Near East. The Assyrians were notorious for their cruelty: they flayed captives alive, burned cities to the ground, and proudly carved their war atrocities into palace walls. For an Israelite in the eighth century BC, a command to go to Nineveh was not merely dangerous. It felt like betraying your own people.

Instead of travelling some 800 kilometres north-east to Nineveh, in today's Iraq, Jonah turns on his heel and runs for Tarshish – probably Tartessos on the southern coast of Spain, some 4,000 kilometres to the west, at the far edge of the known world.

Insights from the Hebrew

These first three verses are packed with literary irony and theological depth:

  • Jonah son of Amittai (Yonah ben-Amittai – יוֹנָה בֶּן־אֲמִתַּי): Yonah means "dove", and Amittai comes from emet (אֱמֶת), "truth" or "faithfulness". In Israel the dove was a picture of peace – and sometimes of a silly, fluttering bird (see Hosea 7:11). The book's deepest irony is already in the name: the "Dove, son of Truth" refuses to carry God's peace and truth to the nations.
  • "Get up, go" (qum, lekh – קוּם לֵךְ): This is the classic, urgent command to a prophet. Verse 3 picks up the same verb with biting irony: "So Jonah got up" (wayyaqom) – "to flee to Tarshish." Jonah does get up. He uses exactly the energy and opportunity God gives him – but runs in the opposite direction.
  • "He went down" (yarad – יָרַד): The verb yarad, "to go down", is the backbone of chapters 1 and 2. Geographically, you really did go down from Israel's hill country to the port of Joppa. But the narrator deliberately turns it into a spiritual motif: Jonah goes down to Joppa (v. 3), down into the ship (v. 3), down into the innermost part of the ship to sleep (v. 5), and finally down to the roots of the mountains on the sea floor (2:7; English Bibles 2:6). Every step away from God's call is a step downward. Even Nineveh's evil has "come up" (v. 2) – it rises to God, while his prophet goes down away from him.
  • "Away from the face of the LORD" (millifnei YHWH – מִלִּפְנֵי יְהוָה): Verse 3 says it twice. As a trained prophet, Jonah knew psalms like Psalm 139: "Where could I go from your Spirit? Where could I flee from your face?" (Ps 139:7). He knew full well that God is everywhere. But "to stand before the face of the LORD" was the set phrase for a prophet's active service (see 1 Kings 17:1). In effect, Jonah is handing in his resignation. He thinks that if he leaves Israel, he can walk away from his calling.

Core Theological Truths

  1. God's sovereign compassion overruns our boundaries. Nineveh's evil has "come up before" God's face (v. 2). God does not ignore sin – he sees Nineveh's injustice clearly. But by sending a prophet to warn them, he already shows that he wants to be gracious (as Jonah himself admits in 4:2). God's heart beats for people we have long since written off.
  2. The anatomy of spiritual rebellion. Jonah doesn't run because he doubts that God exists. He runs precisely because he knows exactly who God is – and disagrees with God's gracious plan. Our hardest crises of obedience often come not when we fail to understand God, but when we understand his will crystal-clear and still prefer our own.
  3. The deception of "favourable circumstances". When Jonah reaches Joppa, a ship for Tarshish just happens to be waiting, and he just happens to have enough money for the fare. We often assume that an "open door" automatically proves God's blessing. Sometimes the ship waiting in Joppa is not God's provision but a test of our heart.
  4. The high cost of running. The text makes a point of it: "He paid the fare." When you walk in God's commission, he provides for the journey. When you run from God, you always pay the fare yourself – and, as we will see, you never even reach the destination you paid for.

Living It Out

Each of us has a Nineveh and a Tarshish in our life of faith.

Your Nineveh is the place, the person or the task God is calling you to that makes you deeply uncomfortable. It may be the person who hurt you, whom you need to pray for and forgive. It may be a difficult conversation you keep putting off, a sin you need to confess, or a call to serve people you don't like, far outside your comfort zone. We would like God to bless only our "Gath Hepher" – our own circle, our own plans, our own comfort. But when his Word challenges our prejudices, we dig in our heels against his voice.

Your Tarshish is your escape route. Nobody today needs to board a wooden ship to flee from God's presence. We run to Tarshish through constant busyness, endless distraction on our screens, overwork, emotional withdrawal – or even by keeping ourselves occupied with other "good" church activities, just so we don't have to do the one specific thing the Lord is asking of us.

Beware, too, of rationalising disobedience. When you walk away from God's clear voice and everything still goes smoothly, don't mistake calm for peace – it may be spiritual numbness. The ship to Tarshish always sails easily out of the harbour. The storm is waiting on the open sea.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What is my "Nineveh" right now? What specific task, conversation, act of forgiveness or step of faith has the Lord laid on my heart that I am deliberately avoiding because it threatens my pride or my comfort?
  • What "Tarshish" am I using to hide? What keeps my mind and my calendar so full that I never have to become still before the Lord?
  • Where am I mistaking convenient circumstances for God's will? Have I recently justified a compromise simply because the opportunity came so easily?
  • Are there people or groups I secretly begrudge God's grace? What is my attitude towards those I see as "enemies" or outsiders?

Prayer

Lord, almighty and merciful God, I bow before you honestly today. You know my heart better than I know it myself. Like Jonah, I am so often eager to receive your blessing and hear your voice when it fits my own plans and comfort – and so quick to shrink back when you send me to my own "Nineveh".

Forgive me for the times I hear what you are asking, then turn and go my own way. Forgive me for the selfish boundaries I try to draw around your grace, and for the times I think I can lock my heart against your presence. Shake me awake today wherever I am "going down" – away from your call and away from closeness with you.

Give me the courage to tear up my ticket to Tarshish. Break my stubbornness, and replace my prejudice and fear with your own compassionate Father's heart. Make me willing to get up and go wherever you send me, knowing that your presence is my greatest safety. In the name of Jesus Christ, who did not run away, but set his face resolutely towards the cross to save me. Amen.
