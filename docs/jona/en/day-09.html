Journey through Jonah · Day 9 / 10

The Plant, the Worm and the Scorching East Wind

Scripture Jonah 4:5–8

5Jonah went out of the city and sat down east of the city. There he made himself a shelter and sat under it in the shade, until he could see what would happen in the city. 6Then the LORD God appointed a leafy plant and made it grow up over Jonah, to be shade over his head, to deliver him from his evil. And Jonah rejoiced over the plant with great joy. 7But God appointed a worm when dawn came up the next day, and it struck the plant, so that it withered. 8When the sun rose, God appointed a scorching east wind, and the sun struck Jonah's head, so that he grew faint. He asked that he might die, and said, "My death is better than my life."

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

At the end of yesterday's passage the Lord asked Jonah a direct, piercing question: "Is it right for your anger to burn?" (4:4). But Jonah gives no answer. Like a sulking child who refuses to talk it through, he turns around, walks out through Nineveh's city gates and climbs the hills east of the city. There he builds himself a simple shelter of branches and sits down like a spectator in a grandstand to see "what would happen in the city". In spite of the city's repentance and God's grace, Jonah still secretly hopes that the forty days will run out and that fire from heaven will fall on his enemies after all!

On the dry, treeless plains of Mesopotamia the summer sun could burn mercilessly, with temperatures easily rising above 40 degrees Celsius. Jonah's homemade shelter soon dries out in the heat – and this is where God switches from a question to a living object lesson. Just as the prophet Nathan had to tell King David a story about a poor man's lamb so that David could see his own sin (2 Samuel 12), God creates a physical parable around Jonah to break open his hardened heart.

Notice the meaningful change in God's names in these verses. In verse 6 the narrator suddenly uses the compound name the LORD God (YHWH Elohim) – a rare combination in the Prophets, best known from the creation and fall narratives of Genesis 2 and 3. He is both YHWH (the loving covenant Lord who cares for his child) and Elohim (the almighty Creator who rules over nature).

The means God uses in this object lesson were well known in the ancient Near East:

  • The plant (qiqayon): Most scholars identify it as the castor-oil plant (Ricinus communis) or a fast-growing gourd. In the fertile soil along the Tigris this plant could shoot up extraordinarily fast, with large, broad leaves giving wonderful, cool shade.
  • The scorching east wind: This refers to the dreaded sirocco (or sharav), a searing, dusty desert wind from the eastern plateaus that sucks the moisture out of the air in an instant, makes the heat unbearable, and leaves people and plants parched and fainting within hours.

Insights from the Hebrew

The Hebrew text of verses 5 to 8 is a literary masterpiece, full of irony and repetition, that lays bare Jonah's spiritual condition:

  • "A shelter" (sukkah – סֻכָּה): The word for the shelter Jonah weaves is sukkah – exactly the word for the booths of Israel's Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot). That feast was a yearly reminder of how graciously God had provided for and protected Israel in the wilderness (Leviticus 23:42–43). The irony is striking: Jonah sits under a sukkah that reminds him of God's undeserved grace to Israel, while he waits for God to destroy Nineveh without grace!
  • "To deliver him from his evil" (lehatsil lo mera'ato – לְהַצִּיל לוֹ מֵרָעָתוֹ): Here the wordplay on ra'ah ("evil", "wickedness", "misery") reaches its climax. The phrase has a deliberate double meaning: on the surface the plant gives shade to relieve Jonah of his physical discomfort, but at a deeper level God does it "to deliver him from his own evil – his bitterness". Nineveh has already been saved from its ra'ah; now the prophet still needs to be delivered from his own ra'ah, his angry, selfish heart! On top of that, lehatsil ("to deliver") echoes tsel ("shade") – the shade itself is a deliverance.
  • "Jonah rejoiced … with great joy" (wayyismach Yonah … simchah gedolah – וַיִּשְׂמַח יוֹנָה … שִׂמְחָה גְדוֹלָה): Literally: "And Jonah rejoiced over the plant with great joy." This is a shocking remark: it is the first and only time in the whole book that Jonah is happy! When the sailors turned to God, he wasn't glad. When Nineveh was saved, it was to him ra'ah gedolah, "a great evil" (4:1). But when a plant shields his own head from the sun, he suddenly feels simchah gedolah – "great joy"!
  • "Appointed" (wayman – וַיְמַן): This key verb appears three times in a row, in verses 6, 7 and 8. In 2:1 (Hebrew numbering; English Bibles 1:17) the LORD appointed a great fish. Now he appoints a plant (v. 6), he appoints a single worm (tola'at, v. 7), and he appoints a scorching east wind (ruach qadim charishit, v. 8). Whether it's a huge sea creature in the deep or a tiny worm at dawn, the whole of creation obeys God's command perfectly – only the heart of his prophet still resists.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The idol of personal comfort. Jonah's emotional see-saw between verse 6 and verse 8 is disturbing. In verse 6 he is ecstatic over a bit of shade; in verse 8 he wants to die because it's hot. When personal ease and comfort become the highest priority in our lives, we become spiritually blind to God's kingdom and emotionally at the mercy of the smallest change in our circumstances.
  2. Our own "shelters" can't protect us. Jonah first tried to build his own sukkah (v. 5), but it wasn't enough against the heat; God had to make the plant grow up to give real shade (v. 6). We constantly try to build our own human shelters and securities to shield ourselves from life, but true refreshment is always a gift of God's grace.
  3. The grace of the worm. We easily understand that the shade plant was a gift from God, but verse 7 teaches us that the worm was just as surely appointed by God. Why does God's worm destroy Jonah's beloved plant? Because Jonah had begun to love the gift more than the Giver, and to value his comfort above human lives. Sometimes the Lord sends a "worm" to make an earthly prop or comfort zone in our lives wither – precisely to shake us awake from our self-centredness.
  4. Jonah on the hill and Christ on the hill. Look at Jonah sitting on a hill east of Nineveh, seeking his own comfort and hoping for the city's destruction. Then look at Jesus Christ, "greater than Jonah", who centuries later looks out over the city from the Mount of Olives – also east of Jerusalem – and weeps because it rejects his peace (Luke 19:41). Where Jonah complained because the sun struck his head, Jesus hung on a cross outside the city gates under the scorching sun and endured the full heat of God's judgement, so that enemies might be saved.

Living It Out

Jonah on the eastern hill is the classic picture of the critical religious spectator. How easily we slip into the same posture! Instead of staying inside "Nineveh", teaching the new believers and celebrating God's grace with them, we withdraw to a safe distance. We sit in our comfortable "shelters", look critically at the brokenness of the world or the failings of other believers, and wait with folded arms to see things go wrong.

This passage also exposes our daily priorities. What gives you "great joy" (simchah gedolah), and what drives you to despair? If we're honest, our emotional life often looks just like Jonah's: we get very excited when our material affairs run smoothly, when our holiday plans work out or our finances flourish – yet we are hardly moved by the spiritual salvation or eternal destiny of the people around us. And as soon as an unexpected "worm" shows up – a financial setback, a plan that falls through, a comfort that's taken away – and the "east wind" of trial blows against us, our whole mood collapses.

If you are experiencing today that a "plant" in which you found joy and rest has suddenly withered, don't immediately conclude that God has abandoned you. Ask instead: What does the Lord want to reveal in my heart through this loss? He never takes away our passing shade to destroy us, but to deliver us from the evil of self-centredness, so that we may know him as our one true refuge.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Have I become a spectator on the hill? Is there a situation, a church or a group of people from which I have critically withdrawn to sit and judge from a distance, instead of staying involved with compassion?
  • What do my joy and my sorrow reveal about my heart? When I look at what made me happiest and angriest this past week, was it mainly about my own temporary comfort ("my plant") or about the things that matter for God's kingdom?
  • What "plant" has the Lord recently allowed to wither in my life? How did I react when a security or comfort zone was taken from me – with bitterness and discouragement, or with an open heart to hear what God wants to teach me?
  • Where do I look for shade when the "scorching east wind" blows? Do I trust the fragile "shelters" I build with my own hands, or do I take refuge under the wings of the Most High?

Prayer

LORD God, sovereign Creator and loving Father, I bow before you today, aware of how small and self-centred my heart so often is. You see how easily I sit on the sidelines, caught up in my own little world, while you are at work in the eternal salvation of the people around me.

Forgive me, Lord, that I so often find "great joy" in the passing comforts and earthly blessings that make my life easy, while my heart stays cold to the spiritual need of others. Forgive me for the times I become discouraged and rebellious as soon as a worm makes my earthly joys wither and the scorching east wind of trial blows over my life.

Thank you that you love me too much to leave me in my selfish displeasure. Thank you for the plants of grace with which you refresh me – but thank you also for the worms you appoint to take away my false securities, so that I lean on you alone. Deliver me today from my own bitterness and small-mindedness. Teach me to find my deepest joy not in shifting shade, but in Jesus Christ, my Rock and my eternal refuge. Amen.
