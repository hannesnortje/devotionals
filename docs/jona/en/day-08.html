Journey through Jonah · Day 8 / 10

The Displeased Prophet: When God's Grace Makes Us Angry

Scripture Jonah 4:1–4

1But to Jonah this was evil – a great evil – and his anger burned. 2He prayed to the LORD and said, "Please, LORD, is this not what I said while I was still in my own country? That is why I fled to Tarshish in the first place. For I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in faithful love, and one who relents over evil. 3So now, LORD, please take my life from me, for my death is better than my life." 4But the LORD said, "Is it right for your anger to burn?"

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

If the book of Jonah ended with chapter 3, Jonah would go down in history as the most successful preacher of the Old Testament. No other prophet – not Isaiah, Jeremiah or Ezekiel – ever saw an entire world city, from king to beggar, bow before the Lord in sackcloth and ashes within a single day. Any preacher would have shouted for joy. But chapter 4 opens with one of the most shocking twists in the whole Bible: Jonah is furious because his sermon worked!

Here, in verse 2, the riddle of chapter 1 is finally solved. Why did Jonah run to Tarshish in the first place? We often assume he was afraid the cruel Assyrians would kill him. But Jonah himself confesses the real reason: he wasn't afraid his mission to Nineveh would fail – he was terrified it would succeed! He knew that if he delivered God's warning, Nineveh might repent. And he knew God well enough to know that God would then forgive them.

Historically, Jonah's anger was rooted in a fiery but twisted patriotism. Jonah's contemporaries, the prophets Hosea and Amos, had already warned that God would use a foreign nation to discipline the northern kingdom of Israel for its refusal to repent (see Hosea 11:5; Amos 5:27). Jonah realised that a spared Nineveh remained a living threat to Israel's future – and indeed, only a few decades later (in 722 BC) Assyria would invade Israel and carry its people away. On top of that, Jonah felt his reputation as a prophet had been destroyed: he had announced that the city would be overturned in forty days, and now its walls were still standing!

Literarily, Jonah's prayer in chapter 4 forms a tragic contrast with his prayer in chapter 2:

  • In chapter 2 he prays from the belly of the fish in gratitude, because God saved him from drowning.
  • In chapter 4 he prays outside Nineveh in anger, because God saved others from destruction.
  • In both cases he prefers death to obedience to God's plan (compare 1:12 with 4:3). Jonah is the Old Testament forerunner of the older brother in Jesus' parable of the lost son (Luke 15:25–32), who stands outside and refuses to go in to the feast because the father has been gracious to an unworthy sinner.

Insights from the Hebrew

The Hebrew text exposes the depth of Jonah's spiritual crisis through brilliant wordplay:

  • "A great evil" (ra'ah gedolah – רָעָה גְדוֹלָה): The narrator plays masterfully with the Hebrew word ra'ah ("evil", "wickedness", "disaster"). In 1:2 Nineveh's ra'ah (evil) came up before God. In 3:8 Nineveh turns from its evil (ra'ah) way, and in 3:10 God relents of the evil (ra'ah) he had said he would do to them. And now, in 4:1, we read: "But to Jonah this was evil – a great evil." God's grace, which wipes out Nineveh's evil, is in Jonah's eyes itself "a great evil"!
  • "His anger burned" (wayyichar lo – וַיִּחַר לוֹ): The verb charah literally means "to burn" or "to blaze". Notice the irony: in 3:9 the king of Nineveh hoped that God would turn "from his burning anger" (charon appo). The moment God's anger stops burning, Jonah's starts!
  • The creed of Exodus 34:6 (El-channun we-rachum, erekh appayim we-rav-chesed – אֵל־חַנּוּן וְרַחוּם אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וְרַב־חֶסֶד): In verse 2 Jonah quotes the most famous and sacred self-revelation of God in the whole Old Testament – the words God gave Moses on Mount Sinai after the sin of the golden calf (Exodus 34:6–7; see also Psalm 103:8 and Joel 2:13):
  • Channun (gracious – he gives undeserved favour).
  • Rachum (compassionate – from rechem, "womb", pointing to a deep, motherly tenderness and mercy).
  • Erekh appayim (slow to anger – literally "long of nostrils": someone who takes a deep breath and is very slow to become angry).
  • Rav-chesed (abounding in faithful love – overflowing in covenant love).

What makes this verse so shocking is that Jonah uses the most beautiful confession of praise in Israel's worship as a reproach and accusation against God! - "Is it right for your anger to burn?" (ha-heitev charah lakh – הַהֵיטֵב חָרָה לָךְ): The LORD does not answer with thunderbolts but with three quiet Hebrew words that literally ask: "Is it good/right that it burns in you?" Just as God asked Cain, "Why has your anger burned?" (Genesis 4:6 – the same verb charah), he uses a diagnostic question to awaken Jonah's own conscience.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The hypocrisy of selective grace. At the end of chapter 2 Jonah cried out wholeheartedly, "Salvation belongs to the LORD!" (2:10; Hebrew numbering – English Bibles 2:9). But in chapter 4 we discover that Jonah thinks the Lord's salvation belongs only to him and his people. We fall into the same trap when we think we have somehow "earned" God's grace, while other people's sins are too great to be forgiven. But if grace is not also available to Nineveh, it is no longer grace.
  2. Right theology without a changed heart is dangerous. Jonah could recite Exodus 34:6 flawlessly from memory, but his heart beat with the opposite attitude. It is possible to know Scripture inside out and to know exactly how God works – and still live in bitterness towards his kingdom, because you put your own agenda above his.
  3. The idol of our own honour and security. Jonah's death wish in verse 3 ("my death is better than my life") reveals what was really driving him. When he saw that God's plan did not fit his political expectations and his standing as a prophet, his world collapsed. Anything – even good things like love for your people or your ministry – that becomes more important than God's will turns into a destructive idol.
  4. God shows patience to his angry prophet. How does God respond when Jonah rails at him over his grace? At that very moment God proves that he truly is erekh appayim – slow to anger! Instead of rejecting Jonah for his disrespectful outburst, the Lord engages him in conversation like a patient father and counsellor.

Living It Out

It's easy to look at Jonah with disapproval – until we realise how often a little Jonah hides inside us. Have you ever felt something "burn" in you with resentment when God blesses or acquits someone who wronged you? When someone who hurt you, or who by your standards "wasn't punished hard enough", turns to God and prospers, our natural sense of justice experiences it as "a great evil" (ra'ah gedolah). For ourselves we would love the God of Exodus 34:6, who covers our faults with the cloak of love – but for our enemies we demand a judge who strikes at once with fire and brimstone.

This passage also challenges how we react when God's ways don't match our expectations. Sometimes we get angry with the Lord – not because he has done anything wrong, but because he refused to act according to our script. When our prayers aren't answered the way we prescribed, or when we don't get our way, we easily slip into spiritual melodrama and self-pity like Jonah in verse 3: we want to throw in the towel, lay down our ministry and feel sorry for ourselves.

But listen today to the gentle voice of the Lord, who comes to you in the middle of your frustration, disappointment or resentment with that one searching question: "Is it right for your anger to burn?" (Is it right that it burns in you like this?) God is not afraid of your raw, honest prayers – Jonah at least brought his anger to the Lord in prayer instead of boarding another ship. But when you pour out your complaint before him, be ready for his loving question to turn the mirror back onto your own heart.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Towards whom am I harbouring an "older brother" attitude right now? Is there someone in my life to whom I refuse, in my heart, to grant forgiveness and restoration, because I feel they must first "pay" for what they did?
  • Where does God's character clash with my preferences? Do I know God's grace and sovereignty only in my head – or do I also trust him when he chooses to work in ways that threaten my pride or my comfort?
  • How do I react when my plans are thrown into disarray? Do I tend, like Jonah, to sink into deep discouragement and self-pity when things don't work out the way I thought they should?
  • How will I answer God's question in verse 4 today? If the Lord asks me today about the specific thing I am bitter, frustrated or angry about – "Is it right for your anger to burn?" – what does that reveal about what matters most to me in life?

Prayer

Lord, gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in faithful love, I bow before you today with an honest heart. When I look at Jonah's anger, I so often see my own reflection. I confess how quickly I claim your overflowing grace for myself when I am in the depths – and how slow I am to grant that same grace to others who have hurt or disappointed me.

Forgive me for the times something burns in me with resentment because your ways are higher than my ways. Forgive me when I put my own reputation, my own sense of justice and my own expectations above your kingdom. Thank you that you do not answer my spiritual small-mindedness with anger, but in your patience you speak with me and search my heart with your gentle questions.

Heal every grudge and every trace of bitterness in me today. Tear down the selfish walls I try to build around your love. Shape my heart so that I know your character not only with my mind, but love what you love and rejoice over everyone who returns from darkness to your light. In the name of Jesus, who prayed even for his enemies from the cross. Amen.
