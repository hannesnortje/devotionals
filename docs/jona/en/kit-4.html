Small-group kit · Session 4 / 4

The Unanswered Question

Present ▶

The big ideaJonah is angry that God is gracious. Through a plant, a worm and a scorching wind God exposes his small heart – and asks a question the book leaves open for us to answer: should I not have pity?

Timing

  • 10 min Welcome & opener
  • 10 min Read the passage
  • 10 min Background & Hebrew
  • 40 min Discussion
  • 15 min Application & prayer

To get started

What small thing can ruin your mood in an instant – a hot day, a slow internet connection, a missed bus? What does that say about us?

Read together · Jonah 4:1–11

Read it as a dialogue with three voices: narrator, Jonah and the LORD. After the last question in verse 11, leave a moment of silence – the book gives no answer.

Where we are in the story

Nineveh has been spared. Instead of rejoicing, Jonah is furious – and now we finally learn why he ran to Tarshish. He sits east of the city to see what will happen, and God teaches him with a plant, a worm and a scorching east wind. The book ends with God's question, and no answer from Jonah.

A look at the Hebrew

  • ra'ah gedolah – רָעָה גְדוֹלָה “a great evil”: Nineveh's evil came up before God (1:2); they turned from their evil way (3:8); God relented of the evil (3:10). Now: "But to Jonah this was evil – a great evil." God's grace itself is evil in Jonah's eyes.
  • charah – חָרָה “anger burned”: The moment God's burning anger stops (3:9), Jonah's starts. God answers with a quiet question, as he did with Cain (Genesis 4:6): "Is it right for your anger to burn?"
  • chus – חוּס “have pity”: Jonah has compassion – but for a plant that was "a son of a night". God argues from the lesser to the greater: if you pity the plant, should I not have pity on Nineveh?

Discussion

  1. Read 4:1–2. Why did Jonah really run to Tarshish? How does this change the way you read chapter 1? (Jonah 4:2)
  2. Jonah quotes Israel's great confession – "a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in faithful love" (Exodus 34:6) – as an accusation. How can we know true things about God and still resent them? (Jonah 4:2)
  3. God answers Jonah's outburst with a question: "Is it right for your anger to burn?" Why a question and not a rebuke? What is God doing? (Jonah 4:4)
  4. The plant is the only thing in the whole book that makes Jonah happy (4:6); the heat makes him want to die (4:8). What do our joys and our anger reveal about what we value most? (Jonah 4:6)
  5. God "appointed" the fish, the plant, the worm and the wind. What does it mean that even the worm was appointed? Have you known a "worm" like that? (Jonah 4:7)
  6. Read 4:10–11. Compare what Jonah pities with what God pities. What argument is God making – and why does he mention the animals at the very end? (Jonah 4:11)
  7. The book ends with a question. How would you answer it – for yourself, and for the "Nineveh" in your own life that you find hard to love? (Jonah 4:11)

For the week ahead

  • Think of one person you hold an "older brother" resentment against (Luke 15:25–32). Pray a blessing on them every day this week.
  • Write down your own one-sentence answer to God's question in 4:11, and share it with one person in the group.

Pray together

Begin with a minute of silence before God's question in 4:11. Then pray for the people and places we find hard to love, and thank God that he is "slow to anger and abounding in faithful love" – towards them, and towards us.

Notes for the leader

  • This session covers three devotionals (Days 8–10) and a long chapter. If time is short, combine questions 4 and 5, or give the session 15 extra minutes.
  • Jonah twice wishes to die (4:3, 4:8). Handle this with care: someone in the group may know despair from the inside. Keep the focus on God's patient response, don't make light of it, and offer to talk afterwards.
  • The "older brother" in Luke 15 is a helpful bridge, but keep the focus on our own hearts, not on judging Jonah – or others in the room.
  • Day 10 suggests that Jonah himself may have written the book as his confession. Present this as a possibility, not a fact.
  • This is the last session. Keep ten minutes at the end to look back over all four chapters: which image or question will people take with them?
  • After the final reading, don't fill the silence too quickly. The open question is the point.

Scripture Jonah 4:1–11

1But to Jonah this was evil – a great evil – and his anger burned. 2He prayed to the LORD and said, "Please, LORD, is this not what I said while I was still in my own country? That is why I fled to Tarshish in the first place. For I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in faithful love, and one who relents over evil. 3So now, LORD, please take my life from me, for my death is better than my life." 4But the LORD said, "Is it right for your anger to burn?" 5Jonah went out of the city and sat down east of the city. There he made himself a shelter and sat under it in the shade, until he could see what would happen in the city. 6Then the LORD God appointed a leafy plant and made it grow up over Jonah, to be shade over his head, to deliver him from his evil. And Jonah rejoiced over the plant with great joy. 7But God appointed a worm when dawn came up the next day, and it struck the plant, so that it withered. 8When the sun rose, God appointed a scorching east wind, and the sun struck Jonah's head, so that he grew faint. He asked that he might die, and said, "My death is better than my life." 9But God said to Jonah, "Is it right for your anger to burn over the plant?" He said, "It is right for my anger to burn – to the point of death!" 10Then the LORD said, "You had pity on the plant, for which you did not labour and which you did not make grow; it came into being in a night and perished in a night. 11And should I not have pity on Nineveh, the great city, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand people who do not know their right hand from their left – and many animals as well?"

Scripture: own translation from the Hebrew (Masoretic Text) by Dr. Johannes A. Nortjé. Verse numbers follow the Hebrew text.
