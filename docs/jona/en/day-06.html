Journey through Jonah · Day 6 / 10

The God of Second Chances and the Cry in Nineveh

Scripture Jonah 3:1–4

1The word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time: 2"Get up, go to Nineveh, the great city, and cry out to it the message that I am telling you." 3So Jonah got up and went to Nineveh, as the LORD had said. Now Nineveh was a great city – great even before God – a three days' journey across. 4Jonah began to go into the city, one day's journey, and he cried out and said, "Forty more days, and Nineveh will be overturned!"

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday we saw the fish vomit Jonah out onto the dry land. Today the book's second great act begins – and chapter 3 opens with a breathtaking literary mirror image of chapter 1. The narrator uses almost word for word the same opening formula as in Jonah 1:1–2. After the depths, God, as it were, presses the "reset button" on Jonah's life and ministry. Where in chapter 1 Jonah got up to flee, in chapter 3 he gets up to go.

The journey from the Mediterranean coast to Nineveh – on the east bank of the Tigris, opposite present-day Mosul in northern Iraq – was a gruelling trek of some 800 to 900 kilometres through mountain passes and dry plains. It would have cost Jonah almost a month of walking under the burning sun – a month in which every step tested his new obedience.

Historically, Jonah lived in the first half of the eighth century BC (about 780–750 BC). Assyrian chronicles – the so-called Eponym Canon – shed fascinating light on why Nineveh might have been so receptive to a message of judgement at precisely this time. Under the Assyrian king Ashur-dan III, the empire went through a series of traumatic crises: internal revolts, famine, two devastating plagues (in 765 and 759 BC), and a total solar eclipse on 15 June 763 BC. In the ancient Mesopotamian worldview, such disasters and cosmic signs were taken as proof that the gods were angry. So when this strange prophet from Israel walked through the city gates – perhaps still with skin and hair bleached by the fish's stomach acid – God had already prepared the ground in Nineveh.

What does verse 3 mean when it says Nineveh was "a three days' journey across"? In the idiom of the ancient Near East it can refer to one of two things (or both): first, to the greater metropolitan district of Nineveh and its surrounding satellite towns (see Genesis 10:11–12), which had a circumference of some 90 kilometres; or second, to the diplomatic protocol of the time, by which an official envoy took three days to visit a great city: day one for arrival and reception, day two for the official proclamation in the city centre, and day three for departure.

Insights from the Hebrew

Every word in these four verses carries special theological weight in the Hebrew text:

  • "A second time" (shenit – שֵׁנִית): This simple word holds an ocean of grace. God could have left Jonah on that beach as a disqualified servant and chosen another prophet. But the word of the LORD comes shenit – a second time. God's calling rests on his faithfulness, not on our failure.
  • "The message that I am telling you" (et-ha-qeri'ah asher anokhi dover eleikha – אֶת־הַקְּרִיאָה אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי דֹּבֵר אֵלֶיךָ): Notice the subtle change between God's command in 1:2 and 3:2. In 1:2 God said, "cry out against it" (qera aleha). Now he says, "cry out to it the message" (qera eleha) – literally, "cry out to her the cry that I am speaking to you". The verb dover is a participle, in the present tense. God does not hand Jonah the whole sermon in advance at Joppa; Jonah has to set out in dependence and hear, step by step, what the Lord gives him to say.
  • "A great city – great even before God" (ir-gedolah le-Elohim – עִיר־גְּדוֹלָה לֵאלֹהִים): Many translations render this "an exceedingly great city". But the Hebrew literally says "a great city to God" (or "before God"), and our translation deliberately keeps that. It can be read as a Hebrew superlative, but the deeper meaning lies on the surface: to Jonah, Nineveh was a despised enemy city; to God, it was a city of immeasurable worth.
  • "Forty more days, and Nineveh will be overturned!" (od arba'im yom we-Nineveh nehpakhet – עוֹד אַרְבָּעִים יוֹם וְנִינְוֵה נֶהְפָּכֶת): In Hebrew, Jonah's entire sermon is just five words! But the last word – nehpakhet, from the root hafakh (הָפַךְ) – is a masterpiece of divine double meaning. On the one hand, hafakh means "to overturn", that is, "to destroy" – the very word used for the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah in Genesis 19:25. On the other hand, hafakh also means "to transform completely", "to turn around for the good" – as when Saul is turned into another man (1 Samuel 10:6), or mourning is turned into dancing (Psalm 30:11; Hebrew 30:12). English has no single word that carries both senses; we translate "overturned", but keep the second meaning in mind as you read on. Jonah thinks he is announcing only destruction. But in God's mouth the same word already carries the promise: in forty days Nineveh will be turned upside down – either by judgement or by repentance!

Core Theological Truths

  1. The God of restoration and second chances. Failure in the past does not mean disqualification for the future. Just as the risen Christ would later restore Peter on a beach beside the Sea of Galilee after his denial (John 21), God restores his runaway prophet on the shore of the Mediterranean and gives him back his commission.
  2. God does not lower his standard to suit us. When the word comes to Jonah a second time, God does not give him an easier assignment. He doesn't send him to a safe village in Galilee; the assignment is still Nineveh. God's grace rescues us from the depths – but it always brings us back to the exact point where we last disobeyed.
  3. Faithfulness to the message. A true messenger of God does not invent his own message to win over his audience; he speaks only what God commands. In the Bible, the number forty (like the 40 days of the flood or the 40 years in the wilderness) often marks a time of testing and of grace held open. Even in a message of judgement, a forty-day period is evidence of God's patience, leaving room for repentance.
  4. The power is in the Word, not the preacher. Jonah preaches the shortest and least friendly sermon in the Bible – five Hebrew words, without a single invitation to grace, without music and without clever rhetoric. And yet these five words will spark the greatest city-wide repentance of the ancient world. It proves that spiritual awakening is the work of God's Spirit and Word, not of human eloquence.

Living It Out

How many of us walk around today with the paralysing feeling that we have forfeited our chance with the Lord? You look back on a season when you ran to your own "Tarshish". And even though you know the Lord has forgiven you and brought you up from the depths, you feel like a second-hand believer who should just sit on the sidelines. Jonah 3:1 breaks through that lie with liberating words: "The word of the LORD came to Jonah a second time." The scars of your past do not disqualify you. On the contrary, the man who comes out of the belly of the fish carries an authority and a testimony he never had before!

But notice that a second chance always calls for new obedience. When God spoke again, Jonah didn't sit down and argue again; he got up and took on the long, dusty road to Nineveh. Where is the Lord calling you back today – to the very thing you once ran away from?

Perhaps the task in front of you feels like a city of "three days' journey" – overwhelmingly big, intimidating, impossible to get through. Look at verse 4: Jonah began by going in one day's journey. You don't have to move the whole mountain today. Obedience begins by tackling the first day's journey today and faithfully speaking the words the Lord lays on your heart. And remember: you are not responsible for how people respond. Your task is simply to deliver honestly the five words God gives you, and to leave the "overturning" (hafakh) of human hearts to him.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Do I really believe that God is a God of second chances – for me? Do I let guilt over yesterday's escapes paralyse me today, or am I ready to accept his renewed calling on my life?
  • Have I gone back to my "point of disobedience"? What original assignment from the Lord is still waiting for my obedience, now that he has rescued me from a crisis?
  • Do I see the world around me as God sees it? Do I see the difficult, broken people and places around me merely as a threat – or do I see that they are "great even before God" (le-Elohim)?
  • What is my "one day's journey" for today? What first, concrete step of obedience can I take today in something that looks to me like an impossible "three-day challenge"?

Prayer

Lord, God of second chances, my heart bows before you today in deep gratitude. Thank you that your Word does not fall silent when I stumble and fail, but that in your incomprehensible grace you speak to your children "a second time". Thank you that you don't leave me on the beach of my past, but pick me up, wash me clean and make me useful in your kingdom again.

Lord, I confess that I often feel intimidated by the size of the challenges and the hardness of the world you send me into. When I look at my own "Nineveh", it seems too big and too dangerous. But today I choose to look no longer at my own weakness, but at the power of your Word.

Give me the grace to get up and walk the first day's journey of obedience. Teach me to listen to your voice in daily dependence and to speak only what you command – without fear and without compromise. I trust you that your Word still has the power today to turn completely around what has been destroyed, for your glory. Amen.
