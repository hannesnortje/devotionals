Behind the Curtain · Day 29 / 31

The Bride Is a City: Where God Himself Is the Temple

Scripture Revelation 21:9–27

9And one of the seven angels who had the seven bowls full of the seven last plagues came and spoke with me, saying, "Come, I will show you the bride, the wife of the Lamb." 10And he carried me away in the Spirit to a great, high mountain and showed me the holy city Jerusalem coming down out of heaven from God, 11having the glory of God. Its radiance was like a most precious stone, like a jasper, clear as crystal. 12It had a great, high wall with twelve gates, and at the gates twelve angels, and names written on them, which are the names of the twelve tribes of the people of Israel: 13on the east three gates, and on the north three gates, and on the south three gates, and on the west three gates. 14And the wall of the city had twelve foundations, and on them the twelve names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb.

15And the one who spoke with me had a gold measuring reed to measure the city and its gates and its wall. 16And the city lies foursquare; its length is as great as its width. And he measured the city with the reed: twelve thousand stadia. Its length and width and height are equal. 17And he measured its wall: a hundred and forty-four cubits, by human measure, which is also an angel's measure. 18And the wall was built of jasper, and the city was pure gold, like clear glass. 19The foundations of the wall of the city were adorned with every kind of precious stone: the first foundation was jasper, the second sapphire, the third chalcedony, the fourth emerald, 20the fifth sardonyx, the sixth carnelian, the seventh chrysolite, the eighth beryl, the ninth topaz, the tenth chrysoprase, the eleventh jacinth, the twelfth amethyst. 21And the twelve gates were twelve pearls; each of the gates was made of a single pearl. And the street of the city was pure gold, like transparent glass.

22And I saw no temple in it, for its temple is the Lord God, the Almighty, and the Lamb. 23And the city has no need of the sun or of the moon to shine on it, for the glory of God has given it light, and its lamp is the Lamb. 24And the nations will walk by its light, and the kings of the earth bring their glory into it. 25And its gates will never be shut by day, for there will be no night there. 26And they will bring the glory and the honour of the nations into it. 27But nothing unclean will ever enter it, nor anyone who practises abomination and lies, but only those who are written in the book of life of the Lamb.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

The one seated on the throne has said, "Look, I am making all things new." Now one of the angels shows John from close up what that means. And the angel sounds familiar: he is "one of the seven angels who had the seven bowls" – exactly the same formula as in 17:1, when John was to see the great prostitute. Then the angel said, "Come, I will show you the judgement of the great prostitute." Now he says, "Come, I will show you the bride, the wife of the Lamb." The book wants us to see the two women side by side.

And then happens again what we saw with the Lion and the Lamb. John hears "the bride", but what he sees is a city. The angel shows him "the holy city Jerusalem coming down out of heaven from God". The seeing interprets the hearing. The city is not first of all a place; it is the people of God, in the glory God gives them. On the gates are the names of the twelve tribes of Israel, on the foundations the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb – the whole people of God, old and new covenant together in one city.

Here John stands on Ezekiel's shoulders. The prophet, in exile in Babylon, was taken in a vision to a very high mountain, where he saw something like a city (Ezekiel 40:2). A man with a measuring reed measured the new temple (Ezekiel 40–48), there were twelve gates bearing the names of the tribes, and the last words of the book are the name of the city: The LORD is there (Ezekiel 48:30–35). Isaiah sang of the same city: gates that never close, nations coming to its light, and the LORD himself as its everlasting light, so that it no longer needs sun or moon (Isaiah 60:3, 11, 19).

Now think of the first readers. Pergamum, Smyrna and Ephesus competed for the honour of having temples to the emperor; the temple was the heart of every city. And the temple in Jerusalem had lain in ruins since AD 70. Into that world John writes the most astonishing sentence of the chapter: "And I saw no temple in it, for its temple is the Lord God, the Almighty, and the Lamb."

Insights from the Greek

  • Come, I will show you (Deuro, deixō soi – Δεῦρο, δείξω σοι, v. 9): Word for word the same invitation as in 17:1. Two women, two cities, two destinies – and the question of whom we belong to is given a face.
  • Foursquare (tetragōnos – τετράγωνος, v. 16): Length, width and height are equal; the city is a cube. In Solomon's temple only one room was built like that: the Most Holy Place, twenty cubits long, wide and high (1 Kings 6:20). The whole city has become the Most Holy Place – the room only the high priest could enter, once a year, is now the home of all who belong to the Lamb.
  • Twelve thousand stadia (stadious dōdeka chiliadōn – σταδίους δώδεκα χιλιάδων, v. 16): About 2,200 kilometres in every direction. The number is not meant for a map but for wonder: twelve (the people of God) times a thousand. The wall of "a hundred and forty-four cubits" is twelve times twelve. There is room for all whom God has called.
  • Temple (naon – ναόν, v. 22): From naos, the word for the sanctuary itself, God's dwelling. In the new Jerusalem there is no separate holy place any more, because God and the Lamb fill everything.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The whole Bible is on its way to this city. In the garden God walked with humankind (Genesis 3:8). After the exodus he lived in a tent among his people (Exodus 40:34), then in the temple (1 Kings 8:10–11). Ezekiel saw the glory depart before the exile (Ezekiel 10) and return (Ezekiel 43:2–5). In Jesus the Word pitched his tent among us (John 1:14), and the church became the temple of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16). In the new Jerusalem the story is completed: God no longer lives in one corner of the world; his presence fills everything.

  2. The prostitute sells her splendour; the bride receives hers. The prostitute was adorned "with gold and precious stone and pearls" (17:4); the bride's gates are pearls, her street is gold, her foundations are adorned with precious stones. Babylon wears the splendour as seduction and sells it; Jerusalem receives it as God's gift. And the kings of the earth, who "committed harlotry with her and lived in luxury" (18:9), here bring their glory into the city. What was good and beautiful in the cultures of the nations is not lost; it is cleansed and brought home.

  3. The gates stand open, and the Lamb holds the book. "And its gates will never be shut by day, for there will be no night there." The open gates are an invitation, not indifference: "nothing unclean will ever enter it". Whoever enters does not come in on their own merit, but because they are "written in the book of life of the Lamb".

Living It Out

We live in cities of a different kind. The shopping centre, the office tower, the stadium – every culture builds its temples to what it values most. And we tend to put God in a room too: Sunday morning, the quiet time, the church building. Revelation 21 shows where everything is heading: a city without a temple, because no part of life lies outside God's presence any more. That future may already come into our Mondays. The kitchen, the office and the classroom are no less holy than the church, for the Spirit lives in you.

This vision also says something about the church you belong to. John hears "bride" and sees a city with twelve tribes and twelve apostles in its foundations. The bride is not a collection of individuals each going to heaven alone; she is a people. The people you sing with on Sunday, with all their quirks, are part of the city God is building. It is sometimes easier to believe in the glory of the future than to be patient with a difficult brother or sister – but it is the same city.

And for anyone bowed down under a burden, this chapter shows a city without night. For believers in Smyrna and Philadelphia, who at times had to live in the darkness of persecution and exclusion, this was a promise to hold on to: a day is coming without night, without locked doors, without anything that hurts. You may walk in that light today, even if your night is not yet over.

This week, choose one "ordinary" place in your life – your desk, your car, your kitchen table – and pray there deliberately: Lord, you live here too.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What "temples" does the culture around you build, and which pulls at you more strongly – the splendour of Babylon or the glory of the bride (vv. 9–11; cf. 17:1–4)?
  • Are there areas of your life where you would rather not have God, because you keep him in a temple corner (v. 22)?
  • How do you see the church you belong to when you remember that the bride is a city with twelve foundations (vv. 12–14)?
  • What does it mean to you that your name is "written in the book of life of the Lamb" (v. 27)?

Prayer

Lord God, the Almighty, and you, Lamb of God: you are the temple and the light of the city that is coming. I worship you for the holy city Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven with your glory, and for the bride you are preparing for yourself from every tribe and nation.

The splendour of Babylon easily dazzles me, and I so often shut you away in one small corner of my life. Forgive me. Forgive me, too, that I sometimes see my brothers and sisters as a burden, when they are part with me of the city you are building.

Thank you that the gates stand open, and that my name is written in the book of life of the Lamb – not by my merit, but by your grace. Let me walk in your light today, so that others may see something of your glory through me. Amen.
