Behind the Curtain · Day 31 / 31

Come, Lord Jesus: The Curtain Stays Open

Scripture Revelation 22:6–21

6And he said to me, "These words are faithful and true. And the Lord, the God of the spirits of the prophets, has sent his angel to show his servants what must soon take place. 7And look, I am coming soon. Blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book."

8And I, John, am the one who heard and saw these things. And when I heard and saw them, I fell down to worship at the feet of the angel who showed them to me. 9And he says to me, "Do not do that! I am a fellow servant with you and with your brothers and sisters the prophets, and with those who keep the words of this book. Worship God!"

10And he says to me, "Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book, for the time is near. 11Let the one who does wrong still do wrong, and the filthy still be filthy, and the righteous still do what is right, and the holy still be holy. 12Look, I am coming soon, and my reward is with me, to repay each one according to what he has done. 13I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the beginning and the end. 14Blessed are those who wash their robes, so that they may have the right to the tree of life and may go through the gates into the city. 15Outside are the dogs and the sorcerers and the sexually immoral and the murderers and the idolaters, and everyone who loves and practises lies. 16I, Jesus, have sent my angel to testify to you about these things for the churches. I am the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star."

17And the Spirit and the bride say, "Come!" And let the one who hears say, "Come!" And let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who wishes take the water of life as a gift.

18I testify to everyone who hears the words of the prophecy of this book: if anyone adds to them, God will add to him the plagues that are written in this book. 19And if anyone takes away from the words of the book of this prophecy, God will take away his share in the tree of life and in the holy city, which are written about in this book.

20The one who testifies to these things says, "Yes, I am coming soon." Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!

21The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

The book – and our journey – comes to a close. We have stood by the river and the tree of life and heard: "they will see his face". And anyone who listens carefully hears the end echoing the beginning. "What must soon take place" (v. 6) are word for word the words of 1:1. "Blessed is the one who keeps the words of the prophecy of this book" (v. 7) repeats the blessing of 1:3. "The time is near" (v. 10) is also in 1:3. The Alpha and the Omega of 1:8 speaks again in verse 13, and the book ends like a letter, with grace (v. 21), just as it began in 1:4. From beginning to end Revelation is one letter to seven churches in Asia – and through them to the church of every age.

One word sounds through the whole ending: "Look, I am coming soon" (vv. 7, 12, 20). For the first readers, living under the pressure of Rome, it was a promise to hold on to. But it was also a question they would ask: if he is coming "soon", why are we still waiting? Peter had already answered the same question: with the Lord one day is like a thousand years, and he is not slow in keeping his promise but is patient, because he wants all to come to repentance (2 Peter 3:8–9). Long before, Habakkuk had heard about the vision: "If it lingers, wait for it, for it will surely come; it will not delay" (Habakkuk 2:3). "Soon" is not a date on a calendar but a promise that keeps every generation on its toes.

And then the striking command of verse 10: "Do not seal up the words of the prophecy of this book." Daniel was told the opposite: he was to shut up the words and seal the book until the time of the end (Daniel 12:4, 9). What was still far off for Daniel has begun with the cross and resurrection of Christ. The scroll that only the Lamb could open (chapter 5) may not be closed again. The curtain drawn back in chapter 4 stays open.

Insights from the Greek

  • I am coming soon (erchomai tachy – ἔρχομαι ταχύ, vv. 7, 12, 20): Erchomai is in the present tense: he is already on his way. Tachy means "soon", "without delay". It describes God's certainty and our readiness, not a timetable.
  • Do not seal up (Mē sphragisēs – Μὴ σφραγίσῃς, v. 10): The same word as for the seals of the scroll in chapters 5–6. The message is not stored away for a distant future; it is for the churches of that day, and for us, to read and to keep now.
  • Worship God! (tō theō proskynēson – τῷ θεῷ προσκύνησον, v. 9): For the second time John falls down before the angel (cf. 19:10), and for the second time he is set right. Even the most glorious messenger is only a fellow servant. The whole book wants to bring us to one place: the worship of God and the Lamb.
  • The bright morning star (ho astēr ho lampros, ho prōinos – ὁ ἀστὴρ ὁ λαμπρός, ὁ πρωϊνός, v. 16): Balaam saw a star rising out of Jacob (Numbers 24:17). The morning star appears when the night is darkest and announces the day. Thyatira was promised: "And I will give him the morning star" (2:28). Jesus himself is that promise.
  • Come, Lord Jesus (erchou, kyrie Iēsou – ἔρχου, κύριε Ἰησοῦ, v. 20): The early church probably prayed the same prayer in Aramaic, Marana tha – "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22). According to the Didache (10:6), an early Christian writing, it was also part of the prayers at the Lord's Supper.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The future is a Person, not a programme. The book does not end with a map of the end times but with Jesus saying: "I, Jesus, have sent my angel … I am the root and the descendant of David" (v. 16). He is at once the root from which David's house grew and the descendant of that house (Isaiah 11:1, 10) – the beginning and the end of God's promises. Whoever wants to know the future must know him.

  2. The invitation is wider than the warning. There are solemn words in this ending: "Outside are the dogs and the sorcerers …" (v. 15), and the warning not to add to the prophecy or take away from it (vv. 18–19; cf. Deuteronomy 4:2). But between these words stands the heart of the gospel: the Spirit and the bride say, "Come!" (v. 17). The thirsty may come and drink without paying. The last invitation in the Bible is a gift.

  3. Washed robes are the only way in. The seventh and last beatitude of the book reads: "Blessed are those who wash their robes, so that they may have the right to the tree of life" (v. 14). The way to the tree, guarded in Genesis 3:24, opens for those who have washed their robes in the blood of the Lamb (7:14). We do not get in by achievement, but by grace.

  4. The last word is grace. After all the visions of judgement and conflict, the very last verse says: "The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all." So the whole story begins and ends: with the grace of God.

Living It Out

Thirty-one days ago we began with the first word of the book, apokalypsis – the curtain is drawn back. We have looked behind the curtain and seen the throne that stands firm, the Lamb who was slaughtered and yet reigns, the accuser thrown down, Babylon falling, and a city where God himself is the temple. It was an exodus: the Passover Lamb who ransomed a people, the plagues and the song of Moses and of the Lamb, the call to come out of Babylon – and at the end the arrival in the promised land of the new creation. The curtain is open, and it stays open – until one day it is taken away altogether and we see his face. Tomorrow we go back to the streets of our own "Pergamum" – the work, the news, the bills, the hospital corridor – but now we know who truly sits on the throne.

That changes how we wait. Jesus' "soon" is not meant to make us speculate about dates or live in fear. It calls us to be awake and faithful, to keep his words (v. 7), to wash our robes, and to invite others to come and drink with us. Whoever truly expects the Bridegroom to come lives like a bride making herself ready.

And it gives us a prayer for every day. "Come, Lord Jesus" is the prayer of the whole church – in the persecution of Smyrna, in the comfort of Laodicea, and in your own life. You can pray it when the world looks dark, when your own heart is tired, and when you simply long to see him. It is the prayer of someone who knows how the story ends.

Pray today, and every morning this week, the last prayer of the Bible: "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!"

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Which picture from these 31 days has helped you most to see behind the curtain? How do you want to live it out this week (v. 7)?
  • Do you live as though Jesus is coming soon – or as though he is never coming (v. 12)?
  • Who in your life is thirsty, and how can you join the Spirit and the bride in saying to them, "Come!" (v. 17)?
  • Can you pray "Come, Lord Jesus" with joy, or rather with hesitation (v. 20)? What does that say about your heart?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the beginning and the end: thank you for drawing back the curtain and letting me see who sits on the throne. You are the root and the descendant of David, the bright morning star who ends the night.

I sometimes live as though you were not coming, and I have given my heart to the things of this passing world. Forgive me. Wash my robes in your blood, and make me faithful to keep your words.

Thank you that your grace is the first and the last word. Make me a voice with the Spirit and the bride, saying to a thirsty world: Come! I long for the day when I will see your face. Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!
