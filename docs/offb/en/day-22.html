Behind the Curtain · Day 22 / 31

The Seven Bowls Are Poured Out: It Is Done!

Scripture Revelation 16:1–21

1And I heard a loud voice from the temple saying to the seven angels, "Go and pour out the seven bowls of the fury of God on the earth!"

2And the first went and poured out his bowl on the earth; and a foul and evil sore came on the people who had the mark of the beast and who worshipped its image.

3And the second poured out his bowl into the sea; and it became blood like that of a dead man, and every living soul died, everything that was in the sea.

4And the third poured out his bowl into the rivers and the springs of water; and they became blood. 5And I heard the angel of the waters say:

Just are you, who is and who was, the Holy One,because you have judged these things;6for they poured out the blood of saints and prophets,and you have given them blood to drink.They deserve it.

7And I heard the altar say:

Yes, Lord God, the Almighty,true and just are your judgements.

8And the fourth poured out his bowl on the sun; and it was allowed to scorch people with fire. 9And the people were scorched with fierce heat, and they blasphemed the name of God, who has authority over these plagues, and they did not repent and give him glory.

10And the fifth poured out his bowl on the throne of the beast; and its kingdom was plunged into darkness, and people gnawed their tongues in pain, 11and they blasphemed the God of heaven because of their pains and their sores, and they did not repent of their works.

12And the sixth poured out his bowl on the great river, the Euphrates; and its water was dried up, so that the way would be prepared for the kings from the rising of the sun. 13And I saw coming out of the mouth of the dragon and out of the mouth of the beast and out of the mouth of the false prophet three unclean spirits like frogs. 14For they are spirits of demons that perform signs, and they go out to the kings of the whole world, to gather them for the war of the great day of God, the Almighty. 15– "Look, I am coming like a thief. Blessed is the one who stays awake and keeps his clothes, so that he does not walk around naked and people see his shame." – 16And they gathered them at the place that is called in Hebrew Armageddon.

17And the seventh poured out his bowl into the air; and a loud voice came out of the temple, from the throne, saying, "It is done!" 18And there were lightning and sounds and thunder, and there was a great earthquake, such as there had never been since people were on the earth, so great an earthquake, so mighty. 19And the great city split into three parts, and the cities of the nations fell. And God remembered Babylon the Great, to give her the cup of the wine of the fury of his wrath. 20And every island fled, and mountains were not found. 21And great hail, each stone as heavy as a talent, comes down from heaven on the people; and the people blasphemed God because of the plague of the hail, for its plague is exceedingly great.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Yesterday we heard God's people singing on the shore of the sea of glass, and the seven angels received seven golden bowls. Today a loud voice rings out from the temple: "Go and pour out the seven bowls of the fury of God on the earth!" What follows is one of the hardest chapters in the Bible to read. But anyone at home in the Old Testament recognises the images: these are the plagues of Egypt. The sores (Exodus 9:9–11), the Nile turned to blood (Exodus 7:17–21), the darkness over the land (Exodus 10:21–23), the frogs (Exodus 8:2–7), the hail (Exodus 9:22–26). Back then God brought the plagues on Pharaoh because he held God's people in slavery and had their sons drowned in the Nile (Exodus 1:22). Now the plagues fall on the kingdom of the beast, on "the people who had the mark of the beast and who worshipped its image", and the fifth bowl is poured out "on the throne of the beast". For believers who lived every day in the shadow of Rome's throne, this was a liberating message: that throne is not eternal.

We have already seen similar images with the trumpets (chapters 8–9), but there a third of the earth was struck, here everything. The bowls are not the next chapter in a calendar but the same reality in its full seriousness: the trumpets warn, the bowls show that God's patience with evil has an end.

The sixth bowl dries up the Euphrates, "so that the way would be prepared for the kings from the rising of the sun". For the first readers the Euphrates was the eastern frontier beyond which the dreaded Parthians waited. But the image is older: according to the Greek historian Herodotus (Histories 1.191), Cyrus took ancient Babylon by diverting the river, and Jeremiah said in God's name: I will dry up her sea (Jeremiah 51:36). Then three unclean spirits like frogs come out of the mouth of the dragon, the beast and the false prophet to gather the kings of the world "at the place that is called in Hebrew Armageddon".

Insights from the Greek

  • Bowls (phialas – φιάλας, v. 1): From phialē. The same word as the "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints" (5:8). Many interpreters see a deliberate link: the bowls of wrath are part of God's answer to the prayers of his people (compare 8:3–5).
  • They deserve it (axioi eisin – ἄξιοί εἰσιν, v. 6): The same word as "Worthy are you" (4:11; 5:9), used here with sharp irony. They "poured out the blood of saints and prophets" – the same verb as the pouring out of the bowls – and now they drink blood. God's judgement fits the evil exactly.
  • They did not repent (ou metenoēsan – οὐ μετενόησαν, vv. 9, 11): As with the trumpets (9:20–21). The plagues by themselves bring no one to repentance, just as Pharaoh's heart grew harder with every plague (Exodus 8:15, 32).
  • Armageddon (Harmagedōn – Ἁρμαγεδών, v. 16): According to most interpreters, "the mountain of Megiddo". Megiddo lies at the edge of the plain where famous battles were fought: Deborah and Barak against the kings of Canaan (Judges 5:19), and there King Josiah fell (2 Kings 23:29). But there is no mountain of Megiddo; the name is a symbol of the final revolt of the kings against God. (Futurists expect a literal battle in Israel.) The battle itself is never described.
  • It is done (Gegonen – Γέγονεν, v. 17): The perfect tense – something has happened and stands. The same verb sounds again at the new creation (Gegonan, 21:6). Many also hear in it an echo of Jesus' last word on the cross, that all is finished (John 19:30), even though a different Greek word stands there.

Core Theological Truths

  1. God's wrath is his just "no" to the evil that destroys his creation and his people. The angel of the waters says, "Just are you, who is and who was, the Holy One", and the altar answers, "Yes, Lord God, the Almighty, true and just are your judgements." Beneath that altar the souls cried, "How long" (6:10). Now the altar says yes. God's wrath is not a mood; it is his faithfulness to the victims.

  2. Judgement alone does not change a heart. Three times the people blaspheme God, and twice we read that they did not repent. They know who has authority over the plagues; the problem is a heart that will not give him glory (v. 9). That is the tragedy of Pharaoh – and a warning to anyone who thinks that a big enough shock would make him turn round.

  3. In the middle of the storm Christ speaks to his own. Suddenly he interrupts the scene: "Look, I am coming like a thief. Blessed is the one who stays awake and keeps his clothes." These are the words to Sardis (3:3) and to Laodicea (3:18). In Egypt God made a distinction between his people in Goshen and the land of Pharaoh (Exodus 8:22; 9:26). The bowls strike those who bear the mark of the beast, not those who bear his seal (7:3). Believers still suffer in this world, but they do not stand under God's wrath.

  4. The end of evil has already been decided. The kings gather for war, but before any battle is described, the voice from the throne rings out: "It is done!" God remembers Babylon, the great city – and tomorrow we see who she really is. The story that began in Egypt does not end at Armageddon but at the new creation (21:6).

Living It Out

Honestly, this chapter makes us uncomfortable. We prefer the Lamb to the bowls. But ask someone who has suffered injustice – a woman abused for years, a church under persecution, a people devastated by war – and they will tell you: a God who never judged evil would not be good. Revelation 16 is comfort for those who suffer, not a weapon for threatening others.

That is why we should not read this chapter about "other people", but about our own hearts. Pain can drive a person closer to God, or it can make you bitter and hard. Which happens to you when life is hard – prayer or bitterness? There is no shame in telling God that you are angry and hurt; the souls under the altar did that too. But there is a difference between crying out to God and cursing God.

And then Jesus' word in the middle: stay awake and keep your clothes. Staying awake does not mean calculating dates or searching the news for signs. It means living every day as someone who belongs to Christ, clothed in his righteousness.

Take two minutes tonight and ask yourself where you have fallen asleep this past week – in your prayer, your honesty, your love. Confess it, and then pray for one person or place in the world that suffers injustice, using the words of verse 7: Yes, Lord, true and just are your judgements.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What injustice – in your own life or in the world – makes you long for God to intervene (vv. 5–7)?
  • When something hurts, do you become softer or harder towards God (vv. 9, 11)?
  • Where have you fallen asleep spiritually lately, and which "clothes" of faith have you left lying about (v. 15)?
  • Do you really believe that the end of evil has already been decided (v. 17)? How would that change your fear today?

Prayer

Just and holy are you, Lord God, the Almighty, and true and just are your judgements. You see every tear of your suffering children and every drop of blood poured out on the earth. You judged Pharaoh's kingdom and set your people free, and you will bring the evil of our time to an end as well.

When things hurt, I easily become bitter, and sometimes I harden my heart against you instead of crying out to you. Forgive me, also for the places where I have fallen asleep and left the clothes of my faith lying about.

Lord Jesus, you are coming soon. Keep me awake, clothe me in your righteousness, and let me live as someone who belongs to you. Thank you that I do not stand under your wrath, because you drank the cup for me. In your name, Amen.
