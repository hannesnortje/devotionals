Behind the Curtain · Day 21 / 31

The Song of Moses and of the Lamb: Singing on the Shore

Scripture Revelation 15:1–8

1And I saw another sign in heaven, great and wonderful: seven angels with seven plagues, the last ones, for with them the fury of God is completed.

2And I saw something like a sea of glass mixed with fire, and those who conquer the beast and its image and the number of its name, standing beside the sea of glass, holding lyres of God. 3And they sing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb, saying:

Great and wonderful are your works,Lord God, the Almighty;just and true are your ways,King of the ages.4Who will not fear you, Lord,and glorify your name?For you alone are holy.For all the nations will comeand worship before you,for your righteous acts have been revealed.

5And after this I looked, and the temple of the tent of witness in heaven was opened, 6and the seven angels who had the seven plagues came out of the temple, dressed in clean, shining linen, with golden sashes around their chests. 7And one of the four living creatures gave the seven angels seven golden bowls full of the fury of God, who lives for ever and ever. 8And the temple was filled with smoke from the glory of God and from his power, and no one could enter the temple until the seven plagues of the seven angels were completed.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

A new sign appears in heaven, "great and wonderful: seven angels with seven plagues, the last ones, for with them the fury of God is completed." After the Lamb on Mount Zion and the harvest of the earth, you would expect the next scene to be the plagues themselves. But before a single bowl is poured out, we first hear a song.

John sees "something like a sea of glass mixed with fire". We have seen this sea before the throne already, "like crystal" (4:6). Now it glows with fire. And beside the sea stand "those who conquer the beast and its image and the number of its name", holding lyres. Anyone who knew the Old Testament would recognise the picture: Israel on the shore of the Red Sea, behind them the water that had covered Pharaoh's army, and Moses and Miriam singing: I sing to the LORD, for he has risen high, so high; horse and rider he has hurled into the sea (Exodus 15:1, 21). In the Bible the sea is often the picture of chaos and threat – the beast came up out of the sea (13:1). But this sea lies still before the throne, and God's people stand safely on the far side of it.

Think of the first readers. For them the beast was no abstract idea. It was the power that demanded that everyone honour its image, the pressure in the marketplace, the price some of their brothers and sisters had already paid for their witness. Yet it is precisely them – not the emperors – whom John sees standing on the shore and singing. They have conquered the beast in the way of the Lamb: by refusing to bow, even when it cost them their lives (12:11).

Then, in verse 5, "the temple of the tent of witness in heaven was opened". This is the language of the wilderness: the tent that Moses built, with the tablets of the testimony in the ark (Exodus 38:21; 40:20). When it was finished, the glory of the LORD filled it, so that Moses could not go in (Exodus 40:34–35; compare Isaiah 6:4). The same happens here: "no one could enter the temple until the seven plagues of the seven angels were completed." Plagues, sea, song and tent – Revelation 15 retells the whole exodus in eight verses, and tomorrow the bowls are poured out.

Insights from the Greek

  • Those who conquer the beast (tous nikōntas ek tou thēriou – τοὺς νικῶντας ἐκ τοῦ θηρίου, v. 2): The participle is in the present tense, like the promise to every church: "To the one who conquers …" (2:7–3:21). The preposition ek ("out of") is unusual; many interpreters hear in it that they have broken free from the grip of the beast.
  • The song of Moses … and the song of the Lamb (tēn ōdēn Mōyseōs … kai tēn ōdēn tou arniou – τὴν ᾠδὴν Μωϋσέως … καὶ τὴν ᾠδὴν τοῦ ἀρνίου, v. 3): Probably not two songs but one: the song of the exodus, now sung about a greater exodus. The blood of the Passover lamb brought Israel out of Egypt (Exodus 12); the blood of the Lamb has purchased people for God "from every tribe and language and people and nation" (5:9).
  • King of the ages (ho basileus tōn aiōnōn – ὁ βασιλεὺς τῶν αἰώνων, v. 3): Emperors come and go; God is King over every era of history.
  • You alone are holy (monos hosios – μόνος ὅσιος, v. 4): Hosios is seldom used of God in the New Testament. In the Greek translation of the song of Moses in Deuteronomy 32:4, God is called dikaios kai hosios, "just and holy" – the two words that are said of God together again in 16:5.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The victory is sung before the battle seems over. The song comes before the bowls, not after them. Israel sang on the shore while the whole wilderness still lay ahead of them, on the way to the land God had promised. In the same way God's people know the outcome before the last plague is poured out: the Lamb has already conquered (5:5), and those who belong to him already stand safely on the far side of the sea.

  2. The song is about God, not about the singers. There is not a word about their own suffering or courage; every line is addressed to God. The words are woven from the Old Testament: the song by the sea (Exodus 15:11), the psalms (Psalm 111:2; 86:9) and Jeremiah's question, who would not fear you, King of the nations? (Jeremiah 10:7).

  3. In the middle of judgement there is hope for the nations. "For all the nations will come and worship before you." This is the promise of the prophets – Micah says: "Many nations will come" (Micah 4:2). God's judgements reveal his "righteous acts", so that people may see him as he is. The bowls of fury and the hope for the nations stand in one and the same chapter.

  4. God judges from his holy presence, according to what he has openly made known. The seven angels come out of "the tent of witness", the place of the covenant and its tablets. God's judgement is not arbitrary; he judges according to his own testimony. The angels are dressed like priests, "with golden sashes around their chests", like the son of man among the lampstands (1:13). The smoke in the temple says that his holiness is serious.

Living It Out

There are times when singing comes easily, and times when you can hardly get the words out. Perhaps you are not yet standing on the shore but in the middle of the sea: a diagnosis that does not change, a marriage that has become hard, a workplace where faithfulness costs you something. Revelation 15 does not ask you to pretend that all is well; it shows you where the story ends: with a people standing beside the sea and singing.

That is why the church's song is not merely an expression of how we feel. It is an act of faith. When a small congregation sings on an ordinary Sunday, it is already rehearsing the song of the shore. Every time we sing "just and true are your ways", we say something we cannot always see yet, but which God already knows.

Notice, too, who the conquerors are. Not the successful or the influential, but ordinary believers who refused to bow before the image of the beast. Today that bowing may look different: going along with dishonesty at work, or worshipping what everyone worships – success, security, status. Every time you say no and stay with the Lamb, you conquer in his way, even if it does not feel like a victory at all. And because the song is not about the singers, it lifts our eyes too, to the one whose works are "great and wonderful". That does not make our problems smaller, but it puts them into bigger hands.

Begin every morning this week with the song of Revelation 15:3–4 – sing it, or say it slowly – and then thank God for one concrete work he has done in your life.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Where are you standing at the moment: still in the sea, or already on the shore? What would it mean to sing the song of verse 3 now already?
  • Which "image of the beast" is currently asking you to bow – at work, among your friends, in your own heart (v. 2)?
  • How many of your prayers are about God himself – his works, his ways, his name – and how many only about you (vv. 3–4)?
  • Do you believe that all the nations will come and worship (v. 4)? For which person or people who seem far from God do you want to start praying?

Prayer

Lord God, the Almighty, King of the ages: great and wonderful are your works, just and true are your ways. You led your people through the sea, and through the blood of the Lamb you have brought us out from the power of sin and death. You alone are holy.

Forgive me for so often singing only when things go well, and falling silent when the water is high. Forgive me for the times I have bowed before the images of this world because it was easier than staying faithful.

Teach me to sing the song of the shore now already. Keep me with the Lamb, and let me one day stand before you with people from all the nations and worship you for ever. Amen.
