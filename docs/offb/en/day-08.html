Philadelphia – Little Power, but an Open Door No One Can Shut

Scripture Revelation 3:7–13

7And to the angel of the church in Philadelphia write: The Holy One, the True One, who has the key of David, who opens and no one will shut, and who shuts and no one opens, says this: 8I know your works – look, I have set before you an open door, which no one is able to shut – that you have little power, and yet you have kept my word and have not denied my name. 9Look, I will give you some from the synagogue of Satan, who say they are Jews and are not, but are lying – look, I will make them come and bow down before your feet, and they will know that I have loved you. 10Because you have kept the word of my endurance, I too will keep you from the hour of testing that is about to come on the whole world, to test those who live on the earth. 11I am coming soon. Hold fast to what you have, so that no one takes your crown. 12The one who conquers, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God, and he will never go out of it again. And I will write on him the name of my God and the name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down out of heaven from my God, and my new name. 13Whoever has an ear should hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

About 45 kilometres south-east of Sardis lies the youngest of the seven cities: Philadelphia (today's Alaşehir in Turkey). The city was founded in the second century BC by King Attalus II of Pergamum, who was so loyal to his brother Eumenes that he was given the nickname Philadelphos (meaning: he who loves his brother) – hence the name brotherly love.

Philadelphia was built for two specific reasons. First, it was a strategic gateway city (an "open door") to the central plateau of Phrygia. It is often said that its founders wanted Philadelphia to serve as a "missionary city" for Greek culture and language, to hellenise the interior. Second, it lay on a fertile volcanic plain that was ideal for vineyards – but this same geological setting came at a terrifying price: earthquakes. In AD 17 Philadelphia (together with Sardis and other cities) was struck by a devastating earthquake. For years afterwards aftershocks shook the city; the ancient geographer Strabo writes that Philadelphia's walls were constantly cracking and that few people lived in the city itself – most lived outside in the countryside. They knew what it feels like when the ground gives way under your feet and you have to flee again and again.

Apart from Smyrna, Philadelphia is the only other church against which Jesus speaks not a single word of criticism or rebuke.

Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into a few Greek key words in the text:

  • Alēthinos – ἀληθινός (true / genuine): In verse 7 Jesus calls himself "the Holy One, the True One". In John's writings the two words overlap, but where alēthēs – ἀληθής often describes someone who speaks the truth (does not lie), alēthinos often stresses what is genuine, authentic and real (as opposed to a counterfeit or a shadow). Over against the hypocritical religion of their opponents, Jesus is the one true God.
  • Mikran dynamin – μικρὰν δύναμιν (little power): When Jesus says in verse 8 "that you have little power", this is not a rebuke for weak faith. It describes their social standing and their small numbers. The church was small, poor, without political influence and without anyone powerful in its ranks.
  • Stylos – στῦλος (pillar): In verse 12 Jesus promises to make the conqueror a stylos (a supporting pillar) in the temple of God. For people living in an earthquake zone, where huge stone columns regularly toppled, the picture of an unshakeable pillar in God's eternal temple was the ultimate symbol of lasting stability and security.

Core Theological Truths

This tender and encouraging letter reveals four great theological truths:

  1. Jesus holds supreme authority over access to God's kingdom (vv. 7–8): Jesus quotes directly from Isaiah 22:22, where Eliakim receives the key of the house of David – the palace and treasury of King Hezekiah. In Philadelphia the local Jewish synagogue had probably excluded the Jewish Christians and slammed the synagogue door in their faces (cf. v. 9). They claimed that these believers now stood outside God's covenant and kingdom. It is as if Jesus answers: "Do not let closed human doors trouble you. I hold the key of David! I open to you the door to the Father's heart and the door for the ministry of the gospel, and no synagogue, emperor or demon can shut what I have opened."
  2. Human weakness that clings to God's word is unstoppable (v. 8): In the eyes of the world Philadelphia had "little power" (mikran dynamin). They had no mega-buildings, no big budgets and no high-profile leaders. But they did two things that count in heaven: they kept his word (lived it out in obedience) and did not deny his name. God is not looking for our impressive abilities; he is looking for our availability and faithfulness. His power is made perfect precisely in our weakness (2 Corinthians 12:9).
  3. Faithfulness today guarantees God's keeping in tomorrow's crisis (vv. 9–10): Jesus promises two wonderful outcomes. First, their fiercest opponents will one day have to acknowledge that God has deeply loved this despised little group of believers (cf. Isaiah 60:14). Second, because they have kept "the word of my endurance" – Jesus' call to endure patiently – he will keep them "from the hour of testing" (tērēsō se ek – τηρήσω σε ἐκ – to keep safe through the danger or out of it) that is about to come on the whole world, "to test those who live on the earth". The preposition ek – ἐκ means "out of". It does not necessarily mean that they will be spared that hour; it can just as well mean that he keeps them through it and brings them out of it. Jesus uses the same words in his prayer in John 17:15: not that the Father should take his disciples out of the world, but that he should "keep them from" the evil one. Whether the world around them shakes or not, their souls are untouchable in his hand.
  4. From earthquake refugees to eternal pillars with a threefold name (v. 12): When Emperor Tiberius helped to rebuild Philadelphia after the earthquake of AD 17, the city out of gratitude changed its name for a time to Neokaisareia (meaning: the new city of Caesar), and later, under Emperor Vespasian, took yet another imperial name. It is as if Jesus says to his children in such an unstable city: "In my kingdom you will never again have to flee from an earthquake ('he will never go out of it again'). You will be firm pillars. And I do not engrave on you the name of a changeable emperor, but a threefold eternal identity: the name of my God (you are his own), the name of the new Jerusalem (your eternal citizenship), and my own new name."

Living It Out

How often do we feel like the church in Philadelphia today! You look at the huge challenges in your family, your workplace or your community, then you look at your own resources and think: "Lord, I just have too little strength. I'm too ordinary, too tired, too insignificant to make a difference."

On top of that you may already know the pain of doors slammed in your face – a job opportunity that fell through because of your integrity, a relationship in which you were rejected, or a dream that lies in pieces.

Philadelphia brings you indescribable comfort today: Your "little power" is no obstacle to the One who holds the key of David! When people slam a door in your face because you stay faithful to Jesus, the King of kings stands ready to open a door before you that no human power can push shut. It is an open door of intimate access to God's throne of grace, and an open door of opportunities to let his light shine exactly where he has placed you.

Perhaps today it feels as if "the earth is shaking" under your feet – financially, emotionally or physically. Everything that looked so strong on earth is cracking like Philadelphia's walls. Do not lose heart. "Hold fast to what you have" (v. 11). Stay faithful to his Word in the little things. Your security does not lie in earthly walls that can fall down, but in the promise that you are an unshakeable pillar in God's eternal house, and that the King has written his own name on your heart.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Do you feel overwhelmed at the moment by your own "little power" in a particular area of your life? How does it change your attitude to know that Jesus sees your very smallness and wants to work through it?
  • Are you staring so hard at a door that has been slammed shut in your life that you may be missing the "open door" of service and faith that Jesus has opened right in front of you?
  • Where in your life are you looking for stability and a "name" in worldly things that can collapse like Philadelphia's buildings, instead of resting in the fact that Christ has written his name on you?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you are the Holy One and the True One – the only one who is truly genuine and trustworthy in a world full of pretence. I thank you that today you hold the key of David in your pierced hand. You know my life; you see how often I feel that I have too little strength for the demands and storms of each day. Thank you that you do not reject me because of my weakness, but that your power is made perfect in my frailty.

Help me to stop grieving over the doors that people or circumstances have slammed shut in front of me, and give me eyes to see the open door you have set before me. Give me the grace to cling to your Word through thick and thin and never to deny your name.

When everything around me shakes as in an earthquake, anchor my soul in the knowledge that I belong to you, that I am a citizen of the new Jerusalem, and that you love me with an everlasting love. Amen.
