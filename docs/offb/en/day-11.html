Behind the Curtain · Day 11 / 31

The Lion Who Is a Lamb: The Key to Revelation

Scripture Revelation 5:1–14

1And I saw in the right hand of the one seated on the throne a scroll written inside and on the back, sealed with seven seals. 2And I saw a mighty angel proclaiming with a loud voice, "Who is worthy to open the scroll and to break its seals?" 3And no one in heaven or on earth or under the earth was able to open the scroll or to look into it. 4And I wept and wept, because no one was found worthy to open the scroll or to look into it. 5And one of the elders says to me, "Do not weep. Look, the Lion from the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered, so that he can open the scroll and its seven seals."

6And I saw in the middle of the throne and of the four living creatures, and in the middle of the elders, a Lamb standing, as though slaughtered. He had seven horns and seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth. 7And he came and took it out of the right hand of the one seated on the throne. 8And when he had taken the scroll, the four living creatures and the twenty-four elders fell down before the Lamb, each holding a lyre and golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints. 9And they sing a new song:

Worthy are you to take the scrolland to open its seals,for you were slaughtered,and by your blood you purchased people for Godfrom every tribe and language and people and nation,10and you made them a kingdom and priests to our God,and they reign on the earth.

11And I looked, and I heard the voice of many angels around the throne and the living creatures and the elders, and their number was ten thousands of ten thousands and thousands of thousands, 12saying with a loud voice:

Worthy is the Lamb who was slaughteredto receive the power and wealth and wisdom and strengthand honour and glory and praise.

13And every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and on the sea, and everything in them, I heard saying:

To the one seated on the throne and to the Lambbe the praise and the honour and the glory and the mightfor ever and ever.

14And the four living creatures said, "Amen." And the elders fell down and worshipped.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In the right hand of the Creator seated on the throne, John now sees something on which everything hangs: a scroll, "written inside and on the back, sealed with seven seals". It recalls the scroll Ezekiel received, also written on both sides (Ezekiel 2:9–10), and the book Daniel was told to seal until the time of the end (Daniel 12:4). The scroll probably stands for God's purpose for history – his judgement on evil and the coming of his kingdom. As long as it stays sealed, everything stands still.

Then the question rings through heaven: "Who is worthy to open the scroll and to break its seals?" And no one – not in heaven, not on earth, not under the earth – is able to do it. John weeps. These are the tears of every believer in Asia who wonders whether the suffering of Smyrna and the blood of the witnesses who lost their lives have any meaning.

One of the elders comforts him: "Do not weep. Look, the Lion from the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered." Every Jewish reader knew these titles. Jacob called Judah a young lion, from whose tribe the sceptre would never depart (Genesis 49:9–10), and Isaiah spoke of the shoot from the stump of Jesse, the Root to whom the nations would turn (Isaiah 11:1, 10). The Lion is the Messiah, the king many expected to crush Israel's enemies.

And then John looks – and sees a Lamb. "And I saw in the middle of the throne and of the four living creatures, and in the middle of the elders, a Lamb standing, as though slaughtered." Here lies the key to the whole book. John hears "Lion", but he sees a slaughtered Lamb – and the seeing interprets the hearing. The Lion of Judah does not conquer by tearing apart, but by being torn. God's power looks like the cross.

Insights from the Greek

  • Lamb (arnion – ἀρνίον, v. 6): John uses this word 29 times in the book, and with one exception (the beast of 13:11) only for Christ. Behind the image stand the Passover lamb of Exodus 12, whose blood set Israel free from Egypt, and the Servant of the LORD who was led like a lamb to the slaughter (Isaiah 53:7).
  • As though slaughtered (hōs esphagmenon – ὡς ἐσφαγμένον, v. 6): The verb form describes something that has happened and leaves its mark. The Lamb is standing – he is alive – but he still bears the wounds. The risen Christ has not left his cross behind; he reigns as the Crucified One.
  • Has conquered (enikēsen – ἐνίκησεν, v. 5): It is the same verb as in the promise to every church: "The one who conquers …" (2:7–3:21). Christ has already conquered, and his people conquer in the same way as he did (3:21).
  • Seven horns and seven eyes (v. 6): In the Old Testament a horn is an image of strength (Deuteronomy 33:17), and the seven eyes come from Zechariah 4:10. The Lamb who looks weak has perfect power and sees everything.
  • Purchased (ēgorasas – ἠγόρασας, v. 9): The word of the market, which could also describe the buying of slaves. By his blood the Lamb "purchased people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation". And verse 10 says, in the present tense, "and they reign on the earth" – not one day, but already now.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The Lamb is the key to Scripture and to Revelation. Israel was looking for a Lion, and God sent a Lamb. This does not mean the promises were falsified; it shows where they were really heading. That is why we read every mighty, even terrifying image still to come in the book through this one: from now on the throne is "the throne of God and of the Lamb" (22:3).

  2. Only the Crucified One can open history. No one else is worthy. The meaning of history does not lie in Rome's hands or in the hands of the powerful of our day, but in the hands of the one who died for the world. The cross is not an interruption of God's plan; it is the opening of it.

  3. The Lamb receives the worship that belongs to God. Yesterday the elders sang, "Worthy are you, our Lord and God." Today they sing the same words to the Lamb: "Worthy are you to take the scroll." And at the end the whole creation sings: "To the one seated on the throne and to the Lamb." Whoever worships the Lamb is not worshipping a second god, but the one God.

  4. The Lamb has made a new people – a kingdom and priests. What God promised Israel at Sinai (Exodus 19:6) comes true for people from every nation. They reign, but like their King: not by violence, but by witness and self-giving love. So Revelation will later say of the accuser: "And they have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb" (12:11).

Living It Out

We all want a Lion. When work wears us down, when our children get hurt, when the church is mocked in society, we long for power that puts things right quickly – a winning argument, influence, a God who simply flattens our enemies. It is not wrong to long for God to intervene. But Revelation 5 asks us to look at what God actually sent. The Lion of Judah conquers as a Lamb, and he invites us to conquer in the same way.

So the places where you feel weak or overlooked are not necessarily places where God is absent. It is often there that his power looks most clearly like the cross: in patience that does not hit back, in forgiveness that costs something, in faithfulness that no one notices.

Look, too, at the golden bowls full of incense, "which are the prayers of the saints" (v. 8; cf. Psalm 141:2). The prayers you have prayed for years, which sometimes seemed to vanish into thin air, have not been thrown away. They stand before the throne, in the hands of those who worship the Lamb.

And if, like John, you have wept because it seems that no one can open up your situation – hear the elder's word: "Do not weep." The scroll is in the right hands.

This week, choose one place where you would love to "roar" – a conflict, an injustice, a frustration – and ask: how would the Lamb conquer here? Then take one small step of self-giving love in that direction.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Which "scroll" in your life seems closed and sealed at the moment, so that you want to weep with John (v. 4)?
  • Where do you long for a Lion rather than the Lamb – for power that sorts things out rather than love that costs something (vv. 5–6)?
  • Do you really believe that your prayers are kept before the throne (v. 8)? How would that change your prayer life?
  • What does it mean for you to belong already to a kingdom and priests from every nation (vv. 9–10)?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, Lion of Judah and Lamb of God, you are worthy. You were slaughtered, and by your blood you purchased people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation – me too. With the angels and the whole creation I bring praise and honour and glory today to you and to the one seated on the throne.

Forgive me that I so often want a Lion to sort out my affairs, and have so little desire to follow the Lamb. Forgive my impatience, my harsh words and my hunger for power. You did not conquer by hitting back, but by giving yourself.

Thank you that the scroll of history, and of my life too, is in your hands, and that my prayers are kept before your throne. Teach me to conquer your way today: with faithfulness, patience and love that costs something. In your name, Amen.
