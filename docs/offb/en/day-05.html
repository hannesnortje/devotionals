Behind the Curtain · Day 5 / 31

Pergamum: Where Satan's Throne Is – The Danger of Subtle Compromise

Scripture Revelation 2:12–17

12And to the angel of the church in Pergamum write: The one who has the sharp two-edged sword says this: 13I know where you live: where the throne of Satan is. Yet you hold fast to my name, and you did not deny your faith in me, even in the days of Antipas, my witness, my faithful one, who was killed among you, where Satan lives. 14But I have a few things against you: you have people there who hold fast to the teaching of Balaam, who taught Balak to put a stumbling block before the people of Israel, so that they would eat food sacrificed to idols and commit sexual immorality. 15So you too have people who in the same way hold fast to the teaching of the Nicolaitans. 16So repent. If not, I am coming to you soon, and I will make war against them with the sword of my mouth. 17Whoever has an ear should hear what the Spirit says to the churches. To the one who conquers I will give some of the hidden manna, and I will give him a white stone, and on the stone a new name is written that no one knows except the one who receives it.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today our journey takes us about 100 kilometres north of Smyrna and a little inland to Pergamum (today's Bergama in Turkey). If Ephesus was the commercial heart of Asia, Pergamum was the old royal city and for a long time the official capital of the Roman province, carrying great political and religious weight. The city was built on an imposing, steep hill that towered some 300 metres above the surrounding plain. Pergamum was famous for its huge library, which according to tradition held 200,000 scrolls (and according to the well-known story, the word parchment comes from the name Pergamum).

In and around the city stood a forest of pagan temples:

  1. On top of the acropolis, the enormous horseshoe-shaped Altar of Zeus, which looked like a giant throne on the hillside.
  2. Down on the plain, the world-famous sanctuary of Asclepius, the god of healing, whose symbol was the serpent and who was honoured as "Saviour" (Sōtēr).
  3. The imperial temple to Augustus and Roma (29 BC) – the first temple in the province to a living emperor; later Pergamum would gain further imperial temples.

What is more, the Roman governor (proconsul), who also held court in Pergamum, possessed the ius gladii (literally, the right of the sword) – the authority to impose the death penalty. So it is fitting that in verse 12 Jesus introduces himself as "The one who has the sharp two-edged sword". The Roman governor may wield an earthly sword, but Jesus holds the supreme sword of eternal judgement.

Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into a few key words of the Greek text:

  • "Live" (katoikeis – κατοικεῖς, v. 13): In verse 13 Jesus says: "I know where you live." Greek has a word for staying temporarily, like a foreigner or someone living in a tent (paroikeō – παροικέω), but katoikeō means to settle permanently and put down roots. The believers' address lay literally in the shadow of the devil's stronghold – but for some in the church the world around them had sadly also begun to feel too much like "home".
  • "Stumbling block" (skandalon – σκάνδαλον, v. 14): In verse 14 Jesus refers to Balaam, who taught Balak to put a skandalon before Israel. The word probably first meant the stick or trigger inside an animal trap to which the bait was tied; in the Bible it already means a snare or stumbling block. As soon as the animal takes the bait, the trap snaps shut. It describes how sin always looks attractive and innocent at first, before it catches you spiritually.
  • "Nicolaitans" (Nikolaitai – Νικολαΐται, v. 15; cf. 2:6): We know little about them for certain. The church father Irenaeus (about AD 180) links them with Nicolas, one of the seven in Acts 6:5; Clement of Alexandria, however, defends Nicolas and says his followers misunderstood him – many interpreters doubt the link. What John himself shows is clearer: verse 15 begins "So you too have" – their teaching was the same kind of teaching as Balaam's: eating meat offered to idols and committing sexual immorality. Many interpreters also hear a wordplay: Nikolaos can be read as "conqueror of the people" (nikaō + laos), and Jewish interpreters explained Balaam's name as "devourer of the people" – two names for the same thing. The same kind of teaching probably appears in Thyatira with "Jezebel" (2:20).
  • "White stone" (psēphos leukē – ψῆφος λευκή, v. 17): There are several explanations, and two fit particularly well. In the ancient world white and black pebbles were used for various purposes. In a law court a judge could drop a black pebble into an urn to declare guilt, and a white pebble for acquittal and innocence. In addition, small tablets or stones (tessera) were sometimes used as admission tickets to a banquet or the games.

Core Theological Truths

This passage sets four vital truths before us:

  1. Jesus knows your address and understands how hard your surroundings are (v. 13): Twice Jesus says that Satan lives in Pergamum and has his throne there. Whether this refers to the giant altar of Zeus, the serpent cult of Asclepius, or the centre of emperor worship and of the Roman power that persecuted Christians – the point is clear: Pergamum was a spiritual war zone. Jesus does not brush their hard circumstances aside. He praises them for clinging to his name under enormous pressure, even when a faithful member named Antipas (according to a later tradition roasted alive inside a hollow bronze bull) was killed for his faith. Jesus honours Antipas with the title he himself bears in Revelation 1:5, "the faithful witness": Antipas is "my witness, my faithful one" (martys – μάρτυς).
  2. Where frontal attacks fail, the enemy uses subtle compromise (vv. 14–15): When the devil saw that bloody persecution and the death of Antipas could not break the church in Pergamum, he changed tactics. In Numbers 22–25 the sorcerer Balaam could not curse the people of Israel, because God protected them. So Balaam gave King Balak devilish advice (Numbers 31:16): don't fight Israel; invite them to your feasts instead, let the Moabite women seduce the men, and soon Israel will provoke its own God. That is exactly what happened in Pergamum through the Nicolaitans. Their reasoning went: "God knows our hearts belong to him; surely it doesn't matter if we just eat meat with our business partners at the pagan temples and join in the wild guild parties to keep our jobs."
  3. Tolerating sin in our midst makes us complicit before God (vv. 14–16): Most of the church in Pergamum may not have committed sexual immorality or eaten idol meat themselves, but they quietly tolerated those who taught it. Whereas Ephesus (day 3) had truth without love, Pergamum had a twisted "love" (tolerance) without truth. Jesus demands that the whole church repent of this spiritual passivity – otherwise he himself will intervene with "the sword of my mouth".
  4. God's reward surpasses every worldly feast you give up for his sake (v. 17): To the believer who refuses to feast at the world's polluted idol banquets, Jesus promises the hidden manna – the true bread of heaven, intimate fellowship with himself (like the golden jar of manna hidden in the ark of the covenant, Hebrews 9:4). And to those rejected by society and found guilty by Roman judges, he gives the white stone of eternal acquittal and full access to the wedding supper of the Lamb (19:9), engraved with a new, intimate name that exists only between you and your Redeemer.

Living It Out

Many of us feel today as if we work or study in "Pergamum". You may find yourself in a corporate environment, on a university campus or in a circle of friends where it feels as if "Satan's throne" stands right next to your desk – a place where Christian values are mocked and dishonesty, lust or materialism are the norm.

It is encouraging to know that Jesus does not expect you to move into a monastery on a mountain. He says to Pergamum: "I know where you live." You can be a light in the darkest place, just like Antipas. His name is a short form of Antipatros, but preachers have long played on it: anti-pas, "against all" – a man willing to stand against the whole current of his culture for Jesus.

But Pergamum warns us against the enemy's most dangerous trick: syncretism (mixing our faith with worldly lifestyles). Few of us today would openly say: "I deny Jesus." We withstand the lions' den but fall for the "Balaam trap" of small, daily compromises. We watch the same impure entertainment as the world, laugh at the same dirty jokes, bend the rules on our tax returns, and justify it all under the cover of "grace" and "we don't want to seem judgemental". We try to stand with one foot in the kingdom while sitting with the other at the world's table. Today Jesus calls us back to undivided loyalty. Whatever you lose on earth because you refuse to compromise is nothing compared with the white stone and the hidden manna from his hand. Ephesus saw this clearly: "you hate the works of the Nicolaitans, which I also hate" (2:6) – the works, not the people. Compromise rarely announces itself as unfaithfulness; it comes as "freedom" or as "just being practical".

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What does the "Pergamum" you live and work in every day look like? What specific pressure do you feel to keep your faith quiet or to blend in with the crowd?
  • Is there a "Balaam stumbling block" in your life right now – an area where you have made a small moral or spiritual compromise and tried to talk yourself into thinking it is "not that bad"?
  • Do you look for your identity and acceptance in this world's "admission tickets" (status, popularity, financial success), or do you rest in the white stone and the new name Christ gives you?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you who hold the sharp two-edged sword of truth, I bow before your authority today. Thank you that you know exactly where I live, work and move, and that you fully understand the pressures and temptations that surround me every day. Lord, give me the backbone of an Antipas, so that I do not deny my faith in you when it becomes uncomfortable or costly.

But above all guard my heart against Balaam's subtle traps. Forgive me for the times I have compromised with the world to fit in, to avoid conflict or to gain an advantage. Search my life with the sword of your Word and cut away every growth of conformity to the world.

I do not need the world's approval or forbidden pleasure; feed my soul today with your hidden manna, and let me rest in the knowledge that I have been acquitted through your blood and have received a new name from your hand. Amen.
