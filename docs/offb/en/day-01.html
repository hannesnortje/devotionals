Behind the Curtain · Day 1 / 31

The Alpha and the Omega – Who Is at the Helm of Your Life?

Scripture Revelation 1:1–8

1The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show his servants what must soon take place. He made it known by sending it through his angel to his servant John, 2who testified to the word of God and to the testimony of Jesus Christ – everything he saw. 3Blessed is the one who reads aloud, and blessed are those who hear the words of the prophecy and keep what is written in it, for the time is near.

4John, to the seven churches in the province of Asia: Grace to you and peace from him who is and who was and who is coming, and from the seven spirits who are before his throne, 5and from Jesus Christ, the faithful witness, the firstborn of the dead and the ruler of the kings of the earth.

To him who loves us and has freed us from our sins by his blood6– and he made us a kingdom, priests to his God and Father –to him be the glory and the might for ever and ever. Amen.7Look, he is coming with the clouds,and every eye will see him,even those who pierced him,and all the tribes of the earth will mourn over him.Yes, amen.

8"I am the Alpha and the Omega," says the Lord God, who is and who was and who is coming, the Almighty.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today we begin a journey of thirty-one days through the book of Revelation. Many people see Revelation simply as a book about the end times, full of strange symbols. Yet it actually begins as a deeply personal pastoral letter. It opens with a breathtaking vision of the glorified Jesus Christ, followed by seven direct, loving but piercing letters to seven real churches in Asia Minor (chapters 1–3). After that the book leads us to the throne in heaven and the Lamb, through the seals, trumpets and bowls and the conflict with the dragon, all the way to the new heaven and the new earth. The very first word of the book, apokalypsis, means unveiling: the curtain is pulled back, and we are allowed to see what is happening behind the scenes.

Over the next 31 days we will walk through the whole book together. We will not merely be looking at ancient history; we will allow the risen Lord to look, through his Word, into our own hearts, habits and faith.

To understand Revelation rightly, we first need to know where and why it was written. Most scholars date the book around AD 95, during the reign of the Roman emperor Domitian (some think of an earlier date, shortly after Nero's persecution). According to the Roman historian Suetonius, Domitian liked to be addressed as Dominus et Deus – "Lord and God". In the cities of Asia Minor (today's western Turkey) honouring the emperor was part of everyday life: temples, festivals and trade guilds paid him homage. For Christians who refused to burn incense before the emperor's image, this could mean social rejection, economic boycott, prison and even death. They were afraid and discouraged, and felt as if the mighty Roman Empire was about to swallow the church.

John himself is on the rocky island of Patmos, probably as an exile. And precisely there, in his isolation, God tears open the curtain of the visible world.

Insights from the Greek

Let us look at a few key words from the original Greek that open up the depth of this introduction:

  • "Revelation" (apokalypsis – ἀποκάλυψις, v. 1): The first word of the Greek text is apokalypsis. In today's language "apocalypse" makes us think of the end of the world and chaos. But in Greek it simply means "the removing of a veil" or "unveiling". This book was not written to hide God's plans behind difficult codes, but precisely to pull the veil aside so that believers can see who really sits on the throne. Notice too: it is the revelation of Jesus Christ – he is both the Giver and the Content of this vision.
  • "What must soon take place" (ha dei genesthai en tachei – ἃ δεῖ γενέσθαι ἐν τάχει, verse 1): John takes up, almost word for word, Daniel 2:28, where Daniel tells Nebuchadnezzar that God has made known what must take place in the last days (so in the Greek translation of the Old Testament). John replaces "in the last days" with "soon": what was still far off for Daniel has begun with Christ. That is why verse 3 says, "for the time is near". For the seven churches this was no distant future but their own time – and because the whole time between Christ's first and second coming is the last days (Acts 2:17; Hebrews 1:2), every generation of believers lives in this "soon".
  • "Blessed" (makarios – μακάριος, v. 3): Verse 3 contains the first of seven beatitudes in Revelation. The word makarios describes a deep, spiritual joy and well-being that does not depend on outward circumstances. This blessing is promised to the one who reads aloud (in those days one person read the scroll aloud in the congregation), to those who hear (listen attentively), and to those who keep (tēreō – τηρέω: live it out and hold on to it).
  • "The Alpha and the Omega" (to Alpha kai to Ō – τὸ Ἄλφα καὶ τὸ Ὦ, v. 8): These are the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. In Jewish thought people sometimes said that something stretched "from Aleph to Tav" (the first and last Hebrew letters) to express completeness. God is saying: I wrote the first sentence of world history, I will write the final full stop, and I govern every letter and chapter in between.
  • "The Almighty" (pantokratōr – παντοκράτωρ, v. 8): At the end of verse 8 stands the mighty title pantokratōr – the one who lays his hand on everything, the Ruler of all. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament this word often stands where the Hebrew says "the LORD of hosts". Domitian thought he ruled the world with his legions; against him stands the Almighty God, who holds past, present and future in his hand.

Core Theological Truths

These first eight verses give us three unshakeable theological anchors:

  1. Our identity rests on Christ's finished work, not on our circumstances. Before John writes a single word about affliction or judgement, he reminds the churches who Jesus is and what he has done (vv. 5–6). The Greek uses the present tense: "To him who loves us" – his love never stops! And then the past tense: "and has freed us from our sins by his blood". (Some later manuscripts, followed by the King James Version, read "washed"; the oldest manuscripts read "freed".) We are not pitiful victims of the world; he "made us a kingdom, priests to his God and Father". A priest has direct access to God, and a king reigns with Christ.
  2. Jesus Christ is the true Ruler over every earthly power. He is described with three titles in verse 5: the faithful witness (he always speaks the full truth in a world full of political and spiritual lies), the firstborn of the dead (he has already conquered the enemy's worst weapon – death), and the ruler of the kings of the earth. No president, dictator, economic system or crisis stands outside his authority.
  3. History is moving towards a visible, certain climax. History is not turning aimlessly in circles. Verse 7 combines prophecies from Daniel 7:13 and Zechariah 12:10: "Look, he is coming with the clouds, and every eye will see him." What is now hidden from the world will one day be unmistakable.

Living It Out

When we look at our own lives, we may not be persecuted by a Roman emperor, but we know the feeling of being overwhelmed. We read the news, look at our bank statements, think about our health or our children's future, and it often feels as if chaos is in charge. We get stuck in the "middle" of our story, where things look uncertain and broken.

Revelation 1:1–8 sets our perspective straight. It asks us to stop staring at the "little emperors" of our day – fear, uncertainty, other people's opinions, or worldly powers – and to lift our eyes to the ruler of the kings of the earth.

Notice how John responds in the middle of his exile in verses 5 and 6: he bursts into a song of praise (a doxology). He does not wait until he is released from Patmos before he praises God. He praises him on Patmos, because he knows his sins are forgiven, he is deeply loved, and his King is coming. When you know who the Alpha and the Omega is, you need not panic about chapter 5 or chapter 12 of your life. The Author of your life is already at the end, and he is good.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What "veil" of fear, worry or daily rush is keeping you at the moment from seeing Jesus' rule over your situation?
  • Verse 3 promises blessing to those who do not only read and hear the Word, but keep it. Are you reading the Bible at the moment only to gather information, or are you letting it shape your choices and attitudes every day?
  • Are you living today as someone who is already loved, freed from your sins and part of Christ's kingdom of priests – or are you living as if you still have to earn God's favour or people's approval?

Prayer

Lord Jesus, you who are and who were and who are coming, the Almighty: today I bow my heart before you as the Alpha and the Omega of my life. Forgive me for so easily letting the storms, uncertainties and noise of this world blind me to your greatness. Please pull the veil from my eyes today.

Thank you that you do not merely rule me from a distance, but that you love me, have freed me from my sins by your own precious blood, and have made me part of your kingdom. When I face choices, pressure or fear today, remind me that you are the faithful Witness and the Ruler of the kings of the earth.

Open my ears to hear your Word on this journey through Revelation, and give me an obedient heart to keep it. To you belong all the glory and the might, today and for ever and ever. Amen.
