Eyes Fixed on Jesus · Day 26 / 28

Sinai and Zion: Receiving a Kingdom That Cannot Be Shaken

Scripture Hebrews 12:14–29

14Pursue peace with everyone, and holiness, without which no one will see the Lord. 15See to it that no one falls short of the grace of God, that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble, and through it many are defiled, 16that no one is sexually immoral or godless like Esau, who sold his birthright for a single meal. 17For you know that later, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected; for he found no place for repentance, although he sought it with tears.

18For you have not approached something that could be touched, and blazing fire, and darkness and gloom and storm, 19and the sound of a trumpet and a voice speaking words, whose hearers begged that no further word be spoken to them, 20for they could not bear the command, "Even if an animal touches the mountain, it shall be stoned." 21And so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, "I am full of fear and trembling." 22But you have approached Mount Zion and the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and myriads of angels, the festal gathering 23and assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in the heavens, and God, the judge of all, and the spirits of the righteous who have been made perfect, 24and Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and the blood of sprinkling that speaks better than Abel.

25See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking. For if they did not escape when they refused the one who warned them on earth, much less shall we, if we turn away from the one who warns from heaven. 26His voice shook the earth then, but now he has promised:

Once more I will shake not only the earthbut also heaven.

27This "Once more" shows that what is shaken will be removed, as things that have been made, so that what is not shaken may remain. 28Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be thankful, and so worship God in a way that pleases him, with reverence and awe; 29for our God is indeed a consuming fire.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In yesterday's devotional (Hebrews 12:1–13) the author encouraged us to lift up our drooping hands and weakened knees and keep running on the Lord's straight path. Now, in Hebrews 12:14–29, the exhortations of the whole letter reach their great rhetorical and visual climax. Here we also find what is often counted as the fifth and last warning passage of the letter (verses 25–29).

The author begins with a pastoral call to watch over one another and points to the tragic example of Esau (verses 14–17). Then, in two breathtaking pictures, he sets the two covenants side by side as two mountains: the terrifying, unapproachable mountain Sinai (verses 18–21) and the joyful, accessible mountain Zion (verses 22–24). Finally he speaks of God's last shaking of the whole cosmos and of receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken (verses 25–29).

Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into the riches of the Greek terms and the theological structure of this passage:

  • Peace, holiness and the danger of Esau's short-sightedness (episkopountes and bebēlos – ἐπισκοποῦντες, βέβηλος, vv. 14–17): The author calls us actively to "pursue" two things (diōkete – διώκετε, like a hunter or an athlete chasing a goal with full effort): peace with one another (eirēnē – εἰρήνη) and practical holiness (hagiasmos – ἁγιασμός). "Pursue peace with everyone, and holiness, without which no one will see the Lord." Without holiness no one will see the Lord – not because we are saved by our own works, but because a life cleansed by the blood of Christ (10:10) is inevitably renewed by his Spirit to fit his holy presence. Further, believers are to "see to it" (episkopountes – related to the word "bishop" or "overseer": we are all to be one another's spiritual overseers!) and guard against three dangers within the church:
    • "that no one falls short of the grace of God" (hysterōn – ὑστερῶν: falling behind on the training track and dropping out).
    • that no "root of bitterness" (rhiza pikrias – ῥίζα πικρίας, a quotation from Deuteronomy 29:18 about someone whose heart turns away from the Lord and who nurses a poisonous, rebellious attitude) springs up, infects the whole community of faith and "defiles" it (miainō – μιαίνω).
    • that no one is "godless" (bebēlos – βέβηλος) like Esau. The Greek word bebēlos literally means something trodden on at the threshold – someone who treats everything holy and spiritual as flat, cheap and ordinary. Esau was completely taken up with his immediate physical hunger; "for a single meal" – a bowl of lentil stew – he threw away his precious covenant birthright (Genesis 25:29–34). When he later wanted to receive the material blessing from Isaac (Genesis 27:34–38), he wept bitterly, but he could not undo what was done: "he found no place for repentance" (metanoias topon – μετανοίας τόπον). His tears were only regret over his loss, not true repentance towards God.
  • The first mountain: the terror and distance of Sinai (psēlaphōmenō – ψηλαφωμένῳ, vv. 18–21): Why would it be so foolish for the Jewish Christians, like Esau, to give up their spiritual inheritance and return to the old Mosaic law? Because of what the old covenant looked like at Sinai (Exodus 19–20; Deuteronomy 4:11): "For you have not approached something that could be touched" (psēlaphōmenō – something physical and earthly that a person could take hold of). The Greek text does not even use the word "mountain", but verse 20 makes it clear: this is Sinai, wrapped in fire, darkness, gloom and storm. The whole atmosphere at Sinai shouted a single message: Keep away! Even a stray animal that crossed the boundary had to be stoned (Exodus 19:13 adds: or shot with arrows), so that no one had to touch the offender. The people begged that God would stop speaking (Exodus 20:19), and even Moses the mediator cried out: "I am full of fear and trembling" (ekphobos eimi kai entromos – ἔκφοβός εἰμι καὶ ἔντρομος; the first words come from Deuteronomy 9:19, where Moses trembles before God's anger after the golden calf). Sinai could reveal God's spotless holiness, but it could not bring a single sinner near.
  • The second mountain: the festal fellowship of Zion (panēgyris and prōtotokōn – πανήγυρις, πρωτοτόκων, vv. 22–24): Against that dark scene of fear, verse 22 breaks out in triumphant contrast: "But you have approached Mount Zion" (proselēlythate – προσεληλύθατε, perfect tense: you are already standing there!). By faith we already stand at the foot of the true, heavenly reality! And look at the seven wonderful realities that surround us at Zion:
    • Mount Zion, the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem: the eternal city with foundations that Abraham was looking forward to (11:10).
    • Myriads of angels, the festal gathering (panēgyris): The Greek word panēgyris refers to a great national festival or celebration (as at the Olympic Games, when the whole city danced and cheered). Where the angels at Sinai surrounded the awe-inspiring giving of the law (cf. 2:2), at Zion they celebrate the redemption of sinners!
    • The assembly of the firstborn (ekklēsia prōtotokōn – ἐκκλησία πρωτοτόκων): Where Esau sold his birthright (v. 16), every single believer in Christ counts as a "firstborn" with full rights of inheritance, "enrolled in the heavens" – their name permanently written in heaven's register of citizens!
    • God, the judge of all: At Sinai it was deadly to come near the Judge, but at Zion we may come right up to the Judge of the universe, because our Mediator has settled our case.
    • The spirits of the righteous who have been made perfect: all the heroes of faith in chapter 11 who, through Christ's work, have already reached their perfected state in heaven (cf. 11:40).
    • Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant: Where Moses trembled with fear at Sinai (v. 21), Jesus stands at Zion as our fearless, loving Mediator.
    • The blood of sprinkling that speaks better than Abel: In Genesis 4:10 the blood of the murdered Abel cried out to God from the ground for vengeance and just punishment on guilty Cain. But the blood of Jesus, shed on the cross, speaks of infinitely better things: from the heavenly altar it cries out for grace, pardon and peace for the guilty sinner!
  • The last shaking and the kingdom that cannot be shaken (saleuō, basileian asaleuton and echōmen charin – σαλεύω, βασιλείαν ἀσάλευτον, ἔχωμεν χάριν, vv. 25–29): Now comes the last warning in verse 25: "See that you do not refuse the one who is speaking" (paraiteomai – παραιτέομαι: making excuses so as not to listen). The same God who spoke at Sinai speaks to us today from heaven, through the blood of his Son. The author quotes Haggai 2:6: at Sinai God's voice shook the earth, but he has promised: "Once more I will shake not only the earth but also heaven" (v. 26). Why does God shake creation (and sometimes our earthly securities too)? Verse 27 gives the answer: "so that what is not shaken may remain" (ta mē saleuomena – τὰ μὴ σαλευόμενα)! Everything that belongs to this temporary, created world – every human empire, every earthly system and every false prop – will be shaken and cleared away, so that only God's eternal creation remains. But for us as believers this does not end in panic! Look at the majestic conclusion in verses 28–29: "Therefore, since we are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, let us be thankful, and so worship God in a way that pleases him, with reverence and awe; for our God is indeed a consuming fire." Echōmen charin means "let us be thankful", but it can also be read as a call to hold on to grace; and the two words for our attitude are reverence (eulabeia – εὐλάβεια) and awe (deos – δέος). The closing words come from Deuteronomy 4:24. The God of Zion is no different from the God of Sinai; his holiness is still a consuming fire that burns up all sin and impurity. But because we are secure in Christ's unshakeable kingdom, our slavish fear is turned into deep, grateful reverence!

Core Theological Truths

  1. Holiness is not the ground of our salvation but its fruit. A life cleansed by the blood of Christ is renewed by his Spirit to fit his holy presence.
  2. We already stand at Zion. By faith we have not approached the fear and distance of Sinai, but the festal city of the living God – as firstborn whose names are enrolled in heaven.
  3. The blood of Jesus speaks better than Abel. Where Abel's blood cried out for retribution, the blood of Jesus cries out for grace, pardon and peace.
  4. God shakes everything created so that only the unshakeable remains. The God of Zion is the same consuming fire as the God of Sinai, but in Christ our fear becomes grateful reverence.

Living It Out

Hebrews 12:14–29 shakes us awake from spiritual slumber and sets our feet on a Rock that can never move.

First, look at the danger of Esau and the root of bitterness in everyday life (vv. 14–17). A root of bitterness always starts underground and unseen – an unforgiven grievance, a disappointment with people or circumstances that we nurse in our hearts until it spreads poison through our words and attitudes into our home or church. And right beside it lies Esau's trap: the temptation to sacrifice your eternal calling and spiritual peace for a moment's "lentil stew" – a quick fleshly gratification, a compromise for financial gain or a brief escape. Do not trade your "birthright" in Christ for a bowl of passing soup!

Second, look at the voice of Jesus' blood in your conscience (v. 24). Every time you stumble, your own guilty conscience and the accuser cry out like Abel's blood: Guilty! You deserve judgement! But at Mount Zion there is a voice that speaks louder and with greater authority in the heavenly throne room than all your failures: the sprinkled blood of Jesus. His blood cries out over your life today: Paid! Redeemed! Welcome to the Father! Listen today to the better voice of the blood of Golgotha.

Third, look at our anchor when the world around us shakes (vv. 26–29). We live in a time when economies, political systems, social norms and human securities keep shaking before our eyes. When things tremble around us, we as children of the living God need not panic. Every shaking in this world only reminds us that earthly things are temporary and that we are citizens of a kingdom that cannot be shaken (basileia asaleutos). And how does a citizen of such a kingdom live? Not with shallow frivolity, nor with slavish anxiety, but with grateful worship and holy reverence, because our King is a consuming fire of pure love and holiness!

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Hidden roots and lentil stew (vv. 15–16): Is there a hidden "root of bitterness" or unforgiveness in my heart at the moment that needs to be pulled up? And what "bowl of soup" of passing comfort sometimes tempts me to neglect my eternal priorities?
  • Sinai or Zion (vv. 18–24)? Do I still approach God in my prayer life with the slavish fear and distance of Sinai – or do I realise that through the blood of Jesus I have already approached the festive city of Zion as a beloved "firstborn"?
  • What shakes, and what stands firm (vv. 27–28)? Which shakeable things in my life or my surroundings are being shaken at the moment, and how does this help me anchor my heart more deeply in the kingdom that cannot be shaken, which I have received in Christ?

Prayer

Holy and living God, judge of all and consuming fire of spotless purity, I bow today before your throne with deep reverence and holy awe. I praise you that by the grace of your Son I need not tremble before the darkness and judgement of Mount Sinai, but have already been welcomed at the heavenly Mount Zion, the city of the living God!

Lord Jesus, mediator of the new covenant, thank you for your precious blood of sprinkling, which today in heaven speaks of infinitely better things than the blood of Abel. Where my sin cried out for judgement, your blood pleads today for my complete forgiveness, for peace and for my acceptance as a firstborn in your family.

Search my heart today by your Spirit: pull up every root of bitterness before it springs up, and keep me from the folly of Esau, who traded eternal treasures for passing pleasure. Help me to pursue peace with everyone and to grow in the holiness that glorifies you. While everything in this created world shakes and trembles around us, I thank you that in you I have received a kingdom that cannot be shaken. I choose to worship you today with a thankful heart, with reverence and awe. Amen.
