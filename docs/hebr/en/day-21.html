Eyes Fixed on Jesus · Day 21 / 28

Endurance in Faith: Do Not Throw Away Your Confidence

Scripture Hebrews 10:26–39

26For if we sin deliberately after we have received the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, 27but only a fearful expectation of judgment and the fury of a fire that will consume the adversaries. 28Anyone who has set aside the law of Moses dies without pity on the testimony of two or three witnesses. 29How much worse punishment, do you think, will be deserved by the one who has trampled on the Son of God, has regarded as unclean the blood of the covenant by which he was made holy, and has insulted the Spirit of grace? 30For we know the one who said:

Vengeance is mine; I will repay.And again:The Lord will judge his people.

31To fall into the hands of the living God is a fearful thing.

32But remember the earlier days, when, after you had been enlightened, you endured a hard struggle with sufferings, 33sometimes being made a public spectacle through reproaches and afflictions, and sometimes standing in fellowship with those who were treated in this way. 34For you sympathized with the prisoners, and you accepted the seizure of your possessions with joy, knowing that you yourselves have a better possession, one that lasts. 35So do not throw away your confidence, which has a great reward. 36For you need endurance, so that when you have done the will of God you may receive the promise.

37For yet a very little while,and the one who is coming will come and will not delay;38but my righteous one will live by faith,and if he draws back,my soul takes no pleasure in him.

39But we are not of those who draw back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

With Hebrews 10:26–39 we complete our journey through the central theological section of the letter. As in chapter 6, an awe-inspiring warning (vv. 26–31) is followed by a warm, encouraging call to endurance and trust (vv. 32–39). Here we enter the fourth great warning passage of Hebrews.

Yesterday, in vv. 19–25, the author showed how the way through the curtain has been opened and how we are now to hold on in faith, hope and love together with other believers. The word "For" (gar – γάρ) at the start of v. 26 ties the warning directly to v. 25: deliberately abandoning the meetings of the church and turning back to the old order was the first visible step towards a final rejection of Christ. Yet the chapter ends on a triumphant note that builds the bridge to the famous faith chapter, Hebrews 11: "But we are not of those who draw back to destruction, but of those who have faith to the preserving of the soul."

When the readers had just come to faith, they went through hard days (vv. 32–34). This may have been at the time when the emperor Claudius had Jews – and Jewish Christians with them – expelled from Rome, around AD 49. Back then they bore reproach, affliction and the loss of their possessions with joy.

Insights from the Greek

Let us look at the original Greek terms and the theological structure of this passage:

  1. Deliberate sin and the one sacrifice (hekousiōs – ἑκουσίως and epignōsis – ἐπίγνωσις, vv. 26–27): The author warns: "For if we sin deliberately after we have received the knowledge of the truth …" The background is Numbers 15:22–31, where sins committed in ignorance are set against sin committed "with a high hand" (Numbers 15:30). This is not about a believer who stumbles into temptation through human weakness and returns to the Lord in repentance (see Hebrews 4:15–16). The word hekousiōs stands at the very start of the Greek sentence for emphasis, and the verb for sinning (hamartanontōn – ἁμαρτανόντων) is a present participle: if we go on sinning, persistently and deliberately. In the context of Hebrews this refers specifically to the sin of apostasy (falling away): having received the full knowledge (epignōsis) of Christ's once-for-all sacrifice, deliberately turning one's back on him. If someone rejects the one, final Redeemer of sinners, there is no other sacrifice left that can take sins away – only the just judgment of God that will strike his "adversaries" (hypenantious – ὑπεναντίους; see Isaiah 26:11).

  2. The threefold contempt for the grace of the triune God (katapatēsas – καταπατήσας, koinon – κοινόν and enybrisas – ἐνυβρίσας, vv. 28–31): Again the author uses the qal wahomer argument (from the lesser to the greater): under the law of Moses (Deuteronomy 17:2–6), someone who had deliberately rejected the covenant to serve idols died without mercy on the testimony of two or three witnesses. How much more serious, then, to reject the new covenant! Verse 29 describes the horror of such a rejection by how it treats the Father's Son, the blood of the cross and the Holy Spirit: - Trampled on the Son of God (katapatēsas): walking over Jesus as though he were worthless, like mud on a road. - Regarded the blood of the covenant as unclean (koinon hēgēsamenos – κοινὸν ἡγησάμενος): the word koinos means "common, ordinary, unholy". It is to claim that Jesus' precious blood on the cross was just the ordinary blood of a human offender, with no atoning power. - Insulted the Spirit of grace (enybrisas): the word is related to hybris – slapping in the face, with arrogant contempt, the very Holy Spirit who holds out grace to you.

The author quotes Deuteronomy 32:35–36 and concludes in v. 31: "To fall into the hands of the living God is a fearful thing."

  1. Memory as an anchor: remember the earlier days (anamimnēskesthe – ἀναμιμνῄσκεσθε and theatrizomenoi – θεατριζόμενοι, vv. 32–34): As in Hebrews 6:9, the author's tone suddenly changes in v. 32 from thunder to pastoral encouragement. He calls the church to think back (anamimnēskesthe – call it vividly to mind) to "the earlier days" just after they had come to faith. Back then they "endured a hard struggle with sufferings" (athlēsis – ἄθλησις, related to our word "athletics": a gruelling contest or fight in the arena): - They were made a public spectacle through reproach and affliction (theatrizomenoi – from which we get "theatre": being put on display on a stage to be mocked). - They stood in fellowship (koinōnoi – κοινωνοί) with prisoners who suffered for Christ. In a world where prisoners depended entirely on friends for food and clothing, it was life-threatening to be identified with a Christian prisoner – yet they did not run away! - And when their own homes and possessions were plundered and confiscated, they "accepted the seizure of your possessions with joy" (meta charas – μετὰ χαρᾶς)! How is it humanly possible to be glad when you lose your property? Verse 34 gives the secret: "knowing that you yourselves have a better (kreittona – κρείττονα) possession, one that lasts" (menousan – μένουσαν). (Many older translations add "in heaven" here; the Greek base text we follow does not have it, but that is the possession the author means.)

  2. Do not throw away your confidence (hypomonē – ὑπομονή and hypostolē – ὑποστολή, vv. 35–39): If they stood so bravely back then, why give up now? So v. 35 says: "So do not throw away your confidence" (parrēsian – παρρησίαν; mē apobalēte – μὴ ἀποβάλητε). In ancient warfare the greatest disgrace for a soldier was to throw away (apoballō) his shield on the battlefield and flee. Our parrēsia (our unashamed trust in Christ) "has a great reward" (misthapodosian – μισθαποδοσίαν). What they need now is endurance (hypomonē – staying on your feet under a heavy load and holding out to the end). To encourage them, the author quotes in vv. 37–38 the well-known prophecy of Habakkuk 2:3–4, introduced by a phrase from Isaiah 26:20: "For yet a very little while" (mikron hoson hoson – μικρὸν ὅσον ὅσον): - In Habakkuk the text is about the vision that will be fulfilled at the appointed time; in Hebrews it becomes a person: "the one who is coming (ho erchomenos – ὁ ἐρχόμενος, a messianic title for Jesus!) will come and will not delay". - And while we wait for his coming, how do we survive? "But my righteous one will live by faith." The chapter closes in v. 39 with a powerful declaration of faith in which the author includes himself and his readers: "But we are not of those who draw back (hypostolēs – ὑποστολῆς: pulling back like a coward and slinking away) to destruction, but of those who have faith (pisteōs – πίστεως) to the preserving (peripoiēsin – περιποίησιν: gaining, keeping safe) of the soul."

Core Theological Truths

  1. There is only one sacrifice for sins. Whoever deliberately rejects Christ's once-for-all sacrifice has no other sacrifice left – only the just judgment of God.
  2. The hands of the living God. For the believer who takes refuge in Christ, the hands of the living God are the safest place in the universe (John 10:28–29); but for the one who tramples on Christ's blood, those same hands are a consuming judgment.
  3. Warning and encouragement belong together. As in chapter 6, the thunder of vv. 26–31 is followed by the pastoral encouragement of vv. 32–39.
  4. Endurance lives from the promise of his coming. The one who is coming will come and will not delay. Meanwhile the righteous one lives by faith and does not draw back.

Living It Out

Hebrews 10:26–39 speaks directly to the believer who, in a long, draining season of life, feels that their spiritual strength is running out.

Look first at the practical power of holy remembering (vv. 32–34). When a new crisis or disappointment stares you in the face today, fear often whispers that you won't make it this time. The author's advice is: Look back at your "earlier days"! Remember the earlier storms in which the Lord carried you. Remember the times when your faith was still young and fervent and you were ready to stake everything on Jesus. The same God who carried you through the earlier struggles (athlēsis) is still with you today.

Look secondly at the liberating perspective on earthly possessions and your eternal possession (v. 34). Our modern culture teaches us to tie our security and joy to financial security, property and comfort. When those things are threatened, we panic or grow bitter. The early Christians could face the loss of their earthly goods with joy – not because they enjoyed pain, but because their accounting was right: they knew that they had a better possession, one that lasts, with God, which no thief, inflation or persecutor can ever touch. The looser our grip on temporary things, the firmer our joy in Christ.

Look thirdly at the call in vv. 35–39: Do not throw down your shield of confidence on the battlefield just before the King arrives! It is often in the last few kilometres of a marathon – when the muscles burn and the finish line is still hidden around the bend – that a runner is tempted to sit down. But v. 37 assures you: "For yet a very little while, and the one who is coming will come"! We do not belong to the camp of those who draw back (hypostolē); by his grace we are people of faith (pistis) who keep walking forward to the finish line!

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Remember the earlier days: As you look back over your journey with the Lord, which particular hard struggle or trial from your past, through which God carried you, can give you fresh courage today for your present battle?
  • The test of earthly loss (v. 34): How does your heart react when you lose earthly comfort, money or people's approval? Does your reaction show that you really believe you have in Christ a better possession, one that lasts?
  • Holding on to your confidence (parrēsia): Where in your life are you tempted right now to throw down your spiritual shield and draw back out of weariness? What does it mean for you to declare afresh today: "my righteous one will live by faith"?

Prayer

Living and holy God, with deep reverence I bow before your majesty. Your word reminds me today how serious and how precious the sacrifice of your Son is. Keep my heart from ever regarding the precious blood of the covenant, by which I was made holy, as something ordinary or to be taken for granted, or from grieving the Spirit of grace through unbelief.

Lord Jesus, thank you that in my past you have already carried me through so many storms and struggles. When I grow tired on the road today, remind me of your faithfulness in the earlier days. Open my eyes to the imperishable treasure I have in you – a better possession, one that lasts, which no earthly loss can ever take from me.

Today I choose not to throw away my confidence and my shield of faith. Give me by your Spirit the endurance and staying power to keep doing your will while I look forward to your coming. Yet a very little while, and you, the one who is coming, will come and will not delay! Thank you that by your grace I do not belong to those who draw back to destruction, but to those who have faith to the preserving of the soul. Amen.
