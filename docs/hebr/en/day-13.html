Eyes Fixed on Jesus · Day 13 / 28

The Order of Melchizedek: King of Righteousness and King of Peace

Scripture Hebrews 7:1–10

1For this Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of God Most High, met Abraham as he was returning from the defeat of the kings and blessed him, 2and Abraham gave him a tenth of everything. His name means first "king of righteousness", and then he is also king of Salem, that is, "king of peace". 3Without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but made like the Son of God, he remains a priest for all time.

4See how great he is, to whom Abraham the patriarch gave a tenth of the best of the spoils. 5Those of the sons of Levi who receive the priestly office have a commandment in the law to collect a tenth from the people, that is, from their brothers and sisters, though these too have come from Abraham's body. 6But this man, who does not trace his descent from them, collected a tenth from Abraham and blessed the one who had the promises. 7Beyond all dispute, the lesser is blessed by the better. 8Here, mortal men receive tithes; there, one of whom it is testified that he lives. 9And one might even say that Levi himself, who receives tithes, paid a tenth through Abraham, 10for he was still in his ancestor's body when Melchizedek met him.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

At the end of chapter 6 the author assured us that the anchor of our soul reaches safe and firm right behind the heavenly curtain, where Jesus has entered as our forerunner (prodromos – πρόδρομος), "having become high priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek" (6:20). Already in Hebrews 5:6 and 5:10 the author wanted to speak about Melchizedek, but first he had to deal with his readers' spiritual sluggishness. Now, in chapter 7, he throws the treasure chest wide open.

For first-century Jewish believers, the confession that Jesus is our high priest raised one big and fair question: How can Jesus be a lawful priest if he was born of the tribe of Judah? Under the law of Moses only direct male descendants of Levi and Aaron could hold the priestly office. To answer this question, the author takes his readers back to the only two places in the Old Testament where the mysterious figure of Melchizedek appears: the historical meeting with Abraham in Genesis 14:18–20 (some four centuries before the law of Moses was given!) and David's messianic prophecy in Psalm 110:4 (centuries after Moses!). With brilliant rabbinic exegesis he shows that from the very beginning there was a priestly order far older, higher and more exalted than that of Levi.

After Abraham had defeated the coalition of four eastern kings to rescue his nephew Lot (Genesis 14), Melchizedek met him in the King's Valley. Melchizedek was a historical Canaanite ruler of the ancient city of Salem (usually linked with later Jerusalem, see Psalm 76:2) who served the one true God, El Elyon ("God Most High"). Under the old covenant Israel had a strict separation of powers: the kings came from Judah and the priests from Levi. No king was ever allowed to enter the priestly office. But in Melchizedek – and finally and perfectly in Jesus Christ (see Zechariah 6:13) – kingship and priesthood are united in one person!

For a Levitical priest his genealogy was everything: if he could not prove his descent back to Aaron, he was at once barred from the priesthood (see Ezra 2:62). Genesis is the book of genealogies (toledot). Every important figure is introduced by who his ancestors were, when he was born and when he died. But when Melchizedek steps onto the stage in Genesis 14, the Holy Spirit deliberately gives no family tree, no date of birth and no date of death!

Insights from the Greek

  • King of righteousness and king of peace (basileus dikaiosynēs – βασιλεὺς δικαιοσύνης; basileus eirēnēs – βασιλεὺς εἰρήνης, vv. 1–2): In verse 2 the author shows how Melchizedek's name and title form a prophetic shadow of Christ. First, his Hebrew name (Malki-Tsedek) literally means "king of righteousness". Second, he was king of Salem (related to the Hebrew word shalom), "that is, 'king of peace'". And notice the divine order: first king of righteousness, and then king of peace. True peace with God is never a cheap compromise; it always rests on the foundation of righteousness fulfilled (see Isaiah 32:17; Romans 5:1).
  • Without genealogy (apatōr, amētōr, agenealogētos – ἀπάτωρ, ἀμήτωρ, ἀγενεαλόγητος, v. 3): At first sight verse 3 sounds puzzling: "Without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life". Does this mean Melchizedek was an angel, or an appearance of Christ before his incarnation? No – the key lies in the rare Greek word agenealogētos ("without family tree, without genealogy") and in the way Genesis is written: Melchizedek's parents, birth and death are simply not recorded.
  • Made like the Son of God (aphōmoiōmenos – ἀφωμοιωμένος, v. 3): Through this inspired "silence of Scripture", Melchizedek in the biblical text is "made like the Son of God" (aphōmoiōmenos – made like a portrait or facsimile of). Melchizedek is not the original; the eternal Son of God is the Original, and Melchizedek's appearance in Genesis is like a portrait painted in advance, designed so that in the witness of Scripture he "remains a priest for all time" (eis to diēnekes – εἰς τὸ διηνεκές, without interruption).
  • "See" (theōreite – θεωρεῖτε) and the patriarch (ho patriarchēs – ὁ πατριάρχης, v. 4): The author calls on his Jewish readers: "See how great he is" – theōreite means to look with attention and wonder. He respectfully calls Abraham "the patriarch" (patriarchēs), a word that in the Greek stands at the very end of the sentence for maximum emphasis: literally something like: … to whom Abraham gave a tenth – the patriarch!
  • The best of the spoils (ek tōn akrothiniōn – ἐκ τῶν ἀκροθινίων, v. 4): Abraham gave Melchizedek "a tenth of the best of the spoils". In the ancient Greek world akrothinia referred to the very top of a heap of grain or of the spoils of war, set apart as a thank-offering for the king or the deity.
  • The better (tou kreittonos – τοῦ κρείττονος, v. 7): The author draws a sharp contrast. The Levitical priests did collect tithes under the law from their fellow Israelites ("that is, from their brothers and sisters") as payment for their service, even though as descendants of Abraham they stood on an equal footing (v. 5). But Melchizedek, who was no part of Levi's family tree at all, received a tenth from Abraham himself and "blessed the one who had the promises" (v. 6). Then comes the irrefutable principle of verse 7: "Beyond all dispute, the lesser is blessed by the better." Kreittōn is the word that everywhere else in Hebrews means "better".
  • Mortal men (apothnēskontes anthrōpoi – ἀποθνῄσκοντες ἄνθρωποι, v. 8): "Here, mortal men receive tithes; there, one of whom it is testified that he lives." Literally they are men who are dying. With Melchizedek the tenth is received by one of whom Scripture testifies that he lives, because it portrays him without end of days – a pointer to the risen Christ.

Core Theological Truths

  1. Christ is both King and Priest. What was strictly separated under the old covenant is foreshadowed in Melchizedek and perfectly united in Jesus Christ. He is first King of righteousness, and then King of peace.
  2. Scripture itself points ahead to Christ. Even the "silence" of Genesis about Melchizedek's origins is part of God's plan to paint a portrait of the eternal Son, whose priesthood does not rest on descent.
  3. Melchizedek's priesthood is higher than Levi's. When Abraham bowed his head before Melchizedek to receive his blessing, the father of the whole Jewish nation acknowledged that Melchizedek's priesthood was higher than himself.
  4. Corporate solidarity. In verses 9–10 the author delivers his masterful final stroke: when Abraham handed over a tenth to Melchizedek that day in the King's Valley, his great-grandson Levi (and the whole later Aaronic priesthood) was "still in his ancestor's body". Through Abraham's hand Levi himself, as it were, paid a tenth to Melchizedek and bowed before a greater priesthood that would one day be fulfilled in Jesus Christ.

Living It Out

At first glance Hebrews 7:1–10 looks like a deep theological argument for first-century Jews, but its spiritual pulse touches every single day of our modern lives.

First, the order of Melchizedek's titles (v. 2) teaches us an indispensable lesson about how God works in our hearts: he is first King of righteousness, and then King of peace. How often do we look for inner peace (Salem) in our lives, but want it without God's righteousness touching our hidden idols, our unconfessed sin or our broken relationships? We look for a psychological plaster while the wound is still dirty. But at Golgotha our true Malki-Tsedek first satisfied the full claim of God's righteousness on the cross, so that he could give us genuine, unshakeable peace. When you feel unrest in your heart today, don't try to fake peace by sweeping things under the carpet; bring them into the light before the King of righteousness, and his peace will follow like a river.

Second, verse 3 frees us from the limits of our own "genealogy" and past. In the world (and in legalistic religion) your worth is measured by your family tree, your background, your qualifications or your achievements. But in the kingdom of Christ only his eternal priesthood counts. Whether you come from a broken background or feel that your spiritual "CV" is full of mistakes – your high priest does not hand out his blessing on the basis of your pedigree, but out of free grace.

Third, Abraham's response in verse 4 calls us to the grateful surrender of our "best of the spoils" (akrothinia). Notice that Abraham was not under the compulsion of the law of Moses when he gave his tenth to Melchizedek; the law would only come some four centuries later! Out of spontaneous wonder and gratitude after the victory he laid the very best part of his gain before the King-Priest, because he had seen Melchizedek's greatness ("See how great he is"). When we open our eyes today and see how great Jesus is – that he has rescued us from the slavery of sin and blesses us with every spiritual blessing – we no longer give our time, our gifts, our attention and our means out of guilt or legal compulsion. We give him the akrothinia – the very top, the best part of our day and our heart – because he is overwhelmingly great and worthy!

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Righteousness and peace: Is there an area of your life where you are lacking inner peace right now because you have not yet allowed Jesus, as the King of righteousness, to address that matter in truth?
  • The best of the spoils (akrothinia) or the leftovers? When you look at your time, your energy and your resources, does King Jesus get the very first and best part of the "heap" – or do you give him only what remains after the world and your own interests have taken their share?
  • A meeting after the battle: When Abraham came back from the war tired and vulnerable, Melchizedek met him with bread, wine and blessing (Genesis 14:18–19; Hebrews 7:1). Do you let your high priest refresh and bless you after your daily battles and exhaustion – or do you try to carry on under your own steam?

Prayer

God Most High, Maker of heaven and earth, I bow before you today in deep wonder. I thank you that through the ages you have unfolded your perfect plan of redemption and have given me a high priest infinitely greater than any human or earthly institution.

Lord Jesus, I worship you today as my true Melchizedek! You are my King of righteousness, who through your spotless life and your death on the cross paid my debt in full and clothed me with your own righteousness. And you are my King of Salem, who makes peace between me and the Father and stills the storms within me. You have no beginning of days and no end of life, and your priesthood stands firm for ever and ever.

Open my spiritual eyes today to see afresh how great and glorious you are. Just as Abraham bowed before Melchizedek, I bow all that I am and have before you today. I do not want to serve you out of cold, legalistic duty, but out of a heart overflowing with gratitude. Take today the first and best part of my time, my thoughts, my work and my love as a thank-offering to you, the living Son of God, who makes me rich with every heavenly blessing. Amen.
