Eyes Fixed on Jesus · Day 9 / 28

The Throne of Grace: Our Great High Priest Who Can Sympathize with Us

Scripture Hebrews 4:14 – 5:10

14Since, then, we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God, let us hold fast to the confession. 15For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who has been tested in every respect in the same way – yet without sin. 16Let us therefore approach the throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace as help at the right time.

1For every high priest is taken from among human beings and appointed on behalf of human beings in what is to be done before God, to offer gifts and sacrifices for sins. 2He is able to deal gently with those who are ignorant and going astray, since he himself is beset with weakness. 3Because of this he must offer sacrifices for sins for himself as well as for the people. 4And no one takes this honour for himself; he is called by God, just as Aaron was.

5So also Christ did not glorify himself to become high priest; it was the one who said to him:

You are my Son;today I have fathered you,

6as he also says in another place:

You are a priest for everaccording to the order of Melchizedek.

7In the days of his flesh he offered up prayers and pleas with loud cries and tears to the one who was able to save him out of death, and he was heard because of his reverence. 8Although he was a Son, he learned obedience through what he suffered; 9and having been made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him, 10addressed by God as high priest according to the order of Melchizedek.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In Hebrews 4:12–13 the author confronted us with the sharp, double-edged sword of God's word, before which all things are "naked and laid bare". It is easy to lose heart and shrink back in fear. How can a broken sinner ever stand before so all-knowing and holy a God? The author's answer in 4:14–5:10 is one of the most comforting passages in all of Scripture: the One before whose eyes we lie exposed is at the same time our great high priest, who intercedes for us.

For Jewish Christians the Levitical high priest was the centre of their worship. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), the high priest passed with fear and trembling through three curtains – from the outer court, through the Holy Place, into the Most Holy Place. In the tabernacle, which Hebrews describes, the ark of the covenant with its atonement cover (kapporet – כַּפֹּרֶת) stood there; in the Second Temple of the readers' own day that room was empty. But now the author shows that Jesus has done something infinitely greater.

Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into the richness of the Greek terms and the theological structure of this passage:

  • Our great high priest who has passed through the heavens (dielēluthota tous ouranous – διεληλυθότα τοὺς οὐρανούς, v. 14): The Old Testament speaks of "the high priest" or "the great priest" (e.g. Leviticus 21:10). The full title "great high priest" (archierea megan – ἀρχιερέα μέγαν) occurs in the New Testament only here, and the author gives it to Jesus. Aaron walked through earthly curtains of linen; Jesus "has passed through the heavens" – beyond the visible sky and the galaxies, right up to the throne of God himself. And notice how the author holds his two natures together in one breath: Jesus (his full humanity, which suffered alongside us) and the Son of God (his full deity, which gives his sacrifice infinite worth).
  • Sympathy without sin (sympatheō – συμπαθέω and chōris hamartias – χωρὶς ἁμαρτίας, v. 15): The author uses a double negative to drive his point home: "For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses." The verb sympatheō (from which we get "sympathy") is much stronger in Greek: literally to suffer with someone from the inside. He "has been tested in every respect in the same way" (pepeirasmenon – πεπειρασμένον), but with one decisive difference: "yet without sin" (chōris hamartias). Some people wrongly think that because Jesus never sinned, he does not really know how hard temptation is. The opposite is true! As C. S. Lewis rightly observed, someone who gives in to a temptation after five minutes does not know how strong it would have become an hour later. Because Jesus resisted every temptation to the very end without ever giving in, he felt its full, crushing hurricane force in a way we never fully will.
  • Confidence before the throne of grace (parrēsia – παρρησία and eukairon boētheian – εὔκαιρον βοήθειαν, v. 16): Because our high priest is already seated at the Father's right hand, God's judgement seat becomes for the believer a throne of grace (recalling the hilastērion – ἱλαστήριον, the atonement cover on the ark where the blood was sprinkled, 9:5). We may now approach "with confidence" (parrēsia – unashamed boldness and openness). There we receive two things: mercy (eleos – ἔλεος – forgiveness and compassion for the failures of our past) and grace (charis – χάρις – God's helping, strengthening power for our present need), "as help at the right time" (eukairon boētheian – at exactly the critical moment when we are about to give up).
  • What makes a true high priest (metriopathein – μετριοπαθεῖν, Hebrews 5:1–6): In chapter 5 the author sets out two requirements for any true high priest:
  • First, human solidarity and gentle compassion (5:1–3): An earthly high priest had to be "able to deal gently with those who are ignorant and going astray". The Greek metriopathein means to moderate one's emotions – not to flare up in anger at those who stumble, nor to walk away from them coldly and indifferently, but to treat them with gentle forbearance. Earthly priests, however, also had to offer sacrifices for their own sins; Christ had no sin of his own, yet showed perfect gentleness towards sinners.
  • Second, a divine appointment (5:4–6): No one in Israel could make himself high priest (think of King Uzziah, struck with leprosy when he took on himself the priestly task of burning incense in the temple, 2 Chronicles 26). In the same way Jesus did not seize the office. The Father himself appointed him, and the author joins two Old Testament sayings: Psalm 2:7 ("You are my Son; today I have fathered you") and Psalm 110:4 ("You are a priest for ever according to the order of Melchizedek"). In Israel kingship (from Judah) and priesthood (from Levi) were strictly separate – but in Christ King and Priest are united for ever!
  • The school of Gethsemane (eulabeia – εὐλάβεια and emathen … tēn hypakoēn – ἔμαθεν … τὴν ὑπακοήν, Hebrews 5:7–10): Verses 7 to 9 take us back to the dark night in the garden of Gethsemane and to the cross ("in the days of his flesh"). We see the eternal Son praying to the Father "with loud cries and tears". The text says "he was heard because of his reverence" (eulabeia – his deep, reverent godly fear and surrender to the Father's will; some read it as "heard and set free from his anguish"). God did not save him from dying on the cross, but brought him through death, "out of death", in the resurrection! Verse 8 contains a brilliant Greek wordplay: emathen aph' hōn epathen (ἔμαθεν ἀφʼ ὧν ἔπαθεν) – "he learned (emathen) obedience through what he suffered (epathen)" As the eternal Son, Jesus was never disobedient; he did not need to learn to stop rebelling as we do. But in becoming human he learned by experience what it costs to remain obedient to the Father as a human being in a broken world, when obedience costs you everything. Through this ordeal he was "made perfect" (teleiōtheis – τελειωθείς – fully qualified as mediator) and became the aitios (αἴτιος – cause, source) "of eternal salvation for all who obey him".

Core Theological Truths

  1. The holy Judge is also our sympathetic high priest. The One before whose eyes all things are naked and laid bare (4:13) also intercedes for us. We need not flee from his holiness, for our high priest has passed through the heavens.
  2. Jesus is truly God and truly human. "Jesus, the Son of God" – his humanity makes him one with us; his deity gives his sacrifice infinite worth.
  3. Sinlessness does not mean ignorance of temptation. Because Jesus never gave in, he bore the full force of temptation – and so he can truly sympathize with our weaknesses.
  4. Christ was appointed by God as King and Priest. He did not take the honour for himself; the Father appointed him, according to Psalm 2 and Psalm 110, as Son and eternal priest according to the order of Melchizedek.
  5. Obedience is learned in suffering. The Son learned obedience through what he suffered, and he is the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him.

Living It Out

When we stumble or go through a spiritual drought, our human hearts nearly always react in one of two wrong ways: either we run away from God because we think he sits frowning on a throne of condemnation, or we try to "clean ourselves up" first and behave well for a few days before we dare to pray again.

Hebrews 4:14–5:10 smashes this religious lie to pieces. Your high priest in heaven is not an impatient judge shaking his head in disgust at your weakness. He has sympatheia and metriopatheia – a deep, gentle, personal compassion. Why? Because he knows the salty taste of tears in Gethsemane. He knows what it is like when body and soul tremble under pressure and you have to cry out to heaven for help.

Notice when verse 16 invites us to approach the throne of grace with confidence: not when we feel strong and have everything under control, but precisely when we need mercy (for our failures) and grace (for our weakness)! You do not have to wait until you have dried your tears before you come to him; you can walk straight into the Most Holy Place, tears and all.

At the same time, verse 8 teaches us a precious lesson about our own trials. If even the sinless Son of God had to learn obedience through what he suffered, why do we think we can grow to spiritual maturity without hardship? Your present suffering or challenge is not a sign that God has forgotten you. It is the classroom in which the Spirit is shaping you to learn, like Jesus, deeper obedience and trust.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • What does God's throne look like in your imagination? When you become aware of your shortcomings or temptations, do you see God's throne as a judge's bench to hide from, or as a throne of grace you can run to with confidence (parrēsia)?
  • The honesty of Gethsemane: In his distress Jesus prayed "with loud cries and tears" (5:7). Do you still pray only neat, polite prayers that hide your real fears, or do you dare to pour out your heart raw and honest before the Father?
  • The classroom of suffering: Which difficult situation or "suffering" in your life right now is God using to teach you a deeper level of obedience and surrender (emathen aph' hōn epathen)?

Prayer

Merciful Father, with deep gratitude and wonder I approach your throne of grace with confidence today. I do not come on the basis of my own achievement or worthiness, but only because my great high priest, Jesus the Son of God, has already passed through the heavens and opened the way for me with his own blood.

Lord Jesus, thank you that you do not stand cold or indifferent towards my human frailty. You know the weight of temptation, you know the pressure of anguish in Gethsemane, and you know what it is like to cry out to the Father with tears. Thank you that you can fully sympathize with my weaknesses, and that you carry me with gentle patience when I go astray or fall short.

Today I ask for your mercy over my failures and your fresh grace for the challenges that lie ahead of me. Give me your help at exactly the right time. Teach me too, as you did, not to rebel against the hard seasons of life, but to learn obedience in every trial and to stay close to the Father's heart. Amen.
