The Will and the Blood: Offered Once to Bear the Sins of Many

Scripture Hebrews 9:15–28

15And for this reason he is mediator of a new covenant, so that, since a death has taken place for redemption from the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who are called may receive the promise of the eternal inheritance. 16For where there is a will, the death of the one who made it must be established. 17For a will takes effect only at death, since it is never in force while the one who made it is alive. 18That is why not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood. 19For when every commandment had been spoken by Moses to all the people according to the law, he took the blood of the calves with water and scarlet wool and hyssop, and sprinkled both the book itself and all the people, 20saying: "This is the blood of the covenant that God has commanded for you." 21In the same way he sprinkled with the blood the tent and all the vessels used in the ministry. 22Indeed, according to the law almost everything is cleansed with blood, and without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness.

23So it was necessary for the copies of the things in the heavens to be cleansed with these, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these. 24For Christ did not enter a sanctuary made with hands, a counterpart of the true one, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf. 25Nor did he enter to offer himself again and again, as the high priest enters the sanctuary year by year with blood that is not his own; 26for then he would have had to suffer again and again since the foundation of the world. But as it is, he has been revealed once at the end of the ages to set aside sin by his sacrifice. 27And just as it is in store for human beings to die once, and after that comes judgment, 28so Christ also, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, apart from sin, to those who eagerly wait for him, for salvation.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In yesterday's devotional (Hebrews 9:11–14) we saw how Christ's precious blood does something no animal sacrifice could ever do: it reaches into our innermost being and cleanses our conscience from dead works, so that we can freely worship the living God. But that still leaves the old, nagging question in the minds of the first-century Jewish readers: Why did the Messiah have to die at all? Could an almighty God not simply have declared by royal decree that our sins are forgiven, without the cruel scandal of a bloody cross?

In Hebrews 9:15–28 the author gives a masterly answer to this question. He explains the unshakeable divine necessity of Jesus' substitutionary death by weaving two images seamlessly together: the legal concept of a will or testament (vv. 15–17) and the Old Testament consecration with blood at Sinai (vv. 18–22). He then lets both lead into the three wonderful "appearings" of Christ in verses 24 to 28.

For the second image the author goes back to Exodus 24:3–8, when the first covenant was inaugurated at the foot of Mount Sinai. After Moses had read out the commandments, he took the blood of the sacrificial animals and sprinkled the altar and the people with it (Hebrews 9:19 adds the book itself), saying solemnly: "This is the blood of the covenant that God has commanded for you." These are almost the same words that Jesus applied to himself at the last Passover in the upper room: "This is my blood of the covenant" (Matthew 26:28; in Luke 22:20 he speaks of the new covenant in his blood)!

Why all this blood on the book, the people and the vessels of the tent? Because Leviticus 17:11 teaches that the life (nefesh) of the flesh is in the blood.

Insights from the Greek

Let us dig into the riches of the Greek terms:

  • "Covenant" and "will" (diathēkē – διαθήκη, vv. 15–17): In the Greek New Testament the same word, diathēkē, is used for both "covenant" and "will" (testament). That is why our translation renders it "will" in verses 16–17, and "covenant" before and after. While the Hebrew word for covenant (berit) refers to a solemn treaty between two parties, in everyday legal Greek a diathēkē was a last will and testament by which someone left his inheritance (klēronomia – κληρονομία) to his heirs. The author uses this play on words to powerful effect: because Jesus wanted to leave "the promise of the eternal inheritance" to "those who are called" (v. 15), the covenant also works like a will.
  • "The one who made it" … "established" (ho diathemenos – ὁ διαθέμενος; pheresthai – φέρεσθαι, vv. 16–17): As long as the one who made the will (ho diathemenos) is alive, the document is only a promise on paper; it comes into effect only when the death of the testator is "established". The verb pheresthai (from pherō, "bring, carry") means here that the death must be brought forward as evidence, that is, proven. For "a will takes effect only at death, since it is never in force while the one who made it is alive".
  • "Inaugurated" (enkainizō – ἐγκαινίζω, v. 18): "Not even the first covenant was inaugurated without blood": it was formally opened and ratified.
  • "Shedding of blood" and "forgiveness" (chōris haimatekchysias – χωρὶς αἱματεκχυσίας; aphesis – ἄφεσις, v. 22): Verse 22 sums up the whole Old Testament sacrificial system in one unbending spiritual law: "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness". Aphesis means release or cancellation of a debt.
  • "To set aside sin" (eis athetēsin hamartias – εἰς ἀθέτησιν ἁμαρτίας, v. 26): The word athetēsis (ἀθέτησις), which we also met in 7:18, means to annul something, declare it void, remove it from office.
  • Three words for "appear" (pephanerōtai – πεφανέρωται; emphanisthēnai – ἐμφανισθῆναι; ophthēsetai – ὀφθήσεται, vv. 24–28): Verses 24 to 28 paint the full panorama of salvation history with three Greek verbs for appearing or being revealed – past, present and future (see below). The word emphanizō (ἐμφανίζω) also has a legal ring: in Acts 24:1 and 25:2 it is used of those who bring a case before the governor.
  • "To bear" (anenenkein – ἀνενεγκεῖν, v. 28): Christ was offered once "to bear the sins of many" – an echo of Isaiah 53:12.
  • "Who eagerly wait for him" (apekdechomenois – ἀπεκδεχομένοις, v. 28): The verb means to wait for someone with outstretched longing.

Core Theological Truths

  1. A will requires a death. Christ did not merely write down an eternal inheritance for us, as it were; he himself died on the cross so that his will could come into effect immediately and irrevocably in our favour – and then he rose from the dead to be the executor of his own will!
  2. Without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness. Sin is not a small mistake that can be ignored with a smile; it is high treason against a holy God, and its wages are death. Forgiveness requires that a substitute life be laid down so that the guilty life can be acquitted.
  3. The heavenly things themselves were cleansed by a better sacrifice. In verse 23 the author says that "the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these" had to be cleansed. How can the perfect heaven need "cleansing"? Heaven itself is not dirty. But because sinful people are called to enter God's holy presence, our transgressions had to be wiped out before God's judgement seat, so that the heavenly throne room might be open to us.
  4. Christ's three "appearings" span past, present and future. - His appearing in the past – to set aside sin (v. 26): "But as it is, he has been revealed once at the end of the ages to set aside sin by his sacrifice." At the cross – the turning point and climax of world history – Jesus finally dethroned the power and guilt of sin. - His appearing in the present – before God's face for us (v. 24): Christ entered heaven itself, "now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf". The picture is that of an advocate who appears before the judge on behalf of his client. At this very moment our glorified High Priest stands with his pierced hands before the Father's face as our living Advocate! - His appearing in the future – for salvation (vv. 27–28): Just as every human being is destined to die once (hapax – ἅπαξ) and after that to face God's judgement, so Christ was offered once to take the sins of many upon himself. That is why he "will appear a second time, apart from sin, to those who eagerly wait for him, for salvation". At his first coming he carried our sin on his shoulders; at his return he does not come again to pay for sin, for that debt has been settled once and for all – he comes to lead those who long for him into their full, glorified redemption!

Living It Out

Hebrews 9:15–28 gives you an unshakeable threefold anchor for your past, your present and your future.

First, look at your past through the lens of the cross (vv. 15–22, 26). Perhaps you are troubled today by guilt over mistakes from your "early days", or you think your sin is too great to be truly forgiven. Hear the unbending justice and the grace of verses 22 and 26: God did not cheaply sweep your sin under the carpet where it could seep out again later. A real death took place! The eternal Son of God shed his blood so that your sin is legally declared void (athetēsis). And because the testator has died (vv. 16–17), no one can ever contest or cancel your inheritance as a child of God – it is sealed with his blood.

Second, look at your present through the lens of the heavenly throne room (v. 24). When you pray today, or when you stumble in your daily work and struggles and feel unworthy to come to God, remember what is happening in heaven now: Christ appears "now … in the presence of God on our behalf". You do not stand alone before the face of a holy God; your Advocate and High Priest appears there for you. When the Father looks at you, he sees his beloved Son interceding for you.

Third, look at your future through the lens of his return (vv. 27–28). For the world outside Christ, death and the judgement that follows (v. 27) are the greatest imaginable terror. But why do you as a believer not need to fear verse 27? Because verse 28 follows immediately: "just as … so Christ also"! Because Christ has already borne your "dying once" and your judgement on the cross, the future changes for you from a terrifying courtroom into a joyful expectation of your Bridegroom and King, who returns to complete your redemption!

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Are you living as an heir of the will? Do you realise that Jesus' death has irrevocably brought the will of God's grace into effect – or are you still living like a spiritual beggar who doubts whether the promises really belong to you?
  • The three "appearings" in your heart: Which of Christ's three appearings do you most need to remember today: that he has already been revealed to wipe out your guilt (past), that he now appears before the Father to intercede for you (present), or that he will appear again to make all things new (future)?
  • Eager expectation (apekdechomai – ἀπεκδέχομαι): Verse 28 describes believers simply as "those who eagerly wait for him". When you look at your daily priorities and conversations, are you living with a living, hopeful expectation of Jesus' return – or have you become too comfortable in this passing world?

Prayer

Holy and righteous Father, today I bow in deep awe before the seriousness of my sin and the infinite price of my redemption. Your Word teaches me that without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness, and I thank you that you gave your own Son to pay the full ransom for my transgressions.

Lord Jesus, thank you that by your death on the cross you sealed with your own blood the new covenant of God's grace, so that today I may have an inalienable share in the eternal inheritance. I praise you for your threefold work of grace: thank you that you were revealed once at the end of the ages on Golgotha to set aside my sin for ever; thank you that at this very moment you appear for me in heaven itself before the Father's face as my living High Priest; and thank you for the blessed promise that you will appear a second time to take me fully to yourself.

Take every fear of death and judgement out of my heart today, because you have already borne my judgement on the cross. Wake my heart, so that today I may live as one who looks forward to your coming with joy, watchfulness and eager longing. Amen.
