To get started

Have you ever opened a cupboard, a fridge or a storeroom that had not been looked at for a long time? What did you find – and how did it get that way?

Where we are in the story

Zephaniah prophesied in the reign of King Josiah (about 640–609 BC), probably just before Josiah's great reform of 622/621 BC. Judah had just come out of the almost sixty dark years of Manasseh and Amon, who had filled the temple with foreign altars and astrologers. Zephaniah's family line goes back four generations to a Hezekiah – most likely the king – so he probably knew the palace circles from the inside, and his name means “The LORD has hidden”. Chapter 1 announces a judgment that reverses creation (1:2–3), strikes Judah and Jerusalem with their divided hearts (1:4–13), and ends with the great “day of the LORD” (1:14–18).

A look at the Hebrew

יוֹם יְהוָה

yom YHWH · “the day of the LORD”

The word “day” tolls through the chapter like a bell – seven times in 1:15–16 alone. The people expected the day of the LORD to be a day when God would destroy their enemies and lift Judah high (compare Amos 5:18); Zephaniah pulls that false security away: the day begins at his own house (compare 1 Peter 4:17). It was fulfilled in the near history when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, and will be fulfilled finally at Christ's return.

A look at the Hebrew

עֶבְרָה

'evrah · “rage”

“A day of rage is that day” (1:15). 'Evrah is anger that overflows and bursts its banks. In the Latin Bible the verse begins Dies irae, dies illa – “Day of wrath, that day” – and from it came the medieval hymn Dies irae, sung for centuries in the Requiem Mass and set to music by Mozart and Verdi.

A look at the Hebrew

הַקֹּפְאִים עַל שִׁמְרֵיהֶם

haqqof'im 'al-shimreihem · “who have thickened on their dregs”

A picture from winemaking (1:12; compare Jeremiah 48:11). Wine was left on its lees for a time to gain strength, but at the right time it had to be poured off; left too long, it turned thick, syrupy and useless. So these comfortable, prosperous people had become spiritually lazy.

The big idea

The day of the LORD begins at God's own house: his holy, loving light searches the hidden corners of our hearts for divided loyalties and spiritual sluggishness – not because he is a cruel tyrant, but because in the fire of his jealousy he refuses to give his people up to empty idols.

Discussion · 1/7

In Genesis 1 God creates fish and birds, then animals, and finally human beings. Zephaniah reverses the order: “I will sweep away man and beast; I will sweep away the birds of the heavens and the fish of the sea”. What does this say about sin – is it ever just a private matter?

Zephaniah 1:2–3

Discussion · 2/7

God speaks of “those who bow down and swear to the LORD and swear by their king” – the people still had the LORD's name on their lips, while looking for security to another “king” (probably the idol Milkom). What are the “Milkoms” of our culture – the things we bow before while still confessing the Lord?

Zephaniah 1:4–6

Discussion · 3/7

“Silence before the Lord, the LORD!” The people thought the day of the LORD would be good news for them and bad news for their enemies. Why is it uncomfortable to hear that God's judgment begins at his own house? What helps us to be still before him rather than defend ourselves?

Zephaniah 1:7

Discussion · 4/7

“At that time I will search Jerusalem with lamps”. Wine left too long on its dregs turns thick and useless. Where can comfort, routine or disappointment make us spiritually sluggish – thick, self-satisfied and asleep?

Zephaniah 1:12

Discussion · 5/7

They say in their heart: “The LORD does no good, and he does no evil.” They did not deny that God exists, only that he is involved. In which areas of the week (work, money, social media, our thought life) do we easily live as if they lay outside his field of vision?

Zephaniah 1:12–13

Discussion · 6/7

“Neither their silver nor their gold will be able to rescue them” – said to a city with a trading quarter where merchants weighed out silver (1:11). What do we expect to protect us when things go wrong? How does the chapter answer that?

Zephaniah 1:18

Discussion · 7/7

Day 1 reads “the fire of his jealousy” as the zeal of a loving covenant God who will not give his children up, and says that the day of rage and darkness was poured out in full on Jesus at Golgotha. How does that change the way you hear this chapter? And if the Lord walked with a lamp through the hidden rooms of your heart, what might he find standing beside your worship of him? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

Zephaniah 1:14–18

For the week ahead

  • Begin each day this week with two minutes of silence before God (1:7). Then ask him to shine his lamp on one area of your life – your diary, your bank statement, your phone – and listen to what he shows you.
  • Name one “Milkom” you are tempted to trust for your security (money, status, approval, control). Take one concrete step this week that shows where your trust really lies – give something away, tell someone, or hand a worry to God in prayer each time it comes back.

Pray together

Begin with a minute of shared silence, as 1:7 calls for. Then let each one, quietly or aloud, ask God to search his or her heart and to forgive a divided heart. Thank Jesus together that he bore the darkness and rage of the day of judgment at Golgotha, so that we can be hidden and safe in him. Close by asking him for an undivided heart that fears and serves him alone.
