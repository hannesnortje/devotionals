He Rejoices over You · Day 1 / 5

The Light That Searches the Shadows of the Heart

Scripture Zephaniah 1:1–18

1The word of the LORD that came to Zephaniah son of Cushi, son of Gedaliah, son of Amariah, son of Hezekiah, in the days of Josiah son of Amon, king of Judah.

2I will sweep away, sweep away everythingfrom the face of the ground –declares the LORD.3I will sweep away man and beast;I will sweep away the birds of the heavensand the fish of the sea,and the stumbling blocks with the wicked.I will cut off mankindfrom the face of the ground –declares the LORD.4I will stretch out my hand against Judahand against all the inhabitants of Jerusalem.I will cut off from this place the remnant of Baal,the name of the idol-priests together with the priests,5and those who bow down on the rooftopsto the host of the heavens,and those who bow down and swear to the LORDand swear by their king,6and those who turn back from following the LORD,who have not sought the LORDand have not inquired of him.7Silence before the Lord, the LORD!For the day of the LORD is near;for the LORD has prepared a sacrifice,he has consecrated those he invited.8On the day of the LORD's sacrificeI will punish the officials and the king's sonsand all who clothe themselves in foreign clothing.9And on that day I will punisheveryone who leaps over the threshold,those who fill the house of their masterwith violence and deceit.10On that day – declares the LORD –a cry will sound from the Fish Gate,wailing from the Second Quarter,and a great crash from the hills.11Wail, you inhabitants of the Mortar!For all the people of Canaan are destroyed;all who weigh out silver are cut off.12At that time I will search Jerusalem with lamps,and I will punish the menwho have thickened on their dregs,who say in their heart:"The LORD does no good,and he does no evil."13Their wealth will become plunderand their houses a desolation.They will build houses but not live in them;they will plant vineyards but not drink their wine.14Near is the great day of the LORD,near and hurrying very fast.Listen! The day of the LORD!Bitterly the warrior cries out there.15A day of rage is that day,a day of distress and anguish,a day of wreck and ruin,a day of darkness and deep gloom,a day of cloud and thick darkness,16a day of ram's horn and battle cryagainst the fortified citiesand against the lofty corner towers.17I will bring distress on mankind,and they will walk like the blind,for they have sinned against the LORD.Their blood will be poured out like dust,and their flesh like dung.18Neither their silver nor their goldwill be able to rescue themon the day of the rage of the LORD.In the fire of his jealousythe whole earth will be devoured;for a full end, yes, a terrifying endhe will make of all the inhabitants of the earth.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today we begin a five-day journey through the book of Zephaniah – three short chapters that start with the dark "day of the LORD" and end with one of the tenderest promises in the whole Old Testament: he rejoices over you with a song of joy (3:17).

The book of Zephaniah opens with a strikingly long family line – four generations back to a certain Hezekiah. This is unique among the prophets, and it immediately gives us a key to Zephaniah's world. Many interpreters take this Hezekiah to be King Hezekiah; the text does not actually call him "king", but why else trace the line back so far? If so, Zephaniah had royal blood in his veins. He was then no outsider criticising the corruption in Jerusalem from a distance; he knew the palace circles from the inside. He prophesied in the reign of King Josiah (about 640–609 BC) – according to many interpreters probably just before Josiah's great religious reform (622/621 BC) got under way.

Judah had just come out of the dark, almost sixty-year reign of King Manasseh (55 years) and his son Amon. Manasseh had filled the temple of the Lord with foreign altars, brought in astrologers and even made his own son pass through the fire (2 Kings 21:3–6). By the time Zephaniah raises his voice, the culture of Jerusalem is soaked in spiritual compromise. The name Zephaniah (Hebrew Tsefanyah – צְפַנְיָה) means "The LORD has hidden" or "The LORD treasures / keeps safe". His name is a prophetic message in itself: in the middle of the coming judgment on the wicked, God will keep his faithful remnant safely hidden.

Insights from the Hebrew

  • "By their king" (malkam – מַלְכָּם, v. 5): God addresses those who bow down and swear to the LORD and swear by their king. The Hebrew malkam means "their king". Some ancient translations (and many modern ones) read Milkom here, the name of the Ammonite idol (1 Kings 11:5, 33). Whichever way we read it, the "king" they swear by is hardly the king in Jerusalem – it is another god honoured as king (Milkom or Molech). They swear to the LORD and by this other king.
  • "Thickened on their dregs" (haqqof'im 'al-shimreihem – הַקֹּפְאִים עַל שִׁמְרֵיהֶם, v. 12): A picture from winemaking. Wine was left on its lees for a time to gain flavour and strength, but at the right time it had to be poured off into clean vessels (compare Jeremiah 48:11). Wine left too long on its dregs turns thick, syrupy and useless.
  • The day of the LORD (yom YHWH – יוֹם יְהוָה, vv. 7, 14–18): The word "day" tolls through this chapter like a bell – seven times in verses 15–16 alone: A day of rage is that day, a day of distress and anguish, a day of wreck and ruin, a day of darkness and deep gloom, a day of cloud and thick darkness, a day of ram's horn and battle cry.
  • "A day of rage" (yom 'evrah – יוֹם עֶבְרָה, v. 15): 'Evrah is often linked with 'avar, "to pass over" – anger that overflows, that bursts its banks. In the Latin Bible (the Vulgate) verse 15 begins with the words Dies irae, dies illa – "Day of wrath, that day". From these words came the famous medieval hymn Dies irae (probably from the thirteenth century; it is often attributed to Thomas of Celano, but that is not certain). For centuries it was sung in the Latin Requiem Mass, and composers such as Mozart and Verdi set it to music. Zephaniah's "day" has echoed through the whole history of the church.

Core Theological Truths

Chapter 1 unfolds powerful theological truths that lay the foundation for the rest of the book:

  1. Creation undone ("de-creation" in vv. 2–3): Zephaniah uses language that points straight back to Genesis 1 and to the flood of Genesis 6–7. In Genesis 1 God creates in this order: fish and birds, animals, and finally human beings. In Zephaniah 1:3 the order is reversed: I will sweep away man and beast; I will sweep away the birds of the heavens and the fish of the sea. Why? To show that persistent sin and rebellion against the Creator are never just a private matter; they unravel the whole order of creation. When humanity turns its back on God, the whole world suffers.
  2. The danger of syncretism – a divided heart (vv. 4–6): The people of Jerusalem had not necessarily stopped going to the temple. They still had the name of the LORD (YHWH) on their lips, but at the same time they looked for security to "their king" (or Milkom, the Ammonite god – the reading is debated) and to the host of the heavens on their flat rooftops. God has no interest in a shared throne. To serve the Lord half-heartedly while we find our security in the idols of our culture is as offensive to him as open atheism.
  3. Practical atheism and spiritual sluggishness (v. 12): One of the sharpest images in the Old Testament appears in verse 12. God says: At that time I will search Jerusalem with lamps, and I will punish the men who have thickened on their dregs. Like wine left too long on its lees, these people had grown rich, comfortable and spiritually lazy. They said in their hearts: "The LORD does no good, and he does no evil." They did not deny God's existence, but his involvement. They lived as if God were a retired spectator who does not care about right or wrong.
  4. The day of the LORD (vv. 7, 14–18): The people believed that "the day of the LORD" would be a day on which God would destroy all their enemies and lift Judah high (compare Amos 5:18). Zephaniah pulls that false security out from under them. The day of the LORD begins at his own house (compare 1 Peter 4:17). On that day – fulfilled in the near history when Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC, and one day to be fulfilled finally at Christ's return – no silver, gold or economic prosperity can save anyone: Neither their silver nor their gold will be able to rescue them on the day of the rage of the LORD (v. 18). "The Mortar" in verse 11 was probably a trading quarter of Jerusalem, where merchants weighed out their silver.

Living It Out

Zephaniah 1 holds up an uncomfortable but life-giving mirror. We no longer burn incense to Baal on our rooftops, but the human heart has not changed at all.

Our greatest temptation in a modern, prosperous or hurried society is the same as Jerusalem's: spiritual syncretism and practical atheism. With our mouths we confess that we trust the Lord (we "swear to the LORD"), but in practice we bow before the "Milkom" of financial security, status, human approval and self-control. When the bank account is full and our plans run smoothly, we easily become like wine left too long on its dregs: thick, self-satisfied and spiritually asleep.

From Monday to Saturday we often live as if we were saying with them: "The LORD does no good, and he does no evil" – as if our daily decisions, our integrity at work, the way we speak on social media and the places our thoughts wander lay outside his field of vision. But Zephaniah reminds us that God's loving, holy eyes move with lamps through the darkest corners of Jerusalem – and of our hearts. He does this not because he is a cruel tyrant, but because the fire of his jealousy (v. 18) is the zeal of a jealous, loving covenant God who refuses to give his children up to empty idols and spiritual death. As believers in Christ we know that this terrifying "day of rage" and darkness (v. 15) was poured out in full on Jesus at Golgotha, so that we need not be destroyed but can be shaken awake to true life.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • If the Lord walked today with a lamp through the hidden rooms of your heart and your daily routine, which "idols" would he find standing beside your worship of him? What do you really trust for your peace and security?
  • Are there areas of your life where you have spiritually "thickened on your dregs" – where comfort, routine or disappointment have made you cold and indifferent to God's voice?
  • Do you live today aware that God is actively involved in your choices, or do you in practice live as though he were far away and uninvolved?

Prayer

Holy and almighty Lord, today I grow still before you. As Zephaniah 1:7 calls me – "Silence before the Lord, the LORD!" – I want to silence my own excuses and the noise of my life before your face. Lord, your Word cuts deep and honest. I confess that my heart is so often divided. I sing your praise and confess your name, yet in the same breath I look for my safety in my own abilities, my possessions and the approval of people. Forgive me for the times I live as if you do not see and as if you are not involved in the small decisions of my day.

Come and search my heart today with your light, Lord. Do not let me grow spiritually sluggish, cold and self-satisfied like wine that has lain too long on its dregs. Shake me awake from my spiritual slumber. Thank you, Lord Jesus, that at Golgotha you bore the full darkness and rage of the day of judgment for me, so that I can be hidden and safe in you. Purify my heart by the fire of your holy love, and teach me to fear and serve you alone with an undivided heart. In Jesus' name, Amen.
