Small-group kit · Session 3 / 3

Yet I Will Rejoice

Present ▶

The big ideaRemembering what God has done gives hope for what lies ahead: even when every earthly security falls away and our bodies tremble, we can rejoice in the God of our salvation, because our joy rests on who he is, not on what he gives.

Timing

  • 10 min Welcome & opener
  • 10 min Read the passage
  • 10 min Background & original language
  • 35 min Discussion
  • 20 min Application & prayer

To get started

Is there a song, a photo or an object that reminds you of a time when you were carried through something difficult? What does it bring back to you?

Read together · Habakkuk 3:1–19

One person reads the prayer of 3:1–2; a second reads 3:3–15 slowly and keeps a moment of silence at each Sela; a third reads 3:16; then the whole group reads 3:17–19 together.

Where we are in the story

Chapter 1 began with a question mark in the dark (1:2); chapter 2 took us to the guard post, where God's promise was written down. Chapter 3 is a prayer-psalm with musical directions at the beginning and the end and three times the word Sela. Habakkuk asks God to bring his work to life (3:2), remembers how God came in power at the exodus, at Sinai and in Joshua's days (3:3–15), trembles at what lies ahead (3:16) – and ends with one of the boldest confessions of faith in Scripture (3:17–19). His circumstances have not improved at all; what has changed is Habakkuk himself.

A look at the Hebrew

  • rachem – רַחֵם “compassion”: Habakkuk prays: “In turmoil, remember compassion.” (3:2). Rachem is deep, motherly tenderness, related to rechem, “womb”. The word rogez, “turmoil”, is often understood as God's wrath; the same root returns in 3:16, where the prophet himself trembles. He no longer asks God to take away the chastisement, but that God's grace will keep his people alive through it.
  • 'alaz / gil – אֶעְלוֹזָה · אָגִילָה “exult / rejoice”: “yet I will exult in the LORD, I will rejoice in the God of my salvation” (3:18). 'Alaz means to triumph, to be exuberantly glad; gil is to shout aloud for joy, perhaps from a root “to go round in a circle”, like someone dancing. This is not teeth-gritting acceptance but radiant joy in God himself – outdoing the Chaldean who “is glad and rejoices” over his catch (1:15).
  • ayyalot – אַיָּלוֹת “deer”: “he makes my feet like those of the deer and lets me tread on my high places” (3:19). Ayyalot are the does, the female deer, which climb steep, dangerous rock ledges with astonishing sureness, without slipping. The LORD does not remove the rocks of the trial; he gives feet to stand on them.

Discussion

  1. “LORD, I have heard the report of you; I fear, LORD, your work.” Then Habakkuk prays: “In the midst of the years, bring it to life”. Where in your life, family or church do you need God to bring new life and compassion in the middle of a dry season? (Habakkuk 3:2)
  2. In 3:3–15 Habakkuk looks back: the exodus through the sea (vv. 8, 15), Sinai in the south (v. 3), sun and moon standing still in Joshua's days (v. 11). Why does remembering what God has done give hope for the future? What do you remember – and what does the cross and empty tomb of Christ add to this? (Habakkuk 3:3–15)
  3. “You went out for the salvation of your people, for the salvation of your anointed.” The word salvation (yesha') shares its root with the names Joshua and Jesus. How does it help to know that God himself goes out to save? (Habakkuk 3:13)
  4. Habakkuk is honest about his body: “I heard, and my insides trembled; at the sound my lips quivered.” Yet he says: “Yet I will wait quietly for the day of distress”. Do you sometimes feel guilty about anxiety or stress, as if it meant you had no faith? What do you learn from trembling and trust standing side by side? (Habakkuk 3:16)
  5. Verse 17 lists six things – figs, vines, olives, fields, flock, cattle – the whole farm economy from small luxuries to bare survival. If you wrote your own verse 17 in today's terms, how would it sound? (Habakkuk 3:17)
  6. “yet I will exult in the LORD, I will rejoice in the God of my salvation.” Many of us live with an unspoken contract: “I will trust you as long as …”. What is the difference between joy in what God gives and joy in God himself? (Habakkuk 3:18)
  7. Looking back over the whole book – from “How long, LORD?” (1:2) to “Yet” (3:18): what has changed in Habakkuk, and what has changed in you during this series? Share only what you are comfortable sharing. (Habakkuk 3:19)

For the week ahead

  • Write your own verse 17 this week, naming your fears one by one (“Though my savings run out, though …”), and then write 3:18 underneath and pray it aloud each day.
  • Make a short list of three times God carried you in the past – your own “3:3–15” – and share one of them with someone this week.

Pray together

Begin by thanking God together for what he has done in the past, in Scripture and in your own lives. Then let anyone who wishes name, in a sentence, where in their life “the fig tree does not blossom”, and let the group answer each one by saying together: “yet I will exult in the LORD, I will rejoice in the God of my salvation.” Close by reading 3:19 aloud over one another as a blessing.

Notes for the leader

  • This last session closes the series. Leave ten minutes at the end to look back at the journey from the question mark of 1:2 to the exclamation mark of 3:18, the series title.
  • 3:3–15 is dense poetry. Don't explain every image; give the group three keys from day 6 (the exodus through the sea, Sinai in Teman and Paran, Joshua's long day in Joshua 10:12–13) and the main point: the God who saved in the past holds the future.
  • Sela (3:3, 9, 13) probably marks a musical pause. Let the reader really pause there – it helps the group take in the chapter. Shigionot (3:1) is a musical term whose meaning is uncertain; no need to linger on it.
  • Verse 17 can touch real losses: illness, unemployment, a death, a broken marriage. Don't let “rejoice” become pressure to hide grief. Verse 16 shows that trembling and trust live side by side; joy in God does not deny the loss.
  • The prayer time may need longer than usual. If needed, shorten the context and take the last discussion question into the prayer time.
