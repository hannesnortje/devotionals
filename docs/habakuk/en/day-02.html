Yet I Will Rejoice · Day 2 / 6

An Unexpected and Shocking Answer

Scripture Habakkuk 1:5–11

5See among the nations, and look;be astonished – be astounded!For I am doing a work in your daysthat you would not believe if it were told.6For look, I am raising up the Chaldeans,that bitter and impetuous nation,who march across the breadth of the earthto take possession of dwellings that are not their own.7Terrible and dreadful is he;from himself come forth his justice and his dignity.8His horses are swifter than leopardsand fiercer than wolves at evening.His horsemen gallop on,his horsemen come from far away;they fly like a vulture that hurries to devour.9All of them come for violence;the set of their faces is forward,and he gathers captives like sand.10He mocks at kings,and rulers are a joke to him.He laughs at every fortress;he heaps up dust and captures it.11Then he sweeps on like the wind and passes by,and he becomes guilty –he whose strength is his god.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

On Day 1 Habakkuk accused God of silence and inaction. He thought the Lord was looking the other way while Judah rotted in corruption. But in verse 5 God breaks the silence – and his answer takes Habakkuk's breath away. God had not been passive at all; behind the scenes he was already orchestrating a world-historical shift that no one in Judah saw coming.

Historical and military background: To feel the shock of verse 6 we need to look at the political map of the late seventh century BC. For centuries the Assyrian Empire had been the dominant superpower of the ancient Near East, with Egypt as the other great power. The Chaldeans (the Neo-Babylonian Empire under Nabopolassar, who became king of Babylon in 626 BC, and his son, crown prince Nebuchadnezzar) had long been only a subordinate group. But within two decades Babylon, together with the Medes, crushed Assyria (Nineveh fell in 612 BC) and defeated the Egyptian army at the famous Battle of Carchemish (605 BC).

For a citizen of Jerusalem the thought that God would use this ruthless, pagan war machine to discipline his own covenant people was simply unthinkable. God describes them in terrifying poetic images: swifter than leopards, fiercer than wolves at evening hunting in the dark, and like a vulture swooping on its prey. They mock fortified cities by simply piling up earthen ramps against the walls and overrunning them ("he heaps up dust and captures it").

Insights from the Hebrew

  • Be astonished – be astounded! (wehittammehu tamahu – וְהִתַּמְּהוּ תְּמָהוּ): God doubles the Hebrew verb tamah. It does not mean being mildly surprised, but being paralysed with shock, bewilderment and disbelief. The New Testament (Acts 13:41) quotes this very verse to warn people not to overlook God's saving work in Christ because of their own prejudices.
  • I am raising up (meqim – מֵקִים): A participle of the verb qum ("rise") in its causative form: "make rise, raise up". Nebuchadnezzar thought he was building his own empire, but the Hebrew makes it crystal clear: it is YHWH who raises the Babylonians onto the world stage.
  • Bitter and impetuous (hammar wehannimhar – הַמַּר וְהַנִּמְהָר): A powerful play on words and sounds in the Hebrew. Mar means bitter, cruel, merciless; nimhar means hasty, reckless, unstoppable.
  • From himself come forth his justice (mimmennu mishpato – מִמֶּנּוּ מִשְׁפָּטוֹ, v. 7): The Babylonians recognise no higher moral law and no divine authority. They are a law to themselves; they decide right and wrong on the basis of sheer military power.
  • He whose strength is his god (v. 11): Verse 11 says that he sweeps on like the wind "and he becomes guilty" (we'ashem – וְאָשֵׁם), because they worship their own abilities and military success as their idol.

Core Theological Truths

  1. God's silence never means God's absence: While Habakkuk thought God was doing nothing (1:2), God says: "For I am doing a work in your days" (1:5). God often works on a large scale, beyond our immediate field of vision.
  2. God is sovereign over all nations, even the godless: The Lord is not merely a local tribal god of Israel; he rules the whole of world history. He can use even broken, secular or hostile powers as instruments in his hand to achieve his purposes and to shake his people awake.
  3. God's answers rarely fit our boxes: Habakkuk had prayed for a tidy, internal spiritual revival in Judah. God answers with a Babylonian invasion. When we pray that God would root out evil and compromise in our lives, he sometimes uses radical and painful pruning shears we would never have chosen ourselves.
  4. Human pride carries the seed of its own downfall: Although God uses the Chaldeans, he does not approve of their cruelty. Verse 11 ends on a decisive note: by making his own strength a god, "he becomes guilty". Their arrogance will in the end be judged by the same sovereign God.

Living It Out

This passage jolts us out of our comfort zone. We like a predictable God – a God who answers our prayers exactly according to the script we have written for him. We ask for patience and expect a warm feeling of peace; instead God brings a difficult situation or a trying person across our path who exposes our impatience. We pray that God would purify our church or our family, and then he shakes our false securities until only he is left.

Sometimes God's answer to our prayers at first brings more questions and discomfort than the original problem! When the foundations of our world shake – through economic uncertainty, political upheaval or personal crises – we easily get stuck in fear of the "Chaldeans" of our time. We stare ourselves blind at the power of the problem, the injustice of people, or the speed at which things spin out of control.

Habakkuk 1:5–11 calls us to look wider: "See among the nations, and look!" Even when earthly powers or the storms of life seem to make their own laws and flatten everything before them, they still stand under the sovereign rule of the Creator. And just as with the Babylonians, we must examine our own hearts: where do we rely on our own cleverness, our bank account or our own control, so that our own strength becomes our god?

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Has God ever answered a prayer in your life in a way that caught you completely off guard – something that first looked like a disaster but later turned out to be his way of shaping or rescuing you?
  • Which "Chaldeans" (threats, crises or powers beyond your control) frighten you at the moment? How does it change your perspective to know that nothing on earth can rise up unless God allows it within his greater purpose?
  • In verses 7 and 11 we read of one from whom "come forth his justice and his dignity" and "whose strength is his god". In which areas of your daily life do you live as if your own strength and abilities were enough, without depending on the Lord?

Prayer

Sovereign Lord, King over the nations and over my life, today I bow in wonder before your greatness. Forgive me for so often trying to fit you into a small, predictable box, and for thinking you are silent just because I cannot see what you are doing. Sometimes your ways frighten me, Lord, for your thoughts are higher than my thoughts.

When the storms of this world rush at me like an impetuous army, keep me from staring in panic at the threat. Open the eyes of my heart to see that your hand stays on the helm even in the midst of chaos.

Today I also confess every place in my own heart where I have made an idol of my own strength, my own plans and my own control. Break my self-willed pride and teach me to trust you, even when your answers leave me utterly astounded. In Jesus' name, Amen.
