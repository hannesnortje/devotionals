Yet I Will Rejoice!

Scripture Habakkuk 3:1–19

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today we stand on the summit of Habakkuk's remarkable spiritual journey – and at the end of our six days together in this book. Chapter 1 began with a painful, raw question mark in the dark ("How long, LORD, shall I cry for help, and you do not hear?", 1:2). Chapter 2 took us into the stillness of the guard post, where God's promises of justice and faith were written down. Now, in chapter 3, everything flows into an exclamation mark of worship!

As literature, this last chapter is a liturgical psalm – complete with musical directions at the beginning ("according to shigionot") and at the end ("For the choir director, on my stringed instruments"), as well as three occurrences of the word Sela (verses 3, 9, 13). The exact meaning of Sela is uncertain; it probably marks a musical pause or interlude, a moment for reverent reflection. This suggests that Habakkuk's personal crisis of faith was worked into a shared song for worship, so that the whole covenant people could be strengthened by it in the time of the coming Babylonian exile.

In verses 3–15 we see a breathtaking theophany (an appearance of God). Habakkuk looks back over Israel's history of salvation. He probably calls to mind the events of the exodus from Egypt (the passage through the sea, vv. 8 and 15), the making of the covenant at Sinai, with God coming from the southern desert regions of Teman and Paran (v. 3; compare Deuteronomy 33:2), and – v. 11 recalls Joshua 10:12–13 – the victories under Joshua when sun and moon stood still. He reminds himself that the God who crushed the forces of nature and the world powers in the past to save his people is exactly the same God who holds the future in his hand.

Insights from the Hebrew

  • According to shigionot (shigionot – שִׁגְיֹנוֹת): In verse 1 this musical term (found only here and in the heading of Psalm 7) may refer to a song with a fast, passionate and changing rhythm – a deeply emotional ode that binds trembling and ecstasy together. Its exact meaning, however, is uncertain.
  • Bring it to life (chayyehu – חַיֵּיהוּ) and compassion (rachem – רַחֵם): In verse 2 Habakkuk prays: "In the midst of the years, bring it to life" (chayyehu – make alive, preserve, raise up) and "In turmoil, remember compassion" (rachem – deep, motherly tenderness that wells up from within; related to rechem, "womb"). The word rogez, "turmoil", is often understood as God's wrath; the same root returns in verse 16, where the prophet himself "trembles". He no longer asks God to take away the Babylonian chastisement; he asks that God's grace will keep his people alive through the judgement.
  • Though … yet (ki … wa'ani – כִּי … וַאֲנִי): The hinge of verses 17–18 lies in the contrast between "though" and "yet" (literally: "And I – in the LORD I will exult").
  • Exult ('alaz – אֶעְלוֹזָה) and rejoice (gil – אָגִילָה): In verse 18 the prophet uses two powerful verbs of joy. 'Alaz means to triumph, to be exuberantly glad, and gil is to shout aloud for joy; some link it with a root meaning "to go round in a circle" – like someone dancing with delight. This is not a stoic, teeth-gritting acceptance of his lot; it is active, radiant joy in God himself! Notice the contrast: the enemy's "exulting" ('alitsut, v. 14) and the Chaldean "rejoicing" over his catch (1:15) are outdone here by the prophet's rejoicing in his God.
  • The God of my salvation (Elohei yish'i – אֱלֹהֵי יִשְׁעִי):* The word for "salvation" is yesha', from the same root as the names *Joshua / Yeshua (Jesus) – "the LORD is salvation". Habakkuk literally rejoices in the God who is his Saviour.
  • Deer (ayyalot – אַיָּלוֹת) and high places (bamotai – בָּמוֹתַי): In verse 19 God makes his feet "like those of the deer" – literally the does, the female deer, which climb steep, dangerous rock ledges with astonishing sureness, without slipping.

Core Theological Truths

  1. Spiritual remembering is the fuel for future hope: When the future looks dark, the believer looks back at God's saving acts in the past (verses 3–15). For us today this means looking back to the cross and the empty tomb of Christ. If God has already defeated our greatest enemy (sin and death), he will carry us through our present storms as well.
  2. True faith does not switch off fear, but takes it captive in peace: See how honest verse 16 is: Habakkuk's insides tremble, his lips quiver, and he trembles where he stands at the thought of the approaching war. To believe does not mean you are made of stone and feel no human fear; it means that in the middle of your trembling you choose: "Yet I will wait quietly for the day of distress".
  3. The total collapse of earthly security (verse 17): The six things listed in verse 17 represent the whole ancient farming economy, from small luxuries to bare survival: fig trees (sweetness/luxury), vines (joy/wine), olives (cooking oil and lamps), terraced fields (daily bread), the flock (wool and milk) and cattle (ploughing power and capital). It describes a total economic collapse.
  4. Unconditional worship rests on who God is, not on what God gives: When all six pillars of earthly prosperity in verse 17 fall away, the one indestructible Pillar of verse 18 remains standing: the LORD, the God of my salvation. If your joy is built on your health, your wallet or your circumstances, any storm can steal it. If your joy is anchored in God, it is untouchable.

Living It Out

Habakkuk 3:17–19 is one of the most liberating passages in the whole of Scripture, precisely because it confronts us with our unspoken "contracts" with God. We often live with a conditional faith: "Lord, I will trust you and be happy as long as my children are safe, as long as the medical tests come back clear, as long as my business grows, and as long as my dreams come true."

But what happens when the fig tree does not blossom and the pens stand empty? What happens when the bad news does come, when the economy tightens, or when a prayer is not answered the way we had hoped? For many people, faith collapses along with their circumstances. Habakkuk shows us what mature, refined faith looks like. Note this: between chapter 1 and chapter 3 Habakkuk's outward circumstances did not improve at all – on the contrary, the Babylonian invasion was closer than ever! What had changed was Habakkuk's inner life.

He moved from focusing on the problem to focusing on the Person of God. That is why, with a trembling body (v. 16) but an unconquerable spirit (v. 18), he can look out over the ruins of his world and cry: "Yet!" And instead of the crisis trampling him, the LORD turns the sharp, dangerous rocks of his trial into "high places" on which he can stand with the nimble feet of a deer.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • If you had to write your own "verse 17" in today's terms, how would it sound? ("Though my savings run out, though this relationship is not restored, though my health …") Have you laid those fears out one by one before God and spoken your "Yet I will rejoice" over them?
  • Look at Habakkuk's physical vulnerability in verse 16 (quivering lips, a body that trembles). Do you sometimes feel guilty for experiencing human anxiety or stress, as if it meant you had no faith? How does it encourage you to see that Habakkuk's trembling and his quiet trust existed side by side?
  • In verse 2 Habakkuk prays: "In the midst of the years, bring it to life". Where in your own spiritual life, your family or your church do you need God to bring new life and compassion today, in the middle of a dry season?

Prayer

Mighty Lord, God of history and God of my salvation, with deep awe I come today to the end of this journey through Habakkuk. When I heard of your power and your righteous ways, my heart too has sometimes trembled, for you are high and exalted far above my small human understanding.

Lord, today I plead with your prophet: bring your work to life in the midst of our years, and in turmoil remember your deep, motherly compassion! Today I lay all my conditions down at your feet. Forgive me that I have so often thanked you only for the fruit on the tree and the cattle in the stall, instead of finding my joy in you yourself.

Today I declare over my life, over my fears and over my uncertain future: though every earthly prop falls away and every harvest fails, yet I will exult in the LORD! I will rejoice in Jesus Christ, the God of my salvation. Be my strength today. Make my weary feet like those of the deer, and let me tread the steep high places of this life in your victory. To you belongs all praise, for ever. Amen.
