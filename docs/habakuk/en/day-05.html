Five Woes and the Glory of the LORD

Scripture Habakkuk 2:6–20

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Having declared in verse 4 that Babylon's proud, "puffed-up" soul will perish while the righteous will live by his faithfulness, God now spells out in verses 6–20 exactly why and how this violent world empire will fall. God puts a forward-looking taunt-song into the mouths of the very peoples oppressed by the Chaldeans. The passage is built around five "Woes" (hoy in Hebrew) – each of about three verses – which lay bare, right down to the bone, the moral rot and false foundations of the Babylonian empire.

King Nebuchadnezzar and his successors turned Babylon into one of the architectural wonders of the ancient world, complete with massive double city walls, the Ishtar Gate and huge temples. But how was this splendid city paid for? By ruthless plunder, slave labour, extortion from smaller nations, and even the large-scale felling and destruction of Lebanon's famous cedar forests (verse 17). God pulls back the curtain on Babylon's apparent glory and shows that its whole empire was built on stolen goods and human blood.

Insights from the Hebrew

  • Woe! (hoy – הוֹי): This cry occurs five times (verses 6, 9, 12, 15, 19). In prophetic literature hoy combines a funeral lament ("Alas, how tragic!") with a divine announcement of judgement ("Beware, the end is coming!"). God, as it were, is already holding Babylon's funeral while it still stands at the height of its power.
  • Pledges ('avtit – עַבְטִיט): In verse 6 a rare Hebrew word is used (only here in the Bible) meaning "pledges, IOUs" – goods obtained through usury. Older translations read it as two words, 'av tit, "a thick layer of mud or clay". The picture is apt: what Babylon thinks are golden treasures is in reality only a heavy load of mud that will make it sink.
  • Glory (kavod – כָּבוֹד): In verse 14 we read of the kavod of the LORD. The root means "to be heavy, weighty"; its opposite, qalon ("disgrace", v. 16), comes from "to be light". While Babylon's power will vanish like a light mist – "You are sated with disgrace instead of glory" (v. 16) – the true "weight" and majesty of God will finally flood the whole earth.
  • Mute idols (elilim illemim – אֱלִילִים אִלְּמִים): In verse 18 Habakkuk uses a sharp Hebrew wordplay. Elilim means "worthless little things / idols" (it calls to mind Elohim, God), and illemim means "mute, speechless". People worship shiny, dead images with "no breath at all inside" them (ruach, v. 19).
  • Silence! (has – הַס): In verse 20 the chapter closes with the Hebrew sound-word has! – just like our "hush!" or "shh!". In contrast to the mute idols that cannot speak, the living God in his temple brings the whole noisy, proud world to reverent silence.

Core Theological Truths

  1. The woe against greed and theft (vv. 6–8): The law of divine retribution (lex talionis). Babylon grew rich by exploitation, but its victims will suddenly rise up like creditors and strip it bare. Dishonest gain is never a blessing; it is a time bomb.
  2. The woe against false security and pride (vv. 9–11): No human fortress is high enough to escape God's judgement. They wanted "to set his nest on high" (like an eagle, out of reach of danger) by trampling on others. But even if every human witness is silenced, the stones in the walls and the rafters of their palaces will cry out as witnesses against their injustice (compare Luke 19:40).
  3. The woe against violence and bloodshed (vv. 12–14): All human empires built without God end in the fire. Verse 13 shows the futility of godless ambition: peoples wear themselves out building empires that in the end are only firewood for history. Why? Because God's final goal for creation is not Babylon's glory, but this: "For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD" (v. 14).
  4. The woe against exploitation and dehumanisation (vv. 15–17): Whoever humiliates others will himself be humiliated by God. Babylon made neighbouring nations politically and militarily "drunk" with false treaties and violence, to leave them defenceless and rob them of their dignity ("nakedness"), and even laid waste to nature (Lebanon's forests and animals). That is why "the cup in the LORD's right hand" (the cup of wrath) will come around to it, so that its apparent glory is covered with disgrace.
  5. The woe against idolatry (vv. 18–20): The folly of man-made gods over against the majesty of the living God. The root of all the preceding social evils (greed, pride, violence and cruelty) is idolatry (v. 18). When people worship the work of their own hands, they lose their moral compass. But verse 20 thunders out the final truth: idols are dead, but the LORD is in his holy temple!

Living It Out

When we look at these five "Woes", it is easy simply to point a finger at the ancient Babylonians or at corrupt world leaders. But the Spirit of God also holds this text up like a mirror to our own modern hearts. We live in a consumer culture still driven by the same five temptations: the urge always to heap up more (v. 6); the attempt to "set our nest on high" through financial security, so that no crisis can touch us (v. 9); the willingness to walk over other people to build our own career or "city" (v. 12); the manipulation or humiliation of others for our own advantage (v. 15); and trust in "mute idols" overlaid with gold and silver – status, technology and possessions with no spiritual life inside them (v. 19).

Yet Habakkuk 2:6–20 also gives two indescribable anchors of comfort to the believer who is discouraged today about the state of the world:

  • First, verse 14: The world will not end in Babylonian chaos. History is moving unstoppably towards the day when the knowledge of the glory of the LORD will cover every square centimetre of the earth, "as the waters cover the sea". Your labour in the Lord is never in vain, because you are building his eternal kingdom, not something destined "for the fire".
  • Second, verse 20: When politics rages, the economy totters and injustice seems overwhelming, hear the voice that says: "silence before him, all the earth!" God's throne is not vacant. He is in his holy temple, fully in control. Where in chapter 1 Habakkuk asked why God keeps silent (1:13), chapter 2 ends with Habakkuk himself falling silent in reverent worship before God's almighty power.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Verse 9 warns against the attempt "to set his nest on high, to be snatched from the grip of evil". Where are you currently looking for your deepest sense of safety and invulnerability – in your bank account, your insurance and your own planning, or in the presence of the Lord?
  • Verse 13 speaks of peoples who "toil only for the fire" and "weary themselves for nothing". Looking at where most of your time, energy and emotion went this past month: how much of it is building things of eternal value (v. 14), and how much of it is merely earthly chasing that will burn up?
  • What does it mean for you in practice to apply verse 20 today – to bring your own inner noise, anxiety and complaints to a standstill ("silence before him"), because you know the LORD is in his holy temple?

Prayer

Holy and almighty LORD of hosts, today I become silent before you. I bow in deep reverence before your throne, for you alone are God, and you are in your holy temple. Lord, search my heart in the light of your Word. Keep me from ever seeking my security in building my own high nest, or from trusting in the shiny, mute idols of this age that have no life inside them.

Forgive me for the times I wear myself out for things that are nothing and will in the end be consumed by the fire. Thank you for the wonderful assurance that no injustice, exploitation or violence will have the last word on this earth. Let my heart take courage today from your promise that the earth will be filled with the knowledge of your glory, as the waters cover the sea.

I bring all my restless thoughts and fears to silence before your majesty. Reign in my heart, O King of the ages. In Jesus' name, Amen.
