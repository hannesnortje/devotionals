Who Is a God Like You? Sins Cast into the Depths of the Sea

Scripture Micah 7:8–20

8Do not rejoice over me, my enemy!When I have fallen, I rise;when I sit in darkness,the LORD is light for me.9The rage of the LORD I will bear,for I have sinned against him,until he pleads my caseand brings about justice for me.He will bring me out into the light;I will see his righteousness.10And my enemy will see it,and shame will cover herwho said to me: "Where is he, the LORD your God?"My eyes will look on her;now she will be trampled downlike mud in the streets.11A day for building your walls!On that day the boundary will be pushed far out.12On that day they will come to you,from Assyria and the cities of Egypt,and from Egypt to the River,from sea to sea and from mountain to mountain.13But the earth will become a desolationbecause of those who live in it,as the fruit of their deeds.14Shepherd your people with your staff,the flock of your inheritance,who live alone in a forest,in the midst of a fruitful land;let them graze in Bashan and Gileadas in ancient days.15As in the days when you came out of the land of Egypt,I will show him wonders.16Nations will see and be ashamedof all their might;they will lay a hand on their mouth;their ears will be deaf.17They will lick dust like the snake,like the creatures that crawl on the earth;they will come trembling out of their strongholds;to the LORD our God they will come in dread,and they will fear you.18Who is a God like you,who bears away guiltand passes over rebellionfor the remnant of his inheritance?He does not hold on to his anger for ever,for he delights in faithful love.19He will again have compassion on us;he will tread our guilt underfoot.And you will cast all their sinsinto the depths of the sea.20You will give truth to Jacob,faithful love to Abraham,as you swore to our fathersfrom days of old.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

With Micah 7:8–20 we reach the majestic closing chord of the whole book of Micah – and the last day of our fourteen-day journey. This passage is written in the form of a prophetic liturgy in which different voices take turns: first the repentant city of Zion speaks to her gloating enemy (vv. 8–10), then the prophetic promise of rebuilding rings out (vv. 11–13), followed by the shepherd's prayer and God's answer (vv. 14–17), and finally the whole community of faith bursts into an overwhelming hymn of praise for God's forgiveness (vv. 18–20).

Notice the radical change in Zion's heart (vv. 8–9). In chapters 2 and 3 the leaders of Jerusalem denied their sin and said arrogantly: "Is not the LORD in our midst? No evil will come upon us." (3:11). But here in chapter 7 we hear the voice of true repentance. Zion makes no more excuses: "The rage of the LORD I will bear, for I have sinned against him" (v. 9). And at the very moment the sinner stops defending himself and confesses his guilt before God, the Lord steps in as his Advocate. The same Lord who in Micah 1:2 stood as a witness against the peoples, and in 6:1–2 pleaded a case (riv) against his people, now in 7:9 takes up the case for his child ("until he pleads my case" – yariv rivi – יָרִיב רִיבִי) and leads her out of the dark dungeon into the light!

The book ends where it began – with Micah's own name. In verse 18 the prophet asks: "Who is a God like you?" In Hebrew this sounds like mi el kamokha – מִי אֵל כָּמוֹךָ – a direct play on the name Micah (Mikhah, short for Mikhayah: who is like the LORD?). It is also the title of this series. In chapter 1 no one was like the LORD in his holy judgement that makes the mountains melt; here in chapter 7 no one is like the LORD in his fathomless forgiveness that buries sins in the depths of the sea.

Insights from the Hebrew

  • When I have fallen, I rise (ki nafalti qamti – כִּי נָפַלְתִּי קָמְתִּי): In the Hebrew poetry these two verbs stand right next to each other with no transition: "I have fallen – I have risen!" For the child of God a fall into sin or discipline is never the end of the story, because the LORD himself is his light in the darkness.
  • Shepherd with your staff – Carmel, Bashan and Gilead (v. 14): In Micah 4:14 (English 5:1) the judge (ruler) of Israel was struck on the cheek with the staff (shevet – שֵׁבֶט), and in 6:9 the city bowed under the disciplining rod (matteh). Now the staff becomes once again the shepherd's staff of care (cf. Psalm 23:4). The word karmel (כַּרְמֶל – orchard land; our translation: "in the midst of a fruitful land", though it is also the name of Mount Carmel), together with Bashan and Gilead (the rich pasture lands east of the Jordan which Assyria had already taken in 732 BC), symbolises the full restoration of the lost inheritance.
  • The trinity of sin and fourfold forgiveness (vv. 18–19): See how thoroughly verses 18 and 19 deal with every facet of our guilt, using the same three key words that appear in Exodus 34:6–7: 1. He bears away guilt (nose' 'awon – נֹשֵׂא עָוֺן): Nasa' literally means to lift up and carry away (like the scapegoat on the great Day of Atonement, or the Lamb of God in John 1:29). It is the same verb as in verse 9: Zion says, "The rage of the LORD I will bear" – and God himself bears her guilt away. 2. He passes over rebellion ('over 'al pesha' – עֹבֵר עַל פֶּשַׁע): The deliberate rebellion (pesha') that brought on the judgement in Micah 1:5 is passed over by God in grace. 3. He treads our guilt underfoot (yikhbosh – יִכְבֹּשׁ): The verb kavash means to subdue an enemy under your feet and trample him. God treats our sins not merely as a debt on an account but as a tyrant whom he crushes under his feet! 4. Into the depths of the sea (bimtsulot yam – בִּמְצֻלוֹת יָם): Just as Pharaoh's chariots at the exodus from Egypt (cf. v. 15; Exodus 15:5) sank like a stone into the depths (metsulot) of the Red Sea, never to rise again, God drowns all our sins (chatta't) for ever in the ocean of his grace.

Core Theological Truths

  1. True repentance turns God from our Judge into our Advocate. As long as we cover and justify our sins, God stands against us; but the moment we, like Zion in verse 9, agree with God about our guilt, he himself steps in to defend our case against the accuser (cf. 1 John 1:9; 2:1).
  2. God's highest joy lies not in anger but in grace. Verse 18 gives us a glimpse into the very depths of God's heart: "He does not hold on to his anger for ever, for he delights in faithful love." He delights (chafets – חָפֵץ) in faithful love (chesed). Judgement is God's "strange work" (Isaiah 28:21), but showing grace and covenant love (chesed) is his passionate joy!
  3. God's forgiveness is both complete and irreversible. Our sins are not laid down on a shallow beach where tomorrow's low tide can wash them up again; they are cast into the depths of the sea. On the cross of Golgotha Jesus Christ took all our 'awon, pesha' and chatta't upon himself (nasa'), crushed the power of sin under his feet (kavash), and blotted out the record of our debt for ever (Colossians 2:14–15).
  4. Our certainty rests on God's unbreakable covenant oath. The last verse of the book (v. 20) anchors our future not in our own fickle faithfulness, but in the truth (emet) and faithful love (chesed) that God promised on oath to Abraham and Jacob (cf. Luke 1:72–73, where Zechariah, looking ahead to the coming of Jesus, clearly echoes this very verse).

Living It Out

At the end of our journey through Micah, chapter 7:8–20 leaves us with three life-changing truths for our everyday walk of faith.

First, verses 8 and 9 give us a powerful weapon against the accusations of the enemy and our own feelings of guilt. When you have stumbled, or are sitting in a dark season of discipline and consequences, the enemy (the "my enemy" of verses 8 and 10, the enemy of your soul) immediately whispers: Look at you! Where is the LORD your God now? You have fallen too far to get up again. Don't argue with the accuser about your own goodness. Do what Micah does: confess your guilt honestly before God, and at the same time point to your Redeemer. Say boldly: Yes, I have fallen, but by his grace I get up again! Even if I sit in the dark today, the LORD is my light, and he will plead my case.

Second, the Jewish tradition of Tashlich (based on Micah 7:18–20, in which believers throw crumbs of bread into flowing water as a symbol of their sins) invites us to stop "fishing" where God has buried our sins. As the well-known Dutch Christian speaker and writer Corrie ten Boom once said: when God casts our sins into the deepest sea, he puts up a sign on the shore that reads, "No fishing allowed!" How often do we dive back in our minds into confessed sins from our past, only to punish ourselves with them all over again? If the holy God has trampled your sins under his feet through the blood of Jesus and thrown them into the deep sea, honour him by accepting his complete acquittal.

Finally, verse 18 connects with Micah 6:8. In 6:8 the Lord asked that we love chesed (merciful, faithful love). Why? Because in 7:18 we see that God himself delights in chesed! When we see how the Lord passes over our great rebellion and casts our guilt into the sea, our own hardness melts away. Whoever has truly stood beneath the cross of this God and cried out, "Who is a God like you?", cannot help but let the guilt of their neighbour sink into the sea of forgiveness as well.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • When you stumble spiritually or go through a dark time, do you let feelings of guilt and the accuser keep you in the mud, or do you cling to the confession of faith in verse 8: "When I have fallen, I rise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD is light for me"?
  • Do you truly believe, deep down, that the Lord delights in faithful love (v. 18) – that he forgives gladly and with joy when you return to him – or do you still think of him as someone who forgives reluctantly and with a frown?
  • Is there an old, already confessed sin or failure from your past that the Lord has long since cast into the "depths of the sea" (v. 19), but that you still keep trying to dredge up in your mind?
  • Towards which person in your life is the Lord calling you today to show the same heart as verse 18: not to hold on to your anger, but to pass over an offence because God has forgiven you so much?

Prayer

Incomparable Lord, my God and my Redeemer – who is a God like you! At the end of this journey through the book of Micah I fall down at your feet in deep worship and wonder. You are the Holy One before whom the mountains melt like wax, and yet you are the Merciful One who finds deep joy in showing grace and bringing sinners home.

Lord, when I sit in the darkness of my own failings and trials, I thank you that you yourself are my Light and my Advocate. Thank you that through the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ you have trampled the power of my sin under your feet and cast all my rebellion into the depths of the sea, where it will never rise against me again.

Shepherd me today and all the days of my life with your shepherd's staff. Make my heart soft and generous in forgiving others, just as you have been towards me. Today I rest completely in your covenant faithfulness and your everlasting faithful love. To you belong all praise, honour and glory, now and for ever! Amen.
