Who Is a God Like You? · Session 7

Who Is a God Like You?

Micah 7:1–20

To get started

Who is someone you could always count on as a child or teenager – and what made them so reliable?

Where we are in the story

Chapter 7 is the last, deeply personal movement of Micah. First the prophet walks like a hungry traveller through a stripped vineyard: there is no faithful one left, and even families have turned on each other (7:1–6). Then comes the turn – “But as for me, I will watch for the LORD” – and the book ends in a liturgy of repentance, hope and praise that asks the question behind Micah's own name and this whole series: “Who is a God like you?”

A look at the Hebrew

חָסִיד

chasid · “the faithful one”

The word chasid comes straight from chesed, the faithful love the LORD asked for in 6:8. Because the people refused to love chesed, there is no chasid – no person of grace and faithfulness – left in the land (7:2).

A look at the Hebrew

צָפָה

tsafah · “watch for”

Tsafah is the word for a watchman high on the city wall, shading his eyes and peering at the horizon for the coming of the king. Together with yachal, “wait” with eager expectation, it describes active, wakeful faith, not passive resignation (7:7).

A look at the Hebrew

נֹשֵׂא עָוֺן

nose' 'awon · “bears away guilt”

Nasa' means to lift up and carry away, like the scapegoat on the Day of Atonement or the Lamb of God (John 1:29). It is the same verb as in verse 9, where Zion says she will bear the LORD's rage – and here God himself bears her guilt away (7:18).

The big idea

When every human support collapses, the one who watches for the LORD finds that he hears, pleads our case and brings us into the light – for no one is like him: he bears away guilt, delights in faithful love and casts our sins into the depths of the sea.

Discussion · 1/7

Micah looks for one cluster of grapes, one early fig, one faithful person – and finds none (7:1–4). Where do you feel the same kind of disappointment today? What is the danger in that feeling?

Micah 7:1–4

Discussion · 2/7

Verses 5–6 show the rot reaching the family itself; Jesus quotes verse 6 in Matthew 10:35–36. Why might faithfulness to Jesus sometimes bring tension even with those closest to us?

Micah 7:5–6

Discussion · 3/7

“But as for me, I will watch for the LORD; I will wait for the God of my salvation; my God will hear me.” What does it look like in practice to “watch” like a watchman on the wall in a situation that has not yet been resolved?

Micah 7:7

Discussion · 4/7

“When I have fallen, I rise; when I sit in darkness, the LORD is light for me.” Zion does not deny her sin (7:9). How does honest confession change God from the one who pleads a case against us (6:1–2) into the one who pleads our case?

Micah 7:8–10

Discussion · 5/7

The staff that struck the ruler (4:14, English 5:1) and the rod over the city (6:9) become a shepherd's staff: “Shepherd your people with your staff.” What does this say about the purpose of God's discipline?

Micah 7:14

Discussion · 6/7

Read verses 18–19 slowly. List what God does with our guilt, rebellion and sins. Which picture speaks most to you – and do you believe that he “delights in faithful love”, that he forgives gladly?

Micah 7:18–19

Discussion · 7/7

Is there a confessed sin you keep fishing up again, although God has cast it “into the depths of the sea”? And is there someone whose offence you are being called to let sink, as God has done for you?

Micah 7:19

For the week ahead

  • Write 7:18–19 on a card and read it every morning this week. When an old, confessed failure comes back to mind, read it aloud and thank God that he has cast it into the sea.
  • Think of one person you are holding something against. This week take one step towards passing over the offence – a prayer of blessing, a message, or a conversation.

Pray together

Begin with a few moments of silent confession, then read 7:18–20 aloud together as a response. Let each person thank God in one sentence for one thing they have discovered about him in Micah, and close together with the question that names the series: “Who is a God like you?”
