Who Is a God Like You? · Day 8 / 14

From Lame Sheep to an Iron Horn: God's Plan Through the Exile

Scripture Micah 4:6–13

6On that day – declares the LORD –I will gather the lameand assemble the outcast,and the one on whom I brought evil.7I will make the lame a remnant,and the one driven far away a mighty nation;and the LORD will reign over them on Mount Zionfrom now on and for ever.8And you, tower of the flock,hill of Daughter Zion,to you it will come:the former dominion will come,the kingdom of Daughter Jerusalem.9Now, why do you cry out so loudly?Is there no king in you?Has your counsellor perished,that pain has seized you like a woman giving birth?10Writhe and push, Daughter Zion,like a woman giving birth!For now you will go out of the cityand live in the open field;you will come as far as Babylon.There you will be rescued;there the LORD will redeem youfrom the hand of your enemies.11And now many nationshave gathered against you,who say: "Let her be defiled,and let our eyes gloat over Zion!"12But they do not know the plans of the LORD,and they do not understand his counsel:that he has gathered them like sheaves to the threshing floor.13Get up and thresh, Daughter Zion!For I will make your horn iron,and your hoofs I will make bronze;you will crush many peoples.And I will devote their gain to the LORD,their wealth to the Lord of all the earth.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

After painting in verses 1 to 5 the breathtaking vision of the "last days" – in which all the nations stream to Zion in peace – Micah brings us back in verses 6 to 13 to the rough road that lies between Judah's present crisis and that final glory. How does a broken, sinful people get from the ruins of Micah 3 to the paradise of Micah 4? Micah's answer is honest and encouraging at once: the road to glory runs right through the fire of refining and exile, but the Shepherd-King never lets the reins slip from his hand.

These eight verses fall into three powerful prophetic scenes: the first is introduced by "On that day" (bayyom hahu – בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא, vv. 6–8), the second by "Now" ('attah – עַתָּה, vv. 9–10) and the third by "And now" (we'attah, vv. 11–13).

Historically, Micah lives during the rise of the Assyrian empire (late eighth century BC). In his day Babylon was a city under Assyrian domination – its king Merodach-baladan did rebel against Assyria and sent envoys to Hezekiah (2 Kings 20:12) – and by no means the world power it would become more than a century later under Nebuchadnezzar. And yet, by the Spirit of God (v. 10), Micah sees more than a hundred years ahead that Jerusalem would finally be carried off into exile to Babylon (586 BC) – and that right there, in the heart of their deepest humiliation, the LORD would redeem them!

Insights from the Hebrew

  • The lame (hatstsole'ah – הַצֹּלֵעָה) made a remnant (she'erit – שְׁאֵרִית): In verses 6 and 7 the LORD promises to gather the sheep that limp or are lame (tsala' – the same verb used in Genesis 32:31, Hebrew 32:32, of Jacob limping after his wrestling with God). Normally a leader builds a mighty nation out of the strongest and bravest. God does the exact opposite: he takes the bruised, the scattered and those who have been bowed down under his discipline, and makes them his invincible remnant (she'erit).
  • Tower of the flock (migdal 'eder – מִגְדַּל עֵדֶר) and Ophel ('ofel – עֹפֶל): In verse 8 Micah addresses Jerusalem as migdal 'eder ("tower of the flock") and 'ofel – in our translation "hill", the fortified ridge just south of the temple mount where the royal palaces probably stood. A tower of the flock was a stone watchpost in the field from which the shepherd guarded his sheep against predators. Although David's earthly royal house would soon look like an abandoned watchpost, God promises that "the former dominion will come" (the messianic kingship of David's line) to Zion.
  • Birth pains instead of death throes (chuli wagochi – חוּלִי וָגֹחִי): In verses 9 and 10 we hear Jerusalem screaming in terror as the enemy surrounds her. With sharp irony Micah asks: "Is there no king in you? Has your counsellor (yo'atsekh – יוֹעֲצֵךְ) perished …?" Their earthly kings and political advisers are powerless – but their true King (v. 7) lives! That is why Micah compares their suffering to a woman in labour (kayyoledah – כַּיּוֹלֵדָה). Death pains end in a grave, but birth pains – however fierce they are – bring forth new life.
  • The plans (machshevot – מַחְשְׁבוֹת) and counsel ('etsah – עֵצָה) of the LORD (v. 12): The enemy nations crowding around Jerusalem to destroy her think they are carrying out their own military agenda. But Micah shows the hidden reality behind the scenes: "they do not know the plans of the LORD, and they do not understand his counsel." They think they are coming as reapers to cut Judah down, but God has in fact gathered them like sheaves ('amir) on his threshing floor!
  • From cowering flock to threshing ox (v. 13): Notice the change of imagery. In the last verse the defenceless Daughter Zion is commanded to get up and thresh (doshi). In the ancient world oxen threshed grain by trampling over the sheaves on the threshing floor, often pulling a threshing sledge; the horn was the picture of strength (see Deuteronomy 33:17). God makes her horn of iron and her hoofs of bronze. And the gain (betsa' – בֶּצַע) that the nations amassed by violence is not kept for self-enrichment but devoted (charam – חָרַם) "to the Lord of all the earth" (adon kol ha'arets – אֲדוֹן כָּל הָאָרֶץ).

Core Theological Truths

  1. God's kingdom is built with broken people restored by grace. The LORD does not reject his sheep because they are lame, wounded or even struck by his own righteous discipline (v. 6). In God's economy weakness is not a disqualification but the place where his power is made perfect (see 2 Corinthians 12:9).
  2. For a child of God, suffering is never a dead end but a birth canal. Judah first had to be torn away from its false securities in the city and live in the open field on the way to Babylon before it could be redeemed ("There you will be rescued" – v. 10). Sometimes God takes us out of our comfort zones into a spiritual "field" where all our human props fall away, so that we can come to know him alone as our Redeemer.
  3. God's sovereign counsel ('etsah) rules even over the wickedness of his enemies. When the powers of darkness conspire against God's people, it looks to human eyes as if evil is winning (v. 11). But according to verse 12, even the enemy unknowingly serves God's higher plan. As at the cross of Golgotha, where the enemies thought they were defeating the Son of God, the Lord carries out his greatest work of salvation precisely through the darkest hour (see Acts 4:27–28).
  4. True victory always ends in devotion to the Lord. Unlike the leaders of Micah 2 and 3, who put unjust gain (betsa') into their own pockets, the treasures of the nations in verse 13 are devoted to the Lord of all the earth. In the Hebrew text the LORD does this himself ("And I will devote their gain to the LORD"); the redeemed people share in his victory and keep nothing of it for themselves.

Living It Out

Micah 4:6–13 speaks today with special power to everyone who feels a huge gap between God's great promises for their life and their present broken reality.

Perhaps you are reading this devotional feeling, spiritually or emotionally, like the sheep in verse 6: lame, tired and pushed to the side. Perhaps you limp because of the hurt other people have caused you, or perhaps you limp because you have had to go through your own wrong choices and the Lord's loving discipline. The enemy loves to whisper to such believers that they are now only of "second-hand" use in God's kingdom. But look at the promise of verse 7: "I will make the lame a remnant, and the one driven far away a mighty nation." The Good Shepherd is not ashamed of your limping walk; he picks you up and builds his future precisely through people who know their own weakness and lean entirely on his grace.

Second, verses 9 to 12 give us new glasses through which to look at our present trials. When a crisis strikes – an illness, a financial setback, or a season in which we are forced out of the safe city of our control into the open field of uncertainty – our first reaction is often panic ("why do you cry out so loudly?"). In the storm we forget that our King and our Wonderful Counsellor (Isaiah 9:6, Hebrew 9:5) is still on his throne!

More than that: the Lord invites you today to see that your pain is not the destruction of your life but birth pains through which he is bringing forth something new, pure and eternal in your character. The circumstances that today feel as though they have conspired to crush you are already lying like sheaves on the threshing floor of God's sovereign plan. In Babylon – at the place where your own strength has completely run out – you will discover that the Lord himself is your Redeemer and your strength.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • In which area of your life do you feel "lame" or broken right now (v. 6), and how does it change your perspective to know that the Lord chooses precisely such people to reveal his power and kingship?
  • When unexpected crises strike, do you react as if you have no king and no counsellor (v. 9), or do you remind your soul that Jesus Christ reigns on the throne of your life?
  • Is there a "Babylon experience" or an "open field" in your life – a place you would never have chosen to go – where you can look back and say: "There the Lord truly set me free from my idols and fears" (v. 10)?
  • Which difficult situation or opposition in your life seems to be gaining the upper hand right now (v. 11), where you need to hold on to the truth of verse 12: that the Lord's plans and counsel are completely in control?

Prayer

Sovereign Lord, King of Zion and Shepherd of my soul, I thank you for the immense comfort of your word today. Thank you that you are not only a God for the strong and the perfect, but that you seek out the lame, the wounded and the scattered sheep and gather them in your arms.

Lord, you know the places in my life where I am limping today and where I am bowed down under my own shortcomings or hurts. By your grace, turn my weakness into a testimony of your power. Forgive me for the times when, in distress, I cry out as if I had no King and no Counsellor. Help me to remember that no enemy and no trial can move outside your plan.

Even when you lead me out of my earthly comfort zones to places where I can only trust in you, let me firmly believe that it is right there that you will meet me and redeem me. Make me strong in your strength today, and let everything I overcome and receive by your grace be devoted only to your honour – to you, the Lord of all the earth. In Jesus' name, Amen.
