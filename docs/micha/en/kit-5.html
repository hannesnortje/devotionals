Small-group kit · Session 5 / 7

The Ruler from Bethlehem

Present ▶

The big ideaOut of little Bethlehem God brings a Ruler whose origins reach back to ancient days, who stands and shepherds in the strength of the LORD and is himself our peace – and under him his people become dew and lion among the nations, while he cuts away every false support they lean on.

Timing

  • 10 min Welcome & opener
  • 10 min Read the passage
  • 10 min Background & original language
  • 40 min Discussion
  • 15 min Application & prayer

To get started

Think of a small, unremarkable place – a village, a school, a corner shop, a kitchen table – that turned out to matter a great deal in your life. What made it so important?

Read together · Micah 5:1–14

Read 4:14 (English 5:1) again as a bridge; then one reader takes 5:1–5 (English 5:2–6), a second 5:6–8 (English 5:7–9), and a third the LORD's words in 5:9–14 (English 5:10–15), pausing briefly after each “I will cut off”.

Where we are in the story

Last week ended with Jerusalem besieged and her judge struck on the cheek (4:14; English 5:1). Now God turns our eyes to a village less than ten kilometres south of Jerusalem: Bethlehem Ephrathah, where David was once called as a shepherd boy. Centuries later the scribes quote this very verse to Herod (Matthew 2:6). The chapter then shows how God's people live under this Shepherd: as dew and as a lion among the nations, and cleansed of everything they trusted instead of him. (Our verse numbers are Hebrew: in English Bibles add one throughout this chapter.)

A look at the Hebrew

  • mimei 'olam – מִימֵי עוֹלָם “from ancient days”: The word 'olam can mean “the distant past” but also “eternity”. Our translation reads “his origins are from of old, from ancient days” (5:1; English 5:2), yet the phrase can just as well mean ‘from the days of eternity’. Day 9 hears both: his roots lie in the ancient promise to David, and his origin reaches back before creation (cf. John 1:1–2).
  • wehayah zeh shalom – וְהָיָה זֶה שָׁלוֹם “and this one will be peace”: The text does not merely say that the Ruler will bring peace or make peace. Literally it says: “And this one will be peace.” (5:4; English 5:5). This One (zeh) is himself the peace – peace is not a treaty but a Person (cf. Ephesians 2:14).
  • wehikhratti – וְהִכְרַתִּי “I will cut off”: Four times in 5:9–12 (English 5:10–13) the LORD says “I will cut off” – horses and chariots, cities and fortresses, sorceries, carved images and sacred pillars – and then “I will uproot” the Asherah poles (5:13). It is the verb for cutting a covenant and for cutting down a tree: God's surgeon's knife removes every crutch that keeps his people from leaning on him.

Discussion

  1. After the humiliated judge of 4:14 comes: “But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, too small to be among the clans of Judah” (5:1; English 5:2). Why does God go back to David's humble roots instead of the palace in Jerusalem? Where in your life do you feel “too small” to matter? (Micah 5:1)
  2. “His origins are from of old, from ancient days.” What does the double meaning of 'olam (the distant past, or eternity) suggest about who this Ruler is? How does Matthew 2:1–6 help us read the verse? (Micah 5:1)
  3. God gives his people up “until the time when she who is in labour has given birth” (5:2; English 5:3). How do we live faithfully in seasons when God's promise seems a long way off? (Micah 5:2)
  4. “He will stand and shepherd in the strength of the LORD … And they will dwell secure” (5:3; English 5:4). Compare this Shepherd with the leaders of chapter 3 who devoured the flock. Are you trying to keep watch yourself through worry and control, instead of resting while he stands? (Micah 5:3)
  5. “And this one will be peace.” (5:4; English 5:5). What is your inner peace built on at the moment – on circumstances going well, or on the Person of Christ? What difference does it make that peace is a Person? (Micah 5:4)
  6. The remnant of Jacob will be “like dew from the LORD, like showers on the grass”, which waits for no human being – and also “like a lion among the animals of the forest” (5:6–7; English 5:7–8). Where do you need to be more like dew (quiet, refreshing, gracious), and where more like the lion (brave and unbending before evil)? (Micah 5:6–7)
  7. The LORD cuts off horses, fortresses, sorceries and images, “and you will no longer bow down to the work of your hands” (5:12; English 5:13). What are today's “horses and fortresses” – savings, expertise, status, control? Has God ever taken such a support away, and can you now see grace in it? (Micah 5:9–13)

For the week ahead

  • Name one “Bethlehem” in your life – a small task, a quiet faithfulness, a person others overlook – and give it your full attention this week, trusting that God works through small things.
  • Identify one “horse” or “fortress” you lean on instead of God, and each day this week bring it to him in prayer, telling him honestly what you would fear if it were gone.

Pray together

Begin by worshipping Christ, the Ruler from Bethlehem, who stands and shepherds his people. Let each person name (aloud or silently) one place where they need his peace this week, and pray for each other: “Be this one's peace.” Close by asking that the group may be like dew from the LORD where they live and work.

Notes for the leader

  • This session uses days 9 and 10. Day 9 (4:14–5:4) starts in chapter 4: reread 4:14 only as a bridge and spend most of your time in chapter 5.
  • Verse numbers: throughout chapter 5 English Bibles are one verse higher (our 5:1 = English 5:2). Point this out at the start, especially if people bring their own Bibles.
  • Matthew 2:6 quotes 5:1, and day 9 reads it of Christ, whose origin reaches into eternity. If someone asks, you can say that Jewish readers understand the verse of a coming king from David's line; acknowledge this respectfully without turning the evening into a debate.
  • “Seven shepherds and eight princes” (5:4) is a Hebrew number idiom for overflowing fullness (cf. Amos 1:3); don't try to identify fifteen people.
  • The lion that “tramples and tears” (5:7) and the vengeance on the nations (5:14) can disturb people. Day 10 reads the lion as God's truth refusing to bow to intimidation, not as a licence for aggression; vengeance belongs to the LORD.
  • The “sorceries” of 5:11 may touch someone with an occult past, and losing a “horse” (a job, income or position) can be raw. Don't tell anyone that God took something “to teach them”; let people discover grace for themselves, and offer to talk afterwards.
