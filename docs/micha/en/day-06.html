Who Is a God Like You? · Day 6 / 14

Filled with the Spirit of the LORD: True Courage and Judgement on Zion

Scripture Micah 3:8–12

8But as for me, I am filled with power,with the Spirit of the LORD,with justice and with might,to declare to Jacob his rebellionand to Israel his sin.9Hear this, please, you heads of the house of Jacoband rulers of the house of Israel,who abhor justiceand twist everything that is straight,10who build Zion with bloodand Jerusalem with injustice!11Her heads judge for a bribe,her priests teach for a price,and her prophets divine for money;yet they lean on the LORD and say:"Is not the LORD in our midst?No evil will come upon us."12Therefore, because of you,Zion will be ploughed as a field,Jerusalem will become heaps of ruins,and the mountain of the house a wooded height.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

With Micah 3:8–12 the first great movement of the book of Micah (chapters 1–3) reaches its dramatic climax. In the preceding verses we saw how the official prophets of Jerusalem were plunged into spiritual darkness and had to cover their mouths in shame because they had sold the truth for money. Over against this corrupt majority Micah now steps forward with one of the most powerful personal confessions in the whole Old Testament: "But as for me, I am filled with power, with the Spirit of the LORD, with justice and with might …" (v. 8).

Historically, Jerusalem in the late eighth century BC was a city full of impressive building projects. After the fall of the Northern Kingdom (722 BC) many refugees probably streamed into Jerusalem, and the city grew rapidly. To fund new defensive walls, water tunnels (see 2 Kings 20:20) and luxurious palaces for the nobility, the leaders laid heavy burdens on the poor rural population. When an ordinary citizen looked at Jerusalem, he saw gleaming limestone walls and a beautiful temple; but when God looked at Jerusalem, he saw a city built "with blood" and "with injustice" (v. 10).

The shock of Micah's closing words in verse 12 can hardly be overstated. For the people of Judah it was unthinkable that God would ever allow his own dwelling place – Mount Zion and Solomon's temple – to be destroyed. And yet we know from Jeremiah 26:17–19 (more than a hundred years later) that Micah's brave preaching was not in vain. When King Hezekiah heard this sharp word of judgement from the mouth of the country prophet from Moresheth, he humbled himself before the LORD, so that God averted the destruction of Jerusalem in Hezekiah's days (until the city was finally destroyed by Babylon in 586 BC).

Insights from the Hebrew

  • "But as for me …" (we'ulam anokhi – וְאוּלָם אָנֹכִי): The Hebrew construction at the start of verse 8 sets up a deliberate, sharp contrast. On one side stand the professional prophets who sell their voices; on the other stands anokhi ("I myself"). Micah does not appeal to a royal appointment, academic standing or family connections, but solely to the ruach YHWH (רוּחַ יְהוָה) – the Spirit of the LORD.
  • The threefold equipping of the Spirit (koach, mishpat, gevurah): Micah names three specific gifts with which the Spirit has filled him:
    • Koach (כֹּחַ – power): the spiritual and physical stamina to stay standing when everyone around you gives way.
    • Mishpat (מִשְׁפָּט – justice): the ability to see right and wrong through God's eyes, untainted by bribes or fear of people.
    • Gevurah (גְּבוּרָה – might): the word is related to gibbor (גִּבּוֹר), a hero or warrior. It is the courage to speak unpopular truths straight into the faces of powerful people.
  • To "twist" everything that is straight (ye'aqqeshu – יְעַקֵּשׁוּ, from 'aqash): In verse 9 Micah accuses the leaders of being those who "twist everything that is straight": what is straight (yashar) they bend crooked and warped ('aqash). Instead of the court being a straight measure of fairness, they bend the rules so that they always favour the rich.
  • False "leaning" on the LORD (yisha'enu – יִשָּׁעֵנוּ, from sha'an – שָׁעַן): In verse 11 all three pillars of Judah's society appear side by side: the heads (politics and the courts), the priests (teaching the law) and the prophets (revelation). All three groups are driven by one thing: money. And yet "they lean on the LORD". The verb sha'an means to lean your full weight on a staff or a wall. They use God's covenant promises as a religious crutch to soothe their conscience while living in open disobedience.
  • Coming full circle – from high places to wooded heights (bamot ya'ar – בָּמוֹת יָעַר): In Micah 1:5 the prophet asked: "And what are the high places (bamot) of Judah? Is it not Jerusalem?" Here at the end of chapter 3 (v. 12) he uses the word bamot again. Because Jerusalem itself has become a spiritual bamah (pagan high place), the temple mount will literally become bamot ya'ar – a wooded height, deserted hills overgrown with scrub. Lamentations 5:18 later describes exactly such a scene: foxes prowling over desolate Mount Zion.

Core Theological Truths

  1. True spiritual authority comes from the Holy Spirit, not from human approval. Micah had no earthly power structure behind him. But because he was filled with the Spirit of the LORD, his words brought a king to repentance and have lived on in Scripture for centuries, while his popular opponents have vanished into oblivion.
  2. When money becomes the driving force of service and worship, a community's soul rots. Verse 11 shows how every noble calling – to judge rightly, to teach God's word and to proclaim his will – is profaned as soon as the question is no longer what is right before God, but what it pays me.
  3. Religious symbols can never protect us from God's judgement if our hearts are far from him. Judah's leaders thought the mere presence of the temple in Jerusalem made them untouchable ("Is not the LORD in our midst?"). But God cares more about an obedient heart than about holy buildings (see Jeremiah 7:4; Matthew 3:9).
  4. God's sharpest word of judgement is always a gracious call to repentance. Why does the LORD announce that Zion will be ploughed as a field? Not because he delights in destruction, but to break open the hard ground of human pride so that there is room for true repentance – exactly the way King Hezekiah responded to this very message.

Living It Out

At the end of these first three chapters of Micah we face a choice between two ways of life: the way of verse 8 (Micah's Spirit-filled integrity) and the way of verse 11 (the leaders' calculating hypocrisy).

Every one of us faces moments today when it is easier, safer and more profitable to swim with the current. At work, in business or in our social circles we are often expected to bend a little of what is straight ('aqash) – to tell a seemingly harmless lie, to keep quiet when someone is treated unfairly, or to leave our Christian principles at the door so that we can get ahead. In our own strength we are all prone to the fear of people. But the same Spirit who filled Micah with power (koach), justice (mishpat) and might (gevurah) lives today in every believer in Christ (see 2 Timothy 1:7: "For God did not give us a spirit of cowardice, but of power and of love and of self-control").

Second, verse 11 warns us against the danger of cheap grace and mere formal religion. It is possible to sit in church every Sunday, quote Christian slogans (the Lord is with us, after all!) and "lean" on outward religious habits, while never asking in our business practices, our finances and our relationships what really pleases the Lord. God does not ask us for a shiny outward façade built at the expense of others; he asks for a life in which our Sunday confession and our Monday integrity are one and the same thing.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • In which specific situation in your life today do you need the Holy Spirit's threefold equipping of power, justice and might (v. 8) to stand up for the truth or to make a difficult but just decision?
  • What are you "building" your career, your finances or your family life with at the moment (v. 10)? Are you building with integrity and a spirit of service, or sometimes at the cost of other people's rest and wellbeing?
  • Examine your heart in the light of verse 11: do you ever use God's grace and his promises of protection as an excuse to carry on with a habit or attitude you know he disapproves of?
  • When King Hezekiah heard Micah 3:12, he humbled himself and changed his ways. What one concrete change is the Lord calling you to make as a result of our journey through Micah 1 to 3?

Prayer

Almighty Father and Lord of hosts, at the end of these deep and searching chapters I bow before you in humility. I confess that so often, out of fear of people or love of my own comfort, I keep silent when I should speak, or give in when I should stand firm.

Fill me again today with your Holy Spirit – give me your power to persevere, your clear insight to tell right from wrong, and your courage to live for your truth with gentleness but without compromise.

Lord, keep me from the self-deception of mere formal religion. Never let me lean on my own religious habits while my heart is far from you. Through your word, plough up today any hard, proud or selfish ground in my heart, so that my life will not be a heap of ruins of self-will but a living temple where your Spirit truly reigns. In the name of Jesus Christ, our true King and Redeemer, Amen.
