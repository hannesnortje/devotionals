Small-group kit · Session 3 / 3

Woe to the City of Bloodshed

Present ▶

The big ideaGod sees through the charm of violence, lies and seduction and brings it to an end: Nineveh finds no comforter and no refuge, its allies fly away like locusts – but the LORD remains good, the refuge that never falls, and the Good Shepherd who never slumbers.

Timing

  • 10 min Welcome & opener
  • 10 min Read the passage
  • 10 min Background & original language
  • 40 min Discussion
  • 15 min Application & prayer

To get started

Think of a product, a place or a promise that looked wonderful in the advert but turned out very different in real life. What gave it away?

Read together · Nahum 3:1–19

Use three readers: the indictment of 3:1–7, the comparison with No-Amon and the fig trees in 3:8–13, and the final taunt and lament in 3:14–19 – read the last question slowly and leave a moment of silence.

Where we are in the story

Chapter 2 showed how Nineveh would fall; chapter 3 shows why. It opens with the cry “Woe!” – both an indictment and a funeral lament – and exposes a city built on blood, deceit and occult seduction (3:1–7). Then Nahum points to No-Amon (Thebes in Egypt), which Assyria itself conquered in 663 BC: if that city with water all around her fell, so will Nineveh (3:8–13). The book ends with mocking calls to strengthen the walls, the image of locusts that fly away, and a lament over the king of Assyria – closing, like Jonah, with a question (3:14–19).

A look at the Hebrew

  • 'ir damim – עִיר דָּמִים “city of bloodshed”: Literally “city of bloods” – plural, bloody deeds. Verse 1 sums up Nineveh in four charges: “Woe to the city of bloodshed! All of it is lies, full of plunder; the prey never departs.” Its splendour had been bought with human blood, its treaties were deceit (kachash), and like a predator it was never satisfied.
  • menachamim – מְנַחֲמִים “comforters”: “Where shall I seek comforters for you?” (3:7). The word comes from the same root as the prophet's name, Nahum (Nachum), “comfort”. Nahum brings rich comfort to oppressed Judah, but for cruel, unrepentant Nineveh no comforter can be found.
  • ma'oz – מָעוֹז “refuge”: “You too will seek a refuge from the enemy.” (3:11). It is the same word as in 1:7: “The LORD is good, a refuge in the day of distress”. Nineveh looks for a refuge and finds none; the LORD alone is the refuge that does not fall.

Discussion

  1. Read the four charges of verse 1. Which of them – violence, lies, plunder, never being satisfied – do you see most clearly in the systems of our world today? Where are we ourselves tempted to bend the truth or get ahead at others' expense? (Nahum 3:1)
  2. Nineveh is pictured as a prostitute, “lovely and charming, mistress of sorceries”, “who sells nations through her harlotries”. Day 5 says sin never knocks at the door in its true form. Which temptations look “lovely and charming” to you, and where do they lead in the end? (Nahum 3:4)
  3. “Who will grieve for her?” Nineveh had used everyone and had no one to comfort her when she fell. How do you treat people when you are in a position of strength? Who could you comfort this week, as the Holy Spirit, the true Comforter, comforts you? (Nahum 3:7)
  4. “Are you better than No-Amon”? Nineveh's own history proved that rivers and armies do not make a city untouchable. What “past examples” do we ignore because we think it could never happen to us? (Nahum 3:8–11)
  5. “Go into the clay and tread the mortar! Take hold of the brick mould!” Nineveh feverishly tried to repair its walls in its own strength instead of humbling itself before God. Where are you “baking bricks” to control a situation you really need to surrender to the Lord? (Nahum 3:14)
  6. Nineveh's merchants, guards and officials were like locusts – many in good times, gone “when the sun rises”. Have you experienced people or supports disappearing in a crisis? How does that help you rest in the Good Shepherd, who does not slumber or fly away (Psalm 121:4; John 10:11–12)? (Nahum 3:16–18)
  7. Jonah ends with God's question of grace over a repentant Nineveh (Jonah 4:11); Nahum ends with “for over whom has your evil not passed continually?” What do the two endings together teach us about God? And looking back over the whole series: which truth about God has comforted you most? (Nahum 3:19)

For the week ahead

  • Ask the Holy Spirit each day this week to show you one place where you keep up a mask – of success, of having it all together, of religion – and bring it to God in honesty.
  • Choose one person who has been hurt by the harshness of the world and be a voice of God's comfort to them this week: a visit, a meal, a message or a prayer together.

Pray together

Begin with a few moments of silent confession, asking God to cleanse anything built on dishonesty or on wronging others. Then thank him together for the Good Shepherd, who took our “fracture” on himself so that through his wounds we are healed (Isaiah 53:5). Close by reading 1:7 aloud together as the summary of the whole series.

Notes for the leader

  • This is the last session. Leave 10–15 minutes at the end to look back over all three chapters: what has the group learned about who God is – patient yet just (chapter 1), mighty over every human power (chapter 2), the refuge that never falls (chapter 3)?
  • Chapter 3 contains hard images: corpses in the streets (3:3), the public exposure of the “prostitute” (3:5), little children dashed to pieces (3:10), and “your people are women” (3:13). Read them, but don't linger on the details. Day 5 explains 3:5 against the background of ancient Near Eastern punishment for adultery and betrayal (Jeremiah 13:22, 26; Ezekiel 16:37) – the image strips away Nineveh's false glamour. Be aware that someone in the group may carry experiences of violence or abuse; never make light of these verses, and offer to talk afterwards.
  • Nineveh is the one called a prostitute here – the image is about a seductive, deceitful empire, not about women. If the conversation drifts, gently bring it back to the question of seduction by sin and worldly power.
  • Groups may ask whether it is right to “clap our hands” over someone's fall (3:19). Follow day 6: the world claps in relief because oppression has ended and God's justice has prevailed. Don't let it turn into wishing revenge on people; leave judgement with God (Romans 12:19), as in session 1.
  • The link to Jonah is the heart of the close: the same God who had pity on a repentant Nineveh (Jonah 4:11) judges an unrepentant Nineveh a century later. Both books end with a question. Let the group sit with that before moving to prayer.
  • Day 6 points from Nineveh's incurable wound to the cross (Jeremiah 30:12, 17; Isaiah 53:5) and from the clapping of hands to the end of history (Revelation 18–19). If time is short, use this as the final word before the prayer.
