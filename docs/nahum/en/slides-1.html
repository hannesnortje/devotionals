A Refuge in the Day of Distress

Nahum 1:1–14

To get started

Think of a time you were caught out by a sudden storm, a power cut or a breakdown on the road. Where did you go to find shelter – and what made it feel safe?

Where we are in the story

Nahum prophesied in the seventh century BC (roughly between 663 and 612 BC), when Assyria was the undisputed world power and Nineveh its seemingly invincible capital. About a century earlier Jonah had preached there, the city repented, and God spared it; but Nineveh slid back into cruelty, idolatry and bloodshed, carried northern Israel into exile in 722 BC and kept squeezing little Judah. Nahum's name means “comfort”: tonight we hear the LORD's majesty in a poem (1:2–8) and then his words turning in turn to Nineveh and to Judah (1:9–14).

A look at the Hebrew

אֵל קַנּוֹא

El qanno' · “A jealous God”

The book opens with “A jealous God, one who takes vengeance, is the LORD” (1:2). This is not petty human envy but the fiery, protective love of the covenant God who will not stand by while his people are destroyed. Three times the verse says the LORD “takes vengeance” (noqem): not a loss of control, but the Judge of all the earth putting things right.

A look at the Hebrew

מָעוֹז

ma'oz · “refuge”

The anchor verse of the book, which gives this series its title: “The LORD is good, a refuge in the day of distress; he knows those who shelter in him.” (1:7). A ma'oz is a mountain stronghold, an impregnable fortress, and “knows” speaks of intimate, personal care. The same word “distress” (tsarah) returns in 1:9: the distress in which God is a refuge is the distress he brings to an end.

A look at the Hebrew

קַלּוֹתָ

qallota · “you weigh nothing”

God's sentence on the Assyrian king ends: “I will make your grave, for you weigh nothing.” (1:14). Qalal means to be light, weightless, of no account – much like Belshazzar in Daniel 5:27, weighed on the scales and found too light. The world thought Assyria a heavyweight; on God's scales its pride weighs nothing.

The big idea

The LORD who is slow to anger is also the Judge who will not let evil go on for ever – and that same God is good, a refuge for everyone who shelters in him and the One who breaks the yoke of the oppressed.

Discussion · 1/7

Nahum begins not with Nineveh but with a portrait of God: jealous, taking vengeance, “slow to anger and great in power”. Which of these words do you find hardest, and which do you find most comforting? Why?

Nahum 1:2–3

Discussion · 2/7

“The LORD is slow to anger” – the very words Jonah used of God (Jonah 4:2) when Nineveh was spared. God had given the city about a century. How do we tell God's patience apart from his absence when the wicked seem to prosper?

Nahum 1:3

Discussion · 3/7

Assyria boasted of horses, chariots and walls, but for the LORD “clouds are the dust of his feet”, and Bashan, Carmel and Lebanon wither at his word. How does this picture of God's power change the size of the problems that loom largest for you?

Nahum 1:3–6

Discussion · 4/7

Verse 7 does not promise that there will be no “day of distress” – it assumes such days will come. Where do you usually run first when the storm breaks: control, distraction, other people, or the Lord? What would sheltering in him look like this week?

Nahum 1:7–8

Discussion · 5/7

“What are you devising against the LORD?” The Assyrians thought they were simply fighting a small state called Judah. What does it mean that to touch God's people is to plot against God himself?

Nahum 1:9–11

Discussion · 6/7

To Judah God says: “I have afflicted you; I will afflict you no more.” Assyria had been the rod of his discipline (Isaiah 10:5), but the season was over. What “yoke” or “bonds” do you long for God to break – and how does it help to know that every hard season has an expiry date?

Nahum 1:12–13

Discussion · 7/7

The problems or people that intimidate us can look like “tangled thorns”, yet on God's scales they weigh nothing. What in your life are you giving more weight than God's promises? Share only what you are comfortable sharing.

Nahum 1:10–14

For the week ahead

  • Write out 1:7 and put it where you will see it each day. When a moment of distress comes this week, read it aloud and name the specific thing you are bringing to your refuge.
  • Is there someone who has wronged you and whom you would like to pay back? Following Romans 12:19, give the case to God in prayer each day this week and ask him to free you from bitterness.

Pray together

Begin by thanking God together for who he is in 1:2–7: holy, patient, mighty and good. Then pray in pairs or threes for the “days of distress” each person is facing, and for the yokes people long to see broken, asking the LORD to be their refuge. Close by praying for those in the world today who live under oppression.
