A Refuge in the Day of Distress · Day 1 / 6

The Majesty of God: Slow to Anger, a Refuge in Distress

Scripture Nahum 1:1–8

1An oracle concerning Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite.

2A jealous God, one who takes vengeance, is the LORD;the LORD takes vengeance and is full of wrath.The LORD takes vengeance on his adversariesand holds it against his enemies.3The LORD is slow to anger and great in power,and the LORD will surely not acquit the guilty.In whirlwind and in storm is his way,and clouds are the dust of his feet.4He rebukes the sea and makes it dry,and all the rivers he dries up.Bashan and Carmel wither,and the blossom of Lebanon withers.5The mountains shake because of him,and the hills melt away;the earth rises up before his face,the world and all who live in it.6Before his fury who can stand?Who can rise up against his burning anger?His wrath pours out like fire,and the rocks are torn down by him.7The LORD is good,a refuge in the day of distress;he knows those who shelter in him.8But with a flood that passes overhe will make a full end of her place,and his enemies he will pursue into darkness.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Welcome to this in-depth journey through the book of Nahum. Nahum is one of the shorter prophetic books of the Old Testament (only three chapters), yet it is a powerful, poetic masterpiece focused on God's sovereignty, his just judgement on evil, and his unshakeable faithfulness as a refuge for his children. Over six days we will work through the text verse by verse.

(A note on verse numbers: we follow the Hebrew text. Chapter 1 is numbered the same in English Bibles, but our 2:1 is English 1:15, and from 2:2 onward English Bibles are one verse lower.)

To understand the book of Nahum, we have to put ourselves in the ancient Near East of the seventh century BC (roughly between 663 and 612 BC). In this period the Assyrian Empire was the undisputed world power, and Nineveh was its dazzling, seemingly invincible capital.

About a century before Nahum, the prophet Jonah had gone to Nineveh and preached repentance. The city repented, and God spared it. But within a few generations Nineveh slid back into an even worse state of extreme cruelty, idolatry and bloodshed. The Assyrians were notorious for their psychological warfare and their barbaric torture of conquered peoples. They had already carried the northern kingdom of Israel into exile in 722 BC, and they constantly threatened and extorted Judah. To the small, defenceless nation of Judah it looked as if evil sat on the throne and God was silent.

It is in this dark hour that God calls Nahum. The whole book is a prophetic message that God is going to judge the cruel oppressor (Nineveh) – which brings deep comfort and relief to God's oppressed people.

Nahum opens his book (verses 2–8) with a majestic theophany (a visible appearing of God's glory in nature), which in the original Hebrew takes the form of a partial acrostic poem (an alphabetic psalm).

Insights from the Hebrew

  • Nahum (Nachum – נַחוּם): The prophet's name means "comfort" or "comforter". The name is already the message: God's judgement on the oppressor is comfort for the oppressed.
  • A jealous God (El qanno' – אֵל קַנּוֹא): "Jealous" here has nothing to do with petty human envy. It speaks of the fiery, protective love of a covenant God who refuses to stand by while his bride (his people) is destroyed or his honour is trampled by idols and tyrants.
  • One who takes vengeance (noqem – נֹקֵם): Three times in a single verse the text says that the LORD "takes vengeance" (the participle noqem, from naqam).
  • Slow to anger (erekh appayim – אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם): Literally "long of nostrils" – a picture of someone who breathes deeply and does not quickly become angry. These are exactly the words Jonah uses of God in Jonah 4:2: "a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in faithful love".
  • Refuge (ma'oz – מָעוֹז): The word for "refuge" means a strong place, a fortress (from the root 'azaz, to be strong) – and early on it was linked with taking refuge.
  • Knows (yodea' – יֹדֵעַ, from yada'): The word for "knows" means far more than intellectual knowledge; it speaks of intimate, personal care and a covenant relationship.
  • The acrostic pattern: In verses 2–8 the lines begin, more or less, with the successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet – from aleph (El, v. 2) to roughly the middle of the alphabet, with a few gaps. The form itself says: God's majesty is ordered and complete.

Core Theological Truths

  1. God's holy jealousy and vengeance (v. 2): When the text says three times in one verse that the LORD takes vengeance, it does not mean that God loses control in a fit of rage. It means that God is the supreme Judge of the universe, who will restore justice and righteousness. If God did not punish evil, he could not be perfectly good and just.
  2. The balance between patience and justice (v. 3): In verse 3 Nahum draws on God's self-revelation to Moses in Exodus 34:6–7: "The LORD is slow to anger". Since Jonah's day God had given Nineveh more than a hundred years to turn from its cruelty! Yet his patience is no sign of weakness or indifference. He is "great in power, and the LORD will surely not acquit the guilty." God's patience has a purpose (to give opportunity for repentance), but it also has a limit.
  3. The Creator's sovereignty over nature and nations (vv. 3b–6): Assyria boasted of its military might, its horses, its chariots and its massive city walls. Nahum shows how small human power is next to the Creator. The fearsome storms are merely the vehicle he rides on, and the thick thunderclouds are nothing but "the dust of his feet". The most fertile regions of the world of that time – Bashan (known for its pastures), Carmel (known for vineyards and orchards) and Lebanon (known for its mighty cedar forests) – wither at his single word. If the mountains shake and the rocks are torn down before his holiness, how can an earthly kingdom imagine it will stand against him?
  4. The anchor verse: the double character of God's judgement (vv. 7–8): Verse 7 is the beating heart of the whole book of Nahum: "The LORD is good, a refuge in the day of distress; he knows those who shelter in him." Notice the contrast between verse 7 and verse 8. For those who flee to God in humility, he is an unshakeable fortress (ma'oz) in the middle of the storm. But for those who rebel against him in pride and oppress others (Nineveh), he is "a flood that passes over", sweeping away their false security. The same God who is a consuming fire to evil is a safe refuge for his children.

Living It Out

In our modern lives we often find ourselves in a position much like that of the believers in Judah. We look at a world full of injustice, corruption, violence and abuse of power – or we face personal "Assyrians": overwhelming crises, unfair treatment, or spiritual attacks that seem too great to overcome.

Nahum 1:1–8 gives us three indispensable anchors for our daily walk:

  • Let go of the burden of your own revenge and bitterness: When we are treated unjustly, our natural reaction is to hit back or to nurse bitterness in our hearts. Nahum reminds us that the judgement seat belongs to God. Because he promises to defend the innocent and to leave no injustice unpunished, we can leave our case in his hands (Romans 12:19). We do not have to be eaten up by resentment, for the Judge of all the earth sees everything.
  • Don't confuse God's patience with his absence: Sometimes we wonder, "Lord, why don't you step in right now?" Verse 3 teaches us that God is "slow to anger". His timing is driven by wisdom and grace, not by haste. When the wicked seem to prosper, remember that the clouds are only the dust of God's feet – he is still on his way and fully in control.
  • Choose your fortress deliberately in the day of distress: Verse 7 does not promise that believers will never go through a "day of distress". On the contrary, it assumes that such days will come. The question is where you run when the storm breaks. Do we run to human solutions, to fear, to control or to distraction? Or do we flee to the ma'oz – the fortress of God's presence – knowing that he knows us by name and cares for us intimately?

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Is there a situation of injustice or hardship in your life where it feels right now as if the "Ninevehs" of this world have the upper hand? How does the picture of God's power in verses 3–6 change your view of that problem?
  • What is your first reaction when a "day of distress" (a crisis, anxiety or disappointment) comes your way? What would it mean for you in practice today to shelter in the Lord instead of trying to carry the load alone?
  • How does the truth that "he knows those who shelter in him" (v. 7) comfort you today in your particular circumstances?

Prayer

Heavenly Father, Ruler of the universe and Judge of all nations, I bow today in reverence before your majesty. When I look at the storms of this life and the injustice in the world around me, my heart often grows small and anxious. Thank you that your Word in Nahum lifts my eyes again to see your greatness. Before you the mountains shake, and the greatest powers of this world are like worthless dust at your feet.

Lord, I confess that I sometimes mistake your patience for slowness, and that I try to fight my own battles in my own strength. Today I lay every scrap of bitterness, fear and uncertainty at your feet. I declare again from Nahum 1:7: You are good! You are my only true refuge and mountain stronghold in the day of distress. Thank you that you do not merely see me from a distance, but know me intimately, understand my heart and hold me safely in your hands. Teach me to walk in your rest today, however hard the storm winds blow outside. In the mighty name of Jesus Christ, my Rock and Redeemer. Amen.
