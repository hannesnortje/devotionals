A Refuge in the Day of Distress · Day 6 / 6

The Final Fall of Oppression and Our Eternal Hope

Scripture Nahum 3:8–19

8Are you better than No-Amon,who sat by the channels of the Nile,with water all around her,whose rampart was the sea,her wall from the sea?9Cush was her strength, and Egypt,and there was no end;Put and the Libyanswere among your helpers.10Yet she too went into exile,she went into captivity;her little children too were dashed to piecesat the head of every street;for her honoured men they cast lots,and all her great men were bound in chains.11You too will be drunk,you will be hidden away;you too will seek a refuge from the enemy.12All your fortresses are fig treeswith first-ripe figs:if they are shaken,they fall into the mouth of the eater.13Look, your people are women in your midst;to your enemies the gates of your landare thrown wide open;fire has devoured your bars.14Draw water for the siege!Strengthen your fortresses!Go into the clayand tread the mortar!Take hold of the brick mould!15There the fire will devour you,the sword will cut you off;it will devour you like the hopper.Make yourself many like the hopper,make yourself many like the locust!16You have made your merchants more numerousthan the stars of the sky;the hopper sheds its skin and flies away.17Your guards are like the locust,your officials like a swarm of locuststhat settle in the wallson a cold day;when the sun rises, it flees,and its place is not known – where are they?18Your shepherds slumber, king of Assyria;your nobles lie down to rest;your people are scattered on the mountains,and there is no one to assemble them.19There is no easing for your fracture;your wound is grievous.All who hear the news of youclap their hands over you;for over whom has your evil not passedcontinually?

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

Today we reach the dramatic and moving close of the book of Nahum – and of our journey together. In these last twelve verses the prophet tears down the last illusion of Assyria's invincibility. To convince Nineveh that her fall is not merely possible but inevitable, Nahum uses a brilliant historical argument, followed by a series of vivid images from nature (ripe figs and locust swarms) and a final funeral lament over the king of Assyria.

This passage also gives us the most precise clue for dating the book. Because Nahum refers to the fall of the Egyptian city of No-Amon (Thebes) as something that has already happened, we know that he delivered his prophecy after 663 BC, when Assyria stood at the very peak of its world power.

The historical mirror of No-Amon / Thebes (vv. 8–10): Nahum asks a cutting rhetorical question: "Are you better than No-Amon"?

  • No-Amon (in Hebrew literally "the city of the god Amun", ancient Thebes in Upper Egypt, today's Luxor) was for centuries the glittering religious and political capital of Egypt. It was surrounded by the broad Nile (poetically called "the sea" here) and a network of canals that served as natural moats – "with water all around her, whose rampart was the sea". On top of that it had powerful African allies: Cush (Nubia, in today's Sudan), Egypt, Put (probably in Libya; some think of Punt on the Red Sea) and the Libyans.
  • Yet in 663 BC the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal achieved the unthinkable: he conquered No-Amon, plundered her temples and carried her nobles away in chains.

The king of Assyria (vv. 18–19): The book ends with a sombre epitaph addressed to the king of Assyria. When Nineveh fell in 612 BC, King Sin-shar-ishkun was reigning there; he probably perished with his city. (The story that the last king burned himself in the flames of his own palace comes from later Greek writers. A last Assyrian king, Ashur-uballit II, held out at Harran until about 609 BC.)

Insights from the Hebrew

  • "A refuge" (ma'oz – מָעוֹז, v. 11): Nineveh "will seek a refuge from the enemy" – the same word used in Nahum 1:7 to show that God alone is the true ma'oz for his children!
  • "First-ripe figs" (bikkurim – בִּכּוּרִים, v. 12): The very first, early summer figs that ripen in June. These early figs were a prized delicacy and hung so loosely on the branches that the slightest shake of the tree dropped them straight into the mouth of the hungry picker.
  • Three words for locusts (vv. 15–17): yeleq (יֶלֶק, "hopper" – the young, wingless stage that crawls and strips everything in its path), arbeh (אַרְבֶּה, "locust" – the adult, winged swarm that darkens the sun) and gov (גוֹב, v. 17, "swarm of locusts").
  • Merchants, guards and officials (vv. 16–17): Nahum compares Nineveh's own merchants (rokhlayikh – רֹכְלַיִךְ), guards (minnezarayikh – מִנְּזָרַיִךְ) and officials (tafsarayikh – טַפְסְרַיִךְ – an Assyrian loanword for high-ranking bureaucrats or scribes) to locusts.
  • "Your shepherds slumber" (namu ro'ekha – נָמוּ רֹעֶיךָ, v. 18): The "shepherds" are the generals, advisers and rulers. That they "slumber" and the nobles "lie down to rest" is a poetic euphemism for the sleep of death.
  • "Your fracture … your wound" (shivrekha – שִׁבְרֶךָ; nachlah makkatekha – נַחְלָה מַכָּתֶךָ, v. 19): Nachlah means "grievous, sick" – a wound that will not heal. Jeremiah 30:12 uses exactly the same expression for Judah's wound of sin.

Core Theological Truths

  1. No city is untouchable (vv. 8–11). Look at the prophet's masterly logic: Nineveh considered herself safe behind the waters of the Tigris and her military might. But Nahum says: your own history proves that no city with rivers and armies is untouchable! If invincible Thebes fell, Nineveh too will drink the cup of God's wrath and stagger drunk (v. 11), and look in vain for a refuge. The LORD alone is the refuge that does not fall.
  2. Ripe figs and paralysed defenders (vv. 12–14). Against the massive border forts and mountain strongholds meant to guard Assyria's frontiers, God sets a simple farming image: "All your fortresses are fig trees with first-ripe figs: if they are shaken, they fall into the mouth of the eater." Just as easily would Nineveh's outer fortresses fall before the Babylonian and Median armies. - In verse 13 the mighty Assyrian soldiers are described as men who have completely lost their fighting spirit and courage, while the strategic mountain passes and river gates of the land are "thrown wide open" and fire devours the wooden bars of the city gates. - Verse 14 is full of prophetic irony: "Draw water for the siege! Strengthen your fortresses! Go into the clay and tread the mortar! Take hold of the brick mould!" Nahum mockingly urges the inhabitants to make mud bricks at top speed to repair the breaches in their walls. But the water Nineveh trusted could not save her: in 2:7 (English 2:6) it is precisely "the gates of the rivers" that are opened, and according to later Greek writers a flood brought down part of the walls. Feverish human effort at the last minute cannot hold back what God has decided to tear down.
  3. The paradox of the locust swarm (vv. 15–17). On the one hand, the enemy will devour Nineveh like a plague of locusts (v. 15a). On the other hand, Nineveh's own merchants, guards and officials are themselves like locusts. Why? When things were going well for Nineveh, her opportunistic merchants and mercenaries swarmed like locusts – "more numerous than the stars of the sky" – to profit from the city's plundered wealth (v. 16). But just as the hopper sheds its last skin, gets wings and flies away as soon as the land has been stripped bare, and just as locusts sit numb with cold on the stone walls on a chilly morning but vanish as soon as the sun rises (v. 17), so all Nineveh's allies, businessmen and officials will vanish without a trace as soon as the heat of war rises!
  4. Sleeping shepherds and the world's applause of relief (vv. 18–19). Without leaders, the Assyrian people are scattered over the mountains like sheep without a shepherd: "and there is no one to assemble them." The final diagnosis in verse 19 is irrevocable: "There is no easing for your fracture; your wound is grievous." - The whole book ends with one of the few rhetorical questions with which a book of the Bible closes (along with Jonah 4:11!): "All who hear the news of you clap their hands over you; for over whom has your evil not passed continually?" - How remarkable this contrast between Jonah and Nahum is! The book of Jonah ends with God's question of grace over a repentant Nineveh: "And should I not have pity on Nineveh, the great city"? The book of Nahum ends about a century later with a question of righteous judgement over an unrepentant Nineveh. When the tyrant finally falls, no one weeps; the whole ancient world claps its hands in relief because God's justice has prevailed!

Living It Out

As we close the book of Nahum, these last verses leave us with deep lessons for our faith, our character and our eternal hope:

Don't lean on "fair-weather locusts". Nineveh thought she was strong because she was surrounded by thousands of merchants, officials and allies (vv. 16–17). But when the sun of trial beat down, they all spread their wings and flew away. Worldly friendships and systems built on self-interest, money, status or mere advantage vanish as soon as you land in a crisis. How different the Lord is! In Nahum 1:7 we read – the words that give this series its title: "The LORD is good, a refuge in the day of distress". When the heat of life rises, God does not fly away; he draws all the closer to those who shelter in him.

Sleeping shepherds and the Good Shepherd. Verse 18 shows the tragedy of earthly leaders ("shepherds") who fail in the hour of need, slumber and leave their people scattered. It lifts our eyes to the true Shepherd of Israel. Psalm 121:4 assures us: "Look, he does not slumber and does not sleep, the keeper of Israel." And in John 10:11–12 Jesus says that the hired hand runs away when he sees the wolf coming (just like Nineveh's officials), but that he is the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep and will never leave them scattered or alone.

The healing of our incurable wound, and our eternal hope. For proud, unrepentant Nineveh the final word was: "your wound is grievous" – a wound without healing (v. 19). In ourselves, our spiritual wound of sin was incurable too (Jeremiah 30:12, 17). But on the cross of Golgotha Jesus Christ took the judgement and the "fracture" upon himself, so that through his wounds complete healing could come to us (Isaiah 53:5)! What is more, the clapping of hands in verse 19 reminds us of the end of history in Revelation 18 and 19: a day is coming when all evil, all pain, all injustice and every form of darkness will be finally destroyed by Christ – and all heaven and earth will burst out in jubilant praise and relief, because God reigns for ever!

Questions for Personal Reflection

  1. In verse 14 Nineveh feverishly tried in her own strength to tread the mortar and take hold of the brick mould to save her crumbling walls, instead of humbling herself before God. Where in your life are you trying right now to "bake bricks" in your own strength to control a situation you really need to surrender to the Lord?
  2. Have you ever experienced earthly supports or people (like the locusts in v. 17) disappearing when the sun of trial rises? How does that experience help you rest more deeply in the faithfulness of the Good Shepherd, who never slumbers or flies away?
  3. As you look back over our whole six-day journey through the book of Nahum (chapters 1 to 3), which one truth about who God is has touched your heart most deeply or brought you the most comfort (nachum)?

Prayer

Sovereign Lord, King of the ages and Judge of all the earth, at the end of this journey through the book of Nahum I bow before you in deep worship. Over these six days your word has shown me how mighty, how holy and how unshakeably just you are. Empires come and go, proud fortresses fall like ripe figs, and worldly allies fly away like locusts in the sun – but your throne stands firm for all eternity!

Father, thank you that you are not a shepherd who slumbers or sleeps like the leaders of this world. Thank you, Lord Jesus, that you are my Good Shepherd, who never leaves me when crisis strikes. When my own spiritual wound of sin was, humanly speaking, incurable, you let yourself be broken on the cross so that I could be made whole, forgiven and saved.

Help me to keep the lessons of Nahum deep in my heart. Never let me put my trust in human "mud bricks", earthly wealth or passing power. Today I choose afresh to anchor my life in the truth of Nahum 1:7: you are good, you are my only true refuge in the day of distress, and you know me by name. Give me the courage and faith to look ahead with hope, in the sure knowledge that all evil and hardship have an expiry date and that your justice and peace will win the final victory. To you belong all praise, honour and glory, now and for ever. Amen.
