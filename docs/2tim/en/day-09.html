Alone in the Dungeon, but the Lord Stands by Me

Scripture 2 Timothy 4:9–22

9Make every effort to come to me soon. 10For Demas has abandoned me, because he loved this present age, and has gone to Thessalonica; Crescens has gone to Galatia, Titus to Dalmatia. 11Only Luke is with me. Pick up Mark and bring him along, for he is useful to me for service. 12I have sent Tychicus to Ephesus. 13When you come, bring the cloak that I left with Carpus in Troas, and the books, especially the parchments. 14Alexander the metalworker did me much harm – the Lord will repay him according to his works. 15You too must be on your guard against him, for he strongly opposed our words.

16At my first defence no one stood by me, but all abandoned me – may it not be counted against them! 17But the Lord stood by me and gave me strength, so that through me the proclamation might be fully carried out and all the nations might hear it; and I was rescued from the mouth of the lion. 18The Lord will rescue me from every evil deed and will save me for his heavenly kingdom. To him be the glory for ever and ever. Amen.

19Greet Prisca and Aquila and the household of Onesiphorus. 20Erastus stayed in Corinth, and I left Trophimus sick in Miletus. 21Make every effort to come before winter. Eubulus greets you, as do Pudens and Linus and Claudia and all the brothers and sisters.

22The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you all.

Digging Deeper: Context and Meaning

Historical, Cultural and Literary Background

In these moving closing verses we hear the very last recorded words from the pen of the apostle Paul before his earthly life was ended – according to tradition, by the sword of Rome. After the majestic victory song of verses 6 to 8, where he spoke of his finished race and the crown of righteousness waiting for him, the tone now drops to the raw, human reality of his imprisonment in Rome.

It was probably around AD 66 or 67, and winter was approaching (v. 21). Once the autumn storms began to blow across the Mediterranean, most shipping stopped until spring; if Timothy could not leave Ephesus before winter, he would probably never see Paul alive again. Paul sits in chains in prison – according to tradition in a cold, damp stone dungeon. He is physically chilled and asks for his heavy cloak; he is intellectually and spiritually hungry for his books and parchments; and he feels the deep pain of human abandonment. While some co-workers (such as Crescens, Titus and Tychicus) have been sent out on legitimate missions and only Luke, the faithful physician, has stayed with him, his heart is bruised by betrayal and loneliness. Yet nowhere does the glory of Christ shine more brightly than in this very abandonment.

Insights from the Greek

The original Greek text paints an unforgettable portrait of human frailty and divine faithfulness:

  • "For Demas has abandoned me, because he loved this present age" (Dēmas gar me egkatelipen agapēsas ton nyn aiōna – Δημᾶς γάρ με ἐγκατέλιπεν ἀγαπήσας τὸν νῦν αἰῶνα, v. 10): In Paul's earlier letters (Philemon 24; Colossians 4:14) Demas is mentioned with honour as a valued co-worker at Paul's side. But the Greek verb egkataleipō means to leave someone in the lurch in a crisis or on the battlefield and run away. Why? Notice the sharp contrast with verse 8: while faithful believers have loved Christ's future appearing (ēgapēkosi), Demas loved this present age (agapēsas). When the price of discipleship became too high, he chose earthly comfort and safety over the cross.
  • "Pick up Mark … for he is useful to me" (Markon analabōn age … euchrēstos – Μᾶρκον ἀναλαβὼν ἄγε … εὔχρηστος, v. 11): Right after the heartache over Demas comes one of the most beautiful moments of grace in the New Testament: "Pick up Mark and bring him along, for he is useful to me for service." Years earlier, on the first missionary journey, a young and frightened John Mark had deserted Paul and Barnabas in Pamphylia (Acts 13:13), which later led to a sharp split between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39). But under the patient mentoring of Barnabas and Peter, Mark grew. Now Paul calls him euchrēstos – exactly the word he used in 2 Timothy 2:21 for a purified "vessel meant for honour", "useful to the master"! Demas started strong and finished badly; Mark stumbled, but by grace finished strong.
  • The cloak, the books and the parchments (ton phailonēn … ta biblia, malista tas membranas – τὸν φαιλόνην … τὰ βιβλία, μάλιστα τὰς μεμβράνας, v. 13): The phailonēs ("cloak") was a thick, circular woollen cape with an opening for the head, which protected travellers against icy rain and winter cold. Along with it the old apostle asks for his biblia (scrolls, probably of papyrus) and especially the membranas – costly, durable sheets of parchment (prepared animal skin). What exactly was written on them we do not know – perhaps notebooks, perhaps parts of the Old Testament. Even at the end of his life, as a celebrated apostle about to enter heaven, Paul very probably never stops studying the Scriptures.
  • The first defence and "the Lord stood by me" (En tē prōtē mou apologia – Ἐν τῇ πρώτῃ μου ἀπολογίᾳ / ho de kyrios moi parestē – ὁ δὲ κύριός μοι παρέστη, vv. 16–17): In the Roman legal system the prōtē apologia ("first defence") was probably the preliminary hearing (prima actio) before the court to establish the charges. Perhaps Alexander the metalworker acted as a witness or accuser against Paul (v. 14) – and Paul does not take revenge himself but leaves the matter with God: "the Lord will repay him according to his works". The atmosphere in Rome was so dangerous that no one had the courage to stand beside Paul in the courtroom as a character witness. All had abandoned him. But like Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34) and Stephen under the stones (Acts 7:60), Paul prays without any bitterness: "may it not be counted against them!" And then comes the glorious turn: "But the Lord stood by me!" The verb paristēmi means to come and stand right beside someone – in a courtroom, like an advocate and defender beside the accused. Christ strengthened Paul inwardly (enedynamōsen), so that before the highest court in the world he could proclaim the gospel to the Roman authorities, and he was for the time being "rescued from the mouth of the lion" – an Old Testament image from Psalm 22:21 (Hebrew 22:22) and Daniel 6 for mortal danger, or for Nero's immediate death sentence.
  • "The Lord will rescue me … and will save me for his heavenly kingdom" (rhysetai me … kai sōsei eis tēn basileian autou tēn epouranion – ῥύσεταί με … καὶ σώσει εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν αὐτοῦ τὴν ἐπουράνιον, v. 18): Paul looks with perfect peace towards the final trial and the sword that awaits him. Even if Rome cuts off his head, he declares that the Lord will rescue him "from every evil deed" (keep him from falling into sin or denying his faith under pressure) and bring him safely through and save him (sōzō) right into his heavenly kingdom!

Core Theological Truths

  1. Where our love lies determines how we finish. A heart that secretly stays in love with "this present age" (like Demas) will in the end drop out when the way of the cross gets hard; only love for Christ and his kingdom keeps us standing to the end.
  2. Past failure is never final with God. The story of Mark proves that God can fully restore broken and fallen servants who repent and make them euchrēstos (highly useful) for his kingdom.
  3. Spiritual maturity is marked by a forgiving spirit and a hunger for the Word. Even in his darkest hour Paul does not nurse grudges against friends who deserted him, and his soul keeps thirsting for the parchments of God's Word.
  4. Christ's presence is enough when every human support falls away. People may leave you, and even believing friends may sometimes, like Trophimus, be left behind sick (v. 20) – but the living Lord personally stands beside his child in the fiery furnace and carries him safely across the threshold of death into eternal glory.

Living It Out

At the end of this unforgettable letter we stand with Paul in the half-light of his Roman cell, and we find that his last words speak deeply to our own hearts.

First, look at the two names Demas and Mark (vv. 10–11). Each of our lives is moving in one of these two directions. It does not matter how well you began your spiritual race or how many years you have been in church: if you allow love for this present age – its status, its money, its pleasure and its human approval – to conquer your heart, you are in danger of becoming a Demas who drops out when sacrifice is required. But perhaps you read these words today and feel like Mark: you look back on a time in your life when you let the Lord or other people down, when you ran away out of fear, and you think you have been thrown on the spiritual scrap heap for good. Hear the gospel today: God is the God of second chances! If you return to him, his grace makes you euchrēstos again – useful and precious in his service.

Second, look at Paul's request for his cloak and the parchments (v. 13), and his remark about Trophimus, whom he left sick in Miletus (v. 20). This reminds us of the honest realism of biblical faith. Even the greatest apostle, through whom God had once done miracles, needed a warm coat in his cold prison and could not heal every sick co-worker on demand. True faith is not a denial of physical cold, pain or illness; it is a deep resting in God's sovereign will in the midst of our earthly frailty – while we keep our hearts warm at the fire of Scripture ("the parchments").

Finally, take verses 16 to 18 with you into every lonely night that may still await you on life's road. There are times in a person's life – in a hospital room, in a divorce court, at an open grave, or when you have to stand up for the truth alone – when even those closest to you cannot or will not stand by you. Do not let human disappointment poison your heart with bitterness; pray with Paul: "may it not be counted against them!" And then look up and see who is standing right beside you: The Lord stands by you! And even when the last earthly storm breaks, he will save you – not necessarily from physical death, but through death into his safe, heavenly kingdom.

Questions for Personal Reflection

  • Is there any secret "Demas love" in my heart for the things of this present age that is slowly cooling my devotion to Christ – or am I letting God restore me, like Mark, to usefulness after my failures?
  • Am I holding on to any grudge or unforgiveness towards people or fellow believers who let me down and left me alone in a time of need?
  • Have I known the deep, personal experience of the Lord Jesus himself coming to stand beside me and filling me with his strength when every human support gives way?

Prayer

Lord Jesus Christ, my faithful Friend, my Advocate and my eternal King:

with a full and grateful heart I bow before you today at the end of this precious letter. Thank you for the life and the last testimony of your servant Paul. When I look at that cold dungeon in Rome, I see that your grace is enough for the darkest night and that your light can never be put out by human evil.

Lord, guard my heart from the tragedy of Demas. Do not let the glittering temptations of this present age ever steal my love for you. And where in the past, like Mark, I have stumbled, been afraid or failed, I thank you that you do not throw me away, but cleanse me through your blood and make me useful again for your kingdom. Give me, right into old age, an unquenchable hunger for the parchments of your Word.

Today I choose to release and forgive everyone who has hurt me or left me in the lurch in my hour of need; do not count it against them, Lord. For I know that I am never truly alone: you stand by me! You give me strength when my own legs give way beneath me. I trust you today with my life and with my dying, in the firm certainty that you will rescue me from every evil deed and one day bring me safely into your heavenly kingdom.

To you be all praise, honour and glory, from now on and for ever and ever! May your grace be with my spirit every day. Amen.
